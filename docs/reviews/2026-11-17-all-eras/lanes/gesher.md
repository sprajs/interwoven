## Evidence and decision

- Alperson-Afil (2008), pp. 1735–1737, Table 1, sections 3–4 and Fig. 2, reports burnt-flint clusters across the eight horizons. The result varies by level. L2 separates burnt from unburnt concentrations, whereas the two are almost coincident in L6. The analysis favors recurrent anthropogenic burning based on clustering and local fire ecology. The conclusion leaves acquisition versus ignition unresolved. The publisher preview and a transcription of the published author copy were inspected. [Published article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.quascirev.2008.06.009) · [Author copy](https://www.academia.edu/12695463/Alperson_Afil_2008_QuaternaryScienceReview)
- Alperson-Afil, Richter and Goren-Inbar (2017), Layer II-6 Level 2, Results, Table 3 and Fig. 4, add a separate measure of heating on a small transect in the same L2 flint assemblage. The flint tends to be more strongly heated toward the concentration. This informs localization of heating. Two authors also worked on the 2022 fish study. [PLOS article](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0188091)
- Zohar et al. (2022), Figs. 4–5, Discussion, Supplementary Texts C–E and Supplementary Figs. 1–2, argue for cooking on the combined evidence of tooth and bone representation, experimental heating comparisons and spatial overlap. They use Area A and Lake Kinneret comparisons for natural accumulation, consider post-burial alteration using fossil and isotopic comparisons, and argue against direct disposal into a hot fire. An earth oven is a discussion hypothesis. Many coordinates in the maps are assigned within recorded excavation spits. The Getis-Ord results quantify clustering within the fish finds. Cross-assemblage association is based on visualized overlap. Some experimental values overlap across heat groups. Table 4 has no L4b or L5 specimens with those horizon labels. The article version transcribed on ResearchGate has the original author-contributions paragraph. The 2023 correction updates that paragraph. [Publisher record](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-022-01910-z) · [Article transcription](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/365369121_Evidence_for_the_cooking_of_fish_780000_years_ago_at_Gesher_Benot_Ya%27aqov_Israel) · [Publisher supplement](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art:10.1038%2Fs41559-022-01910-z/MediaObjects/41559_2022_1910_MOESM1_ESM.pdf) · [Author correction](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-023-02270-y)
- Independent context includes Disspain et al. (2016), sections 2, 4 and 4.3, who experimentally separate several whole-fish treatments including roasting, burning and untreated controls. Their measured structures are mulloway otoliths, vertebrae and scales, so their results cannot calibrate Gesher cyprinid pharyngeal tooth enameloid. Davis et al. (2025), introduction and references, place Gesher among early fire associations and distinguish fire use from archaeological evidence of fire-making. Their new measurements concern Barnham. Neither team tests the Gesher fish-to-flint association. [Disspain et al., accepted manuscript](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/45683/11/JCU_45683_Disspain%20et%20al_2016.pdf) · [Davis et al., version of record](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09855-6)

An independent test would compare the spatial distributions of teeth above and below the proposed heating threshold with burnt versus unburnt flint within each horizon at recorded grid resolution. It would account for excavated volume and general discard patterns. Separate cyprinid experiments could compare intentional cooking, incidental heating near a fire and exposure of refuse during a later burning episode.
