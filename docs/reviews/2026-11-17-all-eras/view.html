Roman volcanic aggregate and the Pantheon

Lightweight volcanic stone in Rome’s concrete vaults
process used by
→
Pantheon graded concrete dome
The Pantheon builders incorporated coarse lightweight volcanic aggregate into the upper dome, laying tuff and scoria in alternating courses.
Scope: Use of Rome’s lightweight coarse-aggregate construction process in the upper dome of the Pantheon.
Uncertainty: The route by which the builders acquired the technique remains unknown. A modern finite-element model predicted lower peak hoop tension with graded densities across the whole structure. The effect of the crown scoria alone remains unquantified by that comparison.
Source passages
  • Pantheon · p. 213, paragraph beginning “La cupola, impostata sull’ultimo cornicione interno”
    From the 1929–1934 restoration, Terenzio directly describes the horizontal courses of the Pantheon vault and their composition, including alternating tuff and lightweight volcanic scoria in its upper zone.
  • Provenancing of Lightweight Volcanic Stones Used in Ancient Roman Concrete Vaulting: Evidence from Rome · pp. 707–709 and 711–713, figs. 2 and 3b
    Rome’s practice of using lightweight caementa is documented in sampled vaults. The authors identify yellow tuff and scoria in the Basilica Ulpia fragment and compare them with the same materials in the Pantheon crown.
  • Archaeological, structural, and compositional observations of the concrete architecture of the Basilica Ulpia and Trajan’s Forum · pp. 79–82, §§3–4, figs. 1, 3 and 5, fragment No. 10
    The field study details the alternating coarse aggregate courses in the Basilica Ulpia fragment.
  • On the Structure of the Roman Pantheon · pp. 26–29, models 5 and 6 and maximum-tension table
    The graded-density version produced lower peak hoop tension in the authors’ uncracked finite-element comparison. Their input on material grading came from De Fine Licht’s survey.

Jacquard weaving and Babbage’s engine design

Jacquard punched-card pattern weaving
adapted by
→
Babbage’s Analytical Engine punched-card design
Babbage adapted the Jacquard loom’s method of using successive perforated cards to select mechanical actions. In his Analytical Engine design, separate card strings specified the arithmetic operations and which columns would supply or receive numbers.
Scope: Transfer of a control method from working looms into an engineering design for calculation.
Source passages
  • Report of the Committee Appointed to Consider the Advisability and to Estimate the Expense of Constructing Mr. Babbage’s Analytical Machine · Footnote 3, December 1839 letter to Arago
    In the committee’s published excerpt, Babbage credits the Jacquard system he used as the basis for communicating calculation instructions to his engine.
  • Sketch of the Analytical Engine invented by Charles Babbage, Esq., with Notes by the Translator · Menabrea, pp. 677–678, and Lovelace, Note E, p. 720
    Menabrea credits Jacquard’s apparatus and explains the two card functions. Lovelace describes the Engine’s prism presenting cards to lever bundles and turning backward to reuse a sequence.
  • The Little Engines that Could've: The Calculating Machines of Charles Babbage · Chapter 3, chapter PDF pp. 5 and 18 (whole PDF pp. 27 and 40), especially nn. 31, 41–42
    Collier’s examination of Babbage’s working notebooks and drawings traces the shift from studded drum control to the Jacquard card method, citing the earlier 1836 notes.
  • Charles Babbage’s Analytical Engine, 1838 · pp. 197 and 214 (§12); p. 217 (§14 and acknowledgments)
    Bromley identifies borrowing from the Jacquard loom and reconstructs the use of ribbon-linked card strings and the Engine’s distinct operation and variable card functions from the surviving designs.

Babbage’s Analytical Engine punched-card design · 1843

Ada Lovelace published her English translation of Luigi Federico Menabrea’s description of the Analytical Engine with her own notes. She explained how reversing the card feed would let the design reuse a sequence of cards.
Publication year of Scientific Memoirs, volume III.
Source passages
  • Sketch of the Analytical Engine invented by Charles Babbage, Esq., with Notes by the Translator · Article XXIX, pp. 666–731, especially pp. 677–678 and Lovelace’s Note E, p. 720 (PDF p. 55)
    Menabrea describes operation and variable cards. Lovelace’s Note E explains how the Engine’s prism would turn backward to move a group of cards back to its former position for another pass.
Unresolved questions
  • Original Babbage manuscript for 1836 conception and manuscript draft of the 1839 letter were not inspected.
  • The archive-dated drawing includes alterations made after the March 1837 bevel-wheel introduction; component-specific dates need further investigation.
  • First encounter with loom controls remains unknown.

Fire and fish cooking at Gesher Benot Ya’aqov

No new established relationship admitted.

Gesher Benot Ya‘aqov inferred fish cooking · About 780,000 years ago

In eight Area B occupation horizons in Layer II-6 (levels 1–7, including 4b), fish remains consist predominantly of pharyngeal teeth and overlap spatially with burnt flint concentrations. X-ray diffraction on selected teeth yielded patterns consistent with moderate heating in some specimens. Zohar and colleagues inferred that hominins cooked fish at the lakeside site.
Intentional cooking is inferred from the pattern of remains and experimental comparisons. The measurements cannot identify a cooking apparatus or technique.
Source passages
  • Evidence for the cooking of fish 780,000 years ago at Gesher Benot Ya’aqov, Israel · Abstract, Results and Fig. 1
    The eight Area B occupation horizons at the site dated to about 0.78 million years ago preserve abundant fish teeth and few fish bones, unlike the natural-death comparison in Area A.
  • Evidence for the cooking of fish 780,000 years ago at Gesher Benot Ya’aqov, Israel · Figs. 4–5 and Supplementary Texts D–E
    Tooth mineral comparisons support moderate heating for some sampled teeth. The density maps compare clusters of fish remains and burnt flint in the same occupation horizons.
Unresolved questions
  • Fire acquisition and ignition, mechanism and timing of fish heating, cooking apparatus and technique remain unresolved.
  • No independent direct corroboration of the causal fire-to-fish relation was established.
  • The independent contextual studies cited in the lane note were not read here and are not needed to accept the candidate question.
  • Did hominins use the recurring fires identified in Area B to cook fish?

Maize preparation in Maya and Nahua communities

No new established relationship admitted.

Alkaline cooking of maize at San Bartolo · Most likely c. 770–895 cal CE

Starch spherulites in sealed refuse deposits associated with Structures 192 and 195 B/C match residues produced by experimental lime treatment of maize. The excavators interpret the residues as likely discarded cooking liquid.
The dating is contextual and the charcoal ranges include intervals outside the authors’ preferred estimate. The deposits establish where the residues ended up. The precise cooking location, recipe and date of invention remain unresolved.
Source passages
  • First archaeological identification of nixtamalized maize, from two pit latrines at the ancient Maya site of San Bartolo, Guatemala · §3, Table 1 and Figs. 3–6
    The sealed household refuse contexts and the authors’ most likely interval of pit use, c. 770–895 cal CE.
  • First archaeological identification of nixtamalized maize, from two pit latrines at the ancient Maya site of San Bartolo, Guatemala · §§4.1, 5 and 6.1–6.2, Figs. 7–11
    Microscopic structure and a calcium-rich elemental signature support the identification. The authors infer that alkaline cooking liquid may have been discarded into the pits.
  • The experimental identification of nixtamalized maize through starch spherulites · §3 and §§7–8, including control trials
    Lime-treated maize yielded distinctive spherulites under these experimental conditions. Non-alkaline controls did not. The archaeological study shares an author with the experiment.
Unresolved questions

  • La Corona sample chronology and controls were not accessible in full.
  • Cultivation onset and later crop and technique transmission to Nahua.
  • San Bartolo contextual dating has tails beyond preferred interval.
  • Can a documented crop lineage or route link the early Xihuatoxtla maize use to the maize processed by the Nahua?

The Antikythera calendar dial

No new established relationship admitted.

Toothed gearing in the Hellenistic Greek world · c. 225 BCE to before the wreck (probably 60 ± 10 BCE)

Freeth and colleagues estimate that the Greek geared device was made around the end of the second century BC. The date of construction remains uncertain. Its eclipse dial represented a 223-lunar-month cycle and marked possible solar or lunar eclipses. About one third of that scale survives. The upper back dial displayed a 19-year calendar of 235 lunar months in a five-turn spiral, consistent with the count in the back-cover inscription. Fragment B preserves part of the scale and part of the pointer, including the guiding pin that followed the gap between turns. Anastasiou and colleagues reconstruct the damaged mounting as a pointer that slid through its hub as the shaft turned. The final gear that drove the pointer is missing.
Jones finds the letter forms compatible with manufacture from about 225 BCE up to the shipwreck. The latest year represents the later end of his probable wreck date, based on ceramics and coins. Both endpoints are approximate.
Source passages
  • Decoding the ancient Greek astronomical calculator known as the Antikythera Mechanism · Abstract
    constructed around the end of the second century bc
  • The Back Plate Inscription and eclipse scheme of the Antikythera Mechanism revisited · Section 1, The Mechanism’s eclipse function
    The spiral scale represents 223 lunar months, includes eclipse-possibility glyphs, and is about one third extant.
  • The Epoch Dates of the Antikythera Mechanism (With an Appendix on its Authenticity) · §1, pp. 1–2, construction chronology; §2, pp. 3–4, Fig. 1 and Back Cover Inscription II.3
    The back-cover inscription gives 235 divisions. The surviving divisions accord with a five-turn spiral for the lunar months of the 19-year calendar. Jones finds the letter forms compatible with manufacture from about 225 BCE to before the shipwreck, which he estimates at 60 ± 10 BCE.
  • The Antikythera Mechanism: The Construction of the Metonic Pointer and the Back Plate Spirals · §1, PDF p. 1 and Fig. 1; §2.1, PDF pp. 3–5 and Figs. 3–5; §2.2, PDF pp. 5–7 and Fig. 6
    CT images show part of the Metonic spiral, pointer and damaged shaft mounting in Fragment B. The authors reconstruct a pin guided by the spiral slot and a pointer sliding through its rotating hub, using the inscription as a check. The last drive gear is missing.
Unresolved questions
  • The specific drive train and final gear remain missing.
  • No established route from Babylonian astronomy to this particular instrument was demonstrated.
  • Full Freeth article and full Iversen/Jones paper were not independently retrieved in this review; saved cited excerpts were available.
  • The 50 BCE end is the later end of the probable wreck range, not an absolute latest possible wreck or construction date.
