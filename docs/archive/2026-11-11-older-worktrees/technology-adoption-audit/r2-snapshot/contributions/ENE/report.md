# Manufacturing an adoptable system: energy, chips and batteries

This contribution investigates five bounded cases: crystalline-silicon photovoltaics, contrasting nuclear construction programmes, planar semiconductors and merchant memory, lithium-ion cells, and lead-acid electric two-wheelers in China. They were selected to discriminate between explanations, not to represent the frequency of technological success. The evidence concerns historical episodes ending principally by 2015, with a separately identified nuclear cancellation in 2017. It contains no forecast or claim about present market leadership.

An invention changes the available technical possibilities. Reproducible production requires another accomplishment: obtaining consistent useful output from materials, equipment and work. Deployment additionally requires purchasers, finance, permission and complementary systems. Diffusion and scaling may then change the technology itself. These distinctions matter because the sources measure different things: a patent, an order, annual shipments, modeled manufacturing costs, completed power stations or partial pollution inventories. None measures welfare automatically.

Fourteen sources were inspected, including original scholarship, a patent, a firsthand inventor account and institutional history. Nine were acquired locally in full; five were inspected through available web-parsed sections after direct downloads failed or returned empty content. Their access and specific inspection boundaries are recorded in sources.json, with acquired originals, download results and inspected web extracts preserved under cache/. A shared publication or underlying dataset is not counted as independent corroboration. The strongest conclusions are bounded contrasts; the causal attribution of finance, skills, policy and learning often remains underdetermined.

## ENE-C01 — Photovoltaics: different buyers, different production capabilities

The early PV problem was not simply how to make grid electricity cheaper. Spacecraft and remote terrestrial installations valued electrical supply under constraints that differed from those of a connected household. Nemet’s reconstruction distinguishes the 1970s shift from space procurement toward terrestrial customers from later crystalline-silicon manufacturing improvement. Customer requirements, customization, competition and possible markups changed together. His engineering model leaves a much larger unexplained residual in the early period than later. A falling transaction price therefore cannot be assigned entirely to practice on the factory floor. This is evidence against a learning-only account, not a measurement of each alternative explanation’s contribution. **ENE-K01.** [Nemet, §§3.1–3.2, pp. 3224–3225](https://wikis.mit.edu/confluence/download/attachments/21139745/Nemet_BeyondtheLearningCurve_EP_06.pdf?api=v2).

Later cost reductions had identifiable physical channels: more useful electrical output per module area, less silicon and other material per unit of output, reduced input prices, larger production facilities and fewer rejected products. Yet decomposition is not uniquely determined. For 1980–2001, Nemet assigns 43% of the decline to plant size; Kavlak and colleagues assign 8%, after changing the cost equation and including additional material effects. Their estimate rises to 36% for 2001–2012. The disagreement is analytically useful: even where the downward cost trend is secure, attributing it to factory scale depends on configuration, model and interval. **ENE-K02.** [Nemet, Table 3](https://wikis.mit.edu/confluence/download/attachments/21139745/Nemet_BeyondtheLearningCurve_EP_06.pdf?api=v2), [Kavlak et al., §4.1 and Table 3, pp. 705–706](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/56afb881e321406be6160228/t/5bff10822b6a2884d7267f17/1543442566890/Evaluating%2Bthe%2Bcauses%2Bof%2Bcost%2Breduction%2Bin%2Bphotovoltaic%2Bmodules.pdf).

This distinction changes the proposed mechanism. Repeated production can reveal defects, but larger plants can also follow expected demand and access to investment capital. Laboratory and pilot-line work can improve efficiency before substantial cumulative output exists. The reconstructions identify silicon supply and process knowledge inherited from semiconductors. That graph link represents a historical contribution, not a claim that more PV installations necessarily create the required knowledge. The 2000s silicon supply transition also prevents treating an early spillover as a permanent industrial relationship. [Kavlak et al., §4.2, p. 707](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/56afb881e321406be6160228/t/5bff10822b6a2884d7267f17/1543442566890/Evaluating%2Bthe%2Bcauses%2Bof%2Bcost%2Breduction%2Bin%2Bphotovoltaic%2Bmodules.pdf).

China supplies a particularly useful separation of production from local adoption. The inspected 2010 working paper reports over one-third of world PV production in 2008 and exports exceeding 95%, while its separate estimate puts Chinese demand at about 2.2% of the world total in 2009. These different years and denominators must not be merged. Interviews support equipment purchases, vendor training and internationally experienced managers as important entry routes. Downstream turnkey lines were more accessible than upstream processes protected by tacit knowledge and secrecy. Early success was consequently uneven within the same national economy. **ENE-K03–K04.** [De la Tour et al., abstract, §§1–2, working-paper pp. 1–11](https://minesparis-psl.hal.science/hal-00498578v1/document).

A cheap-labour-only account predicts less differentiation between stages unless additional differences are supplied. The observed equipment and skill barriers improve that explanation, but do not isolate it: capital intensity, energy cost, concentration and export incentives also differ. Likewise, foreign demand support and Chinese producer initiative are interacting mechanisms. Calling the outcome either government-created or purely market-created erases the relevant transactions. The evidence does not quantify how taxpayers, customers, owners or factory workers divided the gains.

## ENE-C02 — Nuclear construction: capacity growth is not a cost curve

Nuclear reactors offer a countercheck on automatic learning. Lovering and colleagues compile overnight construction costs for 349 reactors across seven countries. Their within-country trajectories include declining Korean construction cohorts and escalating later US and French cohorts. Korea entered after earlier countries had already developed commercial designs; the comparison consequently mixes institutional differences with timing, imported knowledge and different configurations. The authors’ experience curves are descriptive, not identified effects of national organization. Their Korean record and table counts also contain an unresolved discrepancy, so this contribution does not reproduce a precise Korean subgroup denominator. **ENE-K05.** [Lovering et al., §§3–4 and Table 1, pp. 373–379](https://core.ac.uk/download/pdf/82188133.pdf).

France demonstrates why successful scaling cannot be defined as continuously declining construction cost. Grubler’s historical reconstruction associates rapid PWR deployment with bulk orders, licensed designs, a concentrated utility and equipment sector, regulatory continuity and EDF’s engineering resources. It also finds rising real construction costs, particularly across later design series. The same institutional account describes weak public influence over decisions. Coordination enabled a programme without establishing either low costs at every margin or freely expressed citizen preference. **ENE-K06.** [Grubler, §§2–4 and Table 1, pp. 2–8](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/id/eprint/11077/1/The%20French%20Pressurised%20Water%20Reactor%20Programme.pdf).

The discriminating observation is expansion despite escalation. It contradicts the proposition that falling construction costs are necessary for substantial deployment. It does not show which component of coordination caused how much expansion. Fuel-security objectives, finance, grid demand and political authority accompanied it. Nor should Grubler’s broader claim of inevitable negative learning be inherited: the later multinational compilation challenges that generalization, while using different French records and cohort definitions. Disagreement about magnitudes should remain visible rather than being averaged into an apparently precise universal rate.

US component accounts reveal another level of mechanism. Eash-Gates and colleagues reconstruct 61 cost accounts for a representative Westinghouse four-loop plant during 1976–1987. Indirect accounts contribute 72% of the modeled increase. Engineering, planning, supervision and construction services are therefore central to the accounting explanation. That does not mean poor management independently caused 72%: regulation, redesign and schedule change can themselves generate indirect work. It does undermine an explanation confined to the price of additional reactor hardware. **ENE-K07.** [Eash-Gates et al., §2.2, manuscript pp. 3–5](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/133049/Jacopo%27s%20and%20Jessika%27s%20paper%20on%20nuclear%20cost%20Sep%202020.pdf).

Financing creates a second boundary. Overnight cost removes interest incurred before operation. Completed-project histories also omit the full experience of proposed and cancelled investments; the US account separately records the abandonment of the VC Summer expansion in 2017. A purchaser must confront capital commitment, time, uncertainty and the possibility of receiving no operating asset. For electricity users, operating performance, fuel, waste, accidents and displaced generation also matter. These records therefore cannot rank complete social outcomes. **ENE-K08.** [Lovering et al., §3.2 and §5.2](https://core.ac.uk/download/pdf/82188133.pdf), [Eash-Gates et al., introduction](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/133049/Jacopo%27s%20and%20Jessika%27s%20paper%20on%20nuclear%20cost%20Sep%202020.pdf).

## ENE-C03 — Semiconductors: a process, a purchaser and a changing learning problem

Hoerni’s patent illustrates the difference between an electrical idea and a manufacturable configuration. It specifies leaving a nonconducting coating permanently over the surface ends of semiconductor junctions. The museum history separately records a working planar transistor in March 1959 and commercial introduction in April 1960. The coating addressed a concrete reliability problem while permitting later integrated fabrication; continuing bonding and interconnect failures show that one successful process did not finish qualification. The patent is primary evidence of the disclosed method, not a yield series or proof of diffusion. **ENE-K09.** [Hoerni, US3025589A, specification and claim 1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US3025589A/en), [Computer History Museum, planar-process history](https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/invention-of-the-planar-manufacturing-process/).

Apollo provided an early purchasing environment where compactness, electrical demand, timing and reliability justified initially expensive components. NASA’s history describes MIT’s choice of one NOR-gate type despite the availability of more varied circuitry that could reduce component count. Standardization made system design and qualification more manageable. The history estimates that Apollo prototypes consumed a large share of US microcircuits in summer 1963, but the number belongs to that narrow market and moment. It is not NASA’s share of all semiconductor finance or later cost reduction. **ENE-K10.** [Tomayko, printed pp. 33–34](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/computers/Ch2-4.html).

This is a task-specific niche mechanism: a funded programme could purchase capabilities before they were attractive at ordinary prices. It does not imply that any expensive product needs military procurement, or that procurement alone generates commercially transferable reliability. Supplier process work and purchaser qualification had to meet. A useful missing counterfactual is what other military, industrial and consumer buyers would have ordered in Apollo’s absence; the inspected history cannot supply it.

The later DRAM episode tests learning rather than simply invoking it. Irwin and Klenow analyze quarterly merchant-firm shipments and industry prices over 1974–1992. Their structural estimates give own-firm cumulative output a stronger contribution than external output and find no systematically steeper Japanese learning rate. Estimated international spillovers challenge a strictly national learning story; stronger within-Japan spillovers remain less clearly resolved. In adjacent-generation tests, only two of seven transitions show economically important transfer. Prior production therefore cannot be assumed to confer permanent advantage across every redesign. **ENE-K11–K12.** [Irwin and Klenow, §IV and Tables 2–7, pp. 1210–1224](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/experience-curve/1994-irwin.pdf).

These results are conditional on a merchant market, a competition model and inferred costs. The authors do not observe plant-level yields or each firm’s marginal production cost, and exclude internal-only producers. Neither weak cross-generation transfer nor observed new entry means fabrication was easy to enter. Equipment, finance, clean processing, design expertise and customer relationships remain distinct potential barriers. The graph accordingly keeps early planar/Apollo history and later DRAM learning separate: chronology does not establish a causal path from one purchaser to every later semiconductor improvement.

## ENE-C04 — Lithium-ion: useful electrochemistry before saleable products

Yoshino’s development account describes a sequence of rejected and recombined materials. Polyacetylene could participate in rechargeable operation but presented heat and volume limitations. A suitable carbon material became available from another division of the company; pairing it with a lithium-bearing oxide cathode produced the described configuration. Comparative abuse tests then supported continued development before commercialization in Japan in 1991. The account is firsthand but retrospective and selective. It supports separation of laboratory operation, material suitability and qualification; a successful impact test does not establish that every cell, operating condition or later product is safe. **ENE-K13.** [Yoshino, §§1.1–1.5, pp. 207–217](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2019/10/yoshino-lecture.pdf).

The informative rival is not that physics was irrelevant. It is that a rechargeable electrochemical demonstration was sufficient for sale. The rejected configurations and intervening tests contradict that proposition. The episode also shows a concrete knowledge connection across corporate activities rather than an abstract arrow labelled science-to-market. However, the available account does not reconstruct pilot yields, financial decisions, warranties or the complete intervening commercial qualification programme. Those remain gaps in explaining scale.

Ziegler and colleagues address a different episode: representative high-energy-density cylindrical 18650 cells in 1995–2000 versus 2010–2015. Their model allocates 38% of cost decline to charge-density improvements, alongside material-price and manufacturing changes. Increasing stored energy in a fixed package spreads some costs over more useful capacity. Their baseline allocates about 54% to broadly defined R&D, 30% to scale and 2% to routine learning. R&D includes pilot lines and nonroutine process development, and alternative assignments test but cannot eliminate classification uncertainty. These are not shares of public spending effectiveness or workers’ contribution. **ENE-K14–K15.** [Ziegler et al., pp. 6076–6078 and 6085–6091, Figures 5–9](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlepdf/2021/ee/d1ee01313k).

This deliberately narrow service definition is valuable. A cell optimized for energy per volume differs from one optimized for high power, inexpensive materials, long cycling or abuse tolerance. Changes in dollars per watt-hour cannot silently become changes in pack price, lifetime service or every purchaser’s preferred chemistry. The next case provides a contemporary check on that substitution.

## ENE-C05 — Chinese electric two-wheelers: continued lead-acid use

Mid-2000s Chinese electric two-wheelers mostly used valve-regulated lead-acid batteries even though lithium-ion and nickel-metal-hydride products were available. A contemporary comparison estimated roughly fourfold upfront cost for a power-equivalent lithium-ion battery, with two to three times the assumed life. That makes persistence intelligible without treating newer chemistry as unavailable. It does not establish an individual buyer’s reason: liquidity, confidence in longevity, repair access and weight preferences would require direct investigation. Advanced-battery production figures also mix exports with domestic use. **ENE-K16.** [Weinert et al., §2.1.1](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0d05f8v9/qt0d05f8v9.pdf), [Cherry et al., conclusion, p. 289](https://itspubs.ucdavis.edu/download_pdf.php?id=1289).

The assembled vehicle mattered as much as battery chemistry. Field visits described prefabricated motors, controllers, batteries and chargers connected through relatively simple interfaces. Six visited assembly plants relied on manual work with pneumatic tools and little machining. Local rules altered the choice set: motorcycle restrictions could favor e-bikes, while other cities restricted e-bikes themselves. Surveys in selected cities found that many users came from bicycles or public transport. The alternative displaced was therefore not automatically a car. **ENE-K17.** [Weinert et al., §§1.2–2.4 and §3, pp. 2545–2551](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0d05f8v9/qt0d05f8v9.pdf).

Low assembly complexity can lower one entry barrier while shifting complexity upstream to component producers. It is not evidence of low total system complexity or equitable participation. Nor does the presence of a retail choice imply an unconstrained choice: road permissions, affordability and local public transport determine which options are practical. Conversely, restrictions varied enough that a uniform national coercion account would also be misleading. The observed plant tasks and local differences support mechanisms, while subjective force-field rankings and nonrandom surveys cannot identify their national magnitudes.

The environmental comparison likewise changes with its boundary. Cherry and colleagues include regional electricity and upstream battery flows in a partial life-cycle inventory. Lead production, recycling and disposal can expose people far from the rider. Different generation mixes and displaced travel modes change the comparison. The authors explicitly lack the community health measurements needed to convert lead flows into realized disease burdens. Some inventories also rely on older lead data and borrowed vehicle factors. **ENE-K18.** [Cherry et al., §§2–4 and Tables 2 and 6, pp. 282–289](https://itspubs.ucdavis.edu/download_pdf.php?id=1289).

Thus clean air at the point of use, a low purchase price and a beneficial mobility service are distinct propositions. E-bikes may compare favorably with some motorized alternatives while shifting risks to workers and communities who did not select the vehicle. The episode supports keeping those effects in the analysis; it does not establish a single monetized welfare ranking.

## What these comparisons establish—and what they leave open

The cases support a conditional account of manufacturing diffusion. Knowledge must become usable in a particular process; customers must value its actual service; complementary supply and financing must exist; and decision-makers must be able to authorize deployment. Those conditions can be assembled through very different arrangements. Apollo procurement, foreign PV demand support, EDF programme coordination and city road rules are not interchangeable forms of government involvement. Nor are factory scale, individual-unit scale and cumulative output interchangeable measurements of learning.

Labour evidence is especially uneven. PV interviews document skilled mobility and hiring restrictions; reactor accounts identify engineering and supervision work; semiconductor models identify firm learning; e-bike visits identify assembly tasks. None follows a representative worker cohort through technological transition. It would be unjustified to infer accessible employment, wage gains or bargaining strength from cheaper modules, cells or vehicles. Sources describe where work occurred more securely than who ultimately benefited.

The four comparisons in comparisons.json therefore test limited propositions: automatic cost decline, inevitable replacement by newer materials, a uniform state effect, and permanent knowledge barriers. The graph preserves alternatives and joint inputs where supported, but contains no universal prerequisite chain. The unresolved questions concern the actors and observations most easily lost in successful-product histories: cancelled projects, failed entrants, maintenance, constrained users, workers and supply-chain communities.
