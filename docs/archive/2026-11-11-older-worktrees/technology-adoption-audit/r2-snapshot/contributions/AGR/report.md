# Farming and household systems: performance, control and continued use

Research contribution AGR, 5 November 2026. This is a purposive comparison of eight cases, not a census of agricultural innovations. It covers the Lake Titicaca basin, Bali, The Gambia, two distinct Ghanaian settings, modern crop breeding across developing countries, Indian cooking interventions, and United States household production. A later four-country cooking experiment supplies an explicitly bounded contrast. The supporting ledgers contain 24 claims and 16 source records representing 15 works; two records distinguish an inspected working paper from its subsequently published abstract.

The common question is what a technology changes **within an existing production and social system**. Output per hectare, output per workday, income controlled by a particular person, reliable food through a hungry season, and freedom from arduous work are different outcomes. A technology can improve one while leaving another unchanged. In these cases, the alternative to the promoted intervention was often another workable practice: dryland crop rotation, coordinated rice calendars, tidal rice cultivation, fallowing, cautious input expenditure, continued use of an older stove, or purchased household services. These alternatives belong inside the comparison.

Access labels distinguish acquired full texts from an abstract. Reading was targeted to methods, findings and passages relevant to the claims, rather than a claim to have read every page of every work. Scanned material was inspected through locally extracted text or OCR; figures are not evidence for numerical claims unless their surrounding text states the result. Historical interpretation, simulation output and treatment effects are kept separate below.

## AGR-C01 — Raised fields in the Titicaca basin, ancient use and modern rehabilitation

Raised fields were elevated planting surfaces interspersed with canals in the highland lake basin of Peru and Bolivia. Erickson's excavations and reconstructions identify an engineered landscape, with rebuilding over time; his northern-basin chronology places early use approximately three millennia before his 1992 study. This is evidence of long-lived deployment, not an exact invention date or proof that all surviving fields operated simultaneously. Reconstruction made the physical functions testable: lifting crops above waterlogged ground, retaining nearby water, and altering the crop microclimate. His small experimental blocks had warmer, shorter frost exposure on the two reported frost nights. Scaling that thermal result to entire ancient landscapes remains an inference, not a measurement (AGR-K01). [Erickson 1992, pp. 289–296](https://anthropology.sas.upenn.edu/sites/www.sas.upenn.edu.anthropology/files/page/EricksonPrehistoricLandscapeManagementAndeanRaisedField%26EnvironImpact1992b.pdf).

The revival beginning in the 1980s illustrates why feasibility and durable uptake must be separated. Bandy's reassessment describes promoted reconstruction followed by abandonment, drawing partly on Swartley's revisits. The latter's report that her previously observed fields had ceased cultivation in 1996–97 is not a basin-wide census. Bandy also records reconstruction elsewhere through 2000. His evidence supports disappointment and reversals in the programmes described, but not a precise universal abandonment percentage (AGR-K02). [Bandy 2005, pp. 273–275](https://www.academia.edu/6455173/Energetic_Efficiency_and_Political_Expediency_in_Titicaca_Basin_Raised_Field_Agriculture).

The strongest dispute concerns the denominator. Bandy models crop rotation, fallow and maintenance labour, challenging claims that exceptional initial potato harvests established sustained superiority per workday. His review also reports heterogeneous comparisons rather than uniform raised-field inferiority. This is a contested labour-efficiency argument, not a demonstration that ancient farmers chose irrationally (AGR-K03). [Bandy 2005, pp. 280–288](https://www.academia.edu/6455173/Energetic_Efficiency_and_Political_Expediency_in_Titicaca_Basin_Raised_Field_Agriculture).

Several mechanisms therefore remain viable: obtaining more food from scarce suitable land, managing water, occupying productive lakeshore environments, and shifting seasonal work peaks. Political mobilisation of labour is another historical possibility. None follows merely from the existence of earthworks. A discriminating test would compare otherwise similar fields over complete rotations, including rebuilding, seed quality and the opportunity cost of peak-season labour. Consistent superiority after those controls would weaken Bandy's explanation; failure despite such superiority would direct attention toward tenure, organisation or changing livelihood opportunities. Ancient disappearance and twentieth-century non-adoption are separate events requiring separate explanations.

## AGR-C02 — Balinese irrigation: coordination, disruption and recombination

In the Oos–Petanu watershed, rice farmers faced an ecological coordination problem. Synchronised fallow can interrupt pest habitat, while simultaneous planting can concentrate water demand. Lansing and Kremer examined 172 subaks, or irrigation associations, and built a model linked to local geography and crop observations. Their account makes temple-linked calendars a working coordination practice rather than a decorative accompaniment to canals (AGR-K04). The important complement was information and agreement about timing, alongside the physical flow of water. [Lansing and Kremer 1993, pp. 100–103](https://faculty.sites.iastate.edu/tesfatsi/archive/tesfatsi/LansingKremer.BaliWaterTemples.pdf).

The intervention history is often told as a victory of tradition over modern seed. That formulation misses a decisive observation: the documented Manuaba calendar in 1988–89 combined temple coordination with high-yielding rice varieties, including IR64. Janssen recounts earlier national programmes that pushed more intensive cropping and describes pest and water problems and farmers' efforts to restore coordination. The evidence therefore supports selective institutional restoration and technological recombination, rather than wholesale rejection of improved varieties (AGR-K05). [Lansing and Kremer 1993, pp. 100–102](https://faculty.sites.iastate.edu/tesfatsi/archive/tesfatsi/LansingKremer.BaliWaterTemples.pdf); [Janssen, 2006 proof of 2007 article, pp. 2–3](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/34db1e42-4d4f-4b25-a7d7-16e8f023677a/download).

Independent model reanalysis matters here. Janssen reproduced the central result under its original assumptions but showed sensitivity to pest dynamics and farmers' decision rules. Synchronisation is one kind of coordination; where water scarcity dominates, coordinated staggering may be preferable. Optimising a watershed's total harvest can also distribute gains unevenly among subaks (AGR-K06). [Janssen, sections 3 and 5, especially proof pp. 19–20](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstreams/34db1e42-4d4f-4b25-a7d7-16e8f023677a/download).

This case weakens the presumption that each farmer can independently maximise yields without affecting neighbours. It does not establish that any ritual institution is optimal, or that irrigation necessarily creates either centralised states or autonomous villages. The history and the model offer different kinds of support. A useful falsifier would be an independently measured setting with the assumed pest and water interactions where disconnected schedules perform equally well. To distinguish institutional forms, one would need comparisons of feasible coordination arrangements, including their enforcement and maintenance costs, rather than a model that permits only independence or uniformity.

## AGR-C03 — Jahaly-Pacharr rice irrigation, The Gambia, 1984–1990

This project combined pumps, improved water control, mechanical land preparation, short-duration rice, purchased inputs, credit and imposed cultivation schedules. The comparison was with established swamp, rainfed and tidal rice and with competing upland crops. Carney's field research describes how land nominally registered to women could be reclassified as household fields under male control. The technical package and formal registration did not determine who controlled rice or commanded labour (AGR-K07). [Carney 1992, sections 3–5, pp. 4–10](https://media.odi.org/documents/8169.pdf).

Women's responses differed with their alternatives. Access to upland plots, independent crop rights, remuneration and local political connections affected whether they supplied family labour, negotiated a share, or worked for wages. Carney reports withdrawal from uncompensated work and the adaptation of women's labour groups to paid employment. These were responses to altered entitlements, not evidence of unfamiliarity with rice cultivation. She also documents later difficulties with input prices, machinery, spare parts, diesel and marketing; labour conflict is consequently not a complete explanation of every production decline (AGR-K08). [Carney 1992, sections 6–8, pp. 11–18](https://media.odi.org/documents/8169.pdf).

The IFPRI evaluation provides a necessary counterweight to a simple failure narrative. Its household and farm survey found substantial production and income benefits, while recognising that labour drawn into rice reduced production of other crops. Its analysis linked nutrition to income, illness, water and maternal work burdens. It also argued that lower irrigation yields in a good rainfall year could reflect sensible movement of labour back into rainfed crops: a poorly utilised project asset can coexist with improved household allocation (AGR-K09). These estimates are observational, and the authors explicitly caution about the limitations of largely cross-sectional analysis. [von Braun, Puetz and Webb 1989, pp. 9–13 and 106–107](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/42167/files/rr75.pdf).

The apparent contradiction is partly a difference in dates and outcomes: early household income gains do not refute weakened individual control, and later operational problems do not erase earlier gains. The relevant decision-makers included donors, project managers, household heads and women cultivating the crop. A stronger test of the control mechanism would compare labour participation before and after enforceable compensation or crop-rights changes while holding water, input prices and machinery reliability constant. Comparisons across ethnic groups are informative about alternatives, but do not randomly assign institutions.

## AGR-C04 — Modern crop varieties, 1965–2010, with a northwestern India resource check

This case concerns the diffusion and effects of modern varieties within the developing-country food-crop sample studied by Gollin and colleagues; it is not a history of every component of the Green Revolution. Pingali's retrospective stresses the combination of international breeding, national adaptation, irrigation and input systems. Shorter maturity could make an additional crop feasible; suitable water supply and local breeding made an introduced variety practically useful. Early gains were uneven across crops and environments. Low adoption in an unfavourable ecology cannot by itself identify conservatism or a knowledge deficit (AGR-K10). [Pingali 2012, “First GR” and “Marginal Production Environments”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3411969/).

Gollin, Hansen and Wingender use variation in the release dates of varieties and initial crop shares, rather than treating the entire before–after yield increase as a breeding effect. Their estimated direct yield gain is 44% between 1965 and 2010, holding allocations fixed; additional reallocation effects are separate. Their baseline country sample contains 90 countries. The inference relies on counterfactual relative-yield trends and the absence of coincident crop-specific shocks; event studies and robustness checks support, but cannot logically prove, those assumptions. Their large “no Green Revolution” counterfactual is expressly qualified because alternative innovation might have occurred (AGR-K11). [Gollin, Hansen and Wingender 2021, pp. 2346–2347, 2358–2365 and 2378–2379](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2021-gollin.pdf).

Resource accounting supplies a different outcome measure. Rodell and colleagues estimated groundwater loss of 17.7 ± 4.5 cubic kilometres annually across Rajasthan, Punjab and Haryana including Delhi during August 2002–October 2008, using satellite gravity observations and hydrological modelling. They investigated competing water-storage components and judged irrigation and other human withdrawals the likely cause. This is regional depletion evidence, not identification of the fraction caused by modern seeds, an estimate for all India, or a current depletion rate (AGR-K12). [Rodell, Velicogna and Famiglietti 2009, pp. 999–1002 and Methods](https://fore.yale.edu/files/rodell_velicogna_famiglietti.pdf).

The productive benefits and resource costs need not cancel one another rhetorically. They require explicit accounting of food prices, income distribution, extraction costs and foregone future water. Similarly, yield gain does not measure dietary diversity or control over earnings. A useful challenge to the breeding estimate is to identify crop-specific interventions timed with variety releases; a useful challenge to the resource mechanism is a credible water balance explaining the observed decline without net extraction. Neither test is supplied merely by observing that output grew.

## AGR-C05 — Fallowing and tenure in Akwapim, Ghana, late twentieth century

Fallowing is a productive practice whose payoff arrives after land has been left uncultivated. In Goldstein and Udry's two-year survey of Akwapim village clusters, the relevant alternative was to cultivate again sooner. Their evidence connects political position, security of control during fallow, length of fallow and subsequent output. The mechanism reverses the usual image of technology as a purchased object: the investment is deliberately waiting, and its complement is the expectation of recovering the land (AGR-K13). [Goldstein and Udry 2008, pp. 983–988 and sections IV.C–E](https://www.parisschoolofeconomics.com/gurgand-marc/PolPubWeb/GoldsteinUdry2008.pdf).

The study compares plots within households and also plots held by the same cultivator through different routes. Those contrasts help distinguish individual ability from plot-specific security. Women were less often in locally powerful positions and faced weaker practical security. The observed gender difference therefore cannot safely be attributed to farming skill or taste for investment (AGR-K14). This is carefully developed observational identification, not random assignment of political power. Its instrumental-variable interpretation still depends on restrictions concerning other pathways from political status to productivity. [Goldstein and Udry 2008, pp. 1002–1018, especially tables 7–9 and Conclusion](https://www.parisschoolofeconomics.com/gurgand-marc/PolPubWeb/GoldsteinUdry2008.pdf).

A rival account is that better-connected farmers possess unmeasured wealth, labour access or land quality. The within-person and land-provenance comparisons make some versions less persuasive, without eliminating all possibilities. A falsifying pattern would be equal expropriation risks and unchanged fallow duration after a credible security improvement, with other determinants measured. The paper does not establish that individual formal titles are the uniquely effective remedy; practical security and a legal document are different variables.

This case should also not be collapsed into the northern Ghana experiment below. The crops, institutions, rainfall environment and observed decisions differ. Their shared country label supplies no causal equivalence. Here, knowing the agronomy and having enough cash would not necessarily remove the risk that another person captures an investment's return. The farmer's feasible time horizon is partly an institutional outcome.

## AGR-C06 — Rainfall insurance and farm investment in northern Ghana, 2009–2011

Karlan and colleagues experimentally varied cash grants, rainfall-index insurance grants and opportunities to buy insurance. This design tests competing constraints more directly than asking farmers to name their principal obstacle. Insurance increased agricultural expenditure and shifted production choices toward greater risk, while cash grants alone produced relatively small investment responses. For these sampled maize farmers, the insurance-only response demonstrates that some resources could be mobilised once catastrophic rainfall risk was reduced (AGR-K15). It does not show that poor farmers never face credit constraints. [Karlan et al. 2014, pp. 599–601, experiment description and investment results](https://www.atai-research.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/Agricultural-Decisions-after-Relaxing-Credit-and-Risk-Constraints.pdf).

Insurance also had to be adopted. Subsequent demand depended on price and on experienced or network-observed payouts. The authors consider imperfect trust, recency effects and basis risk—the mismatch between an index-triggered payment and a farmer's actual loss. Thus, the complement intended to enable input adoption had its own information, measurement and credibility requirements. The conclusion explicitly leaves the welfare interpretation of increased investment unresolved, including why profit effects were limited or difficult to measure (AGR-K16). [Karlan et al. 2014, sections VI–VIII, pp. 637–650](https://www.atai-research.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/Agricultural-Decisions-after-Relaxing-Credit-and-Risk-Constraints.pdf).

The causal result is an intervention effect on decisions under the tested contracts. A separate claim that insurance produces sustained unsubsidised diffusion would need renewal and cost evidence beyond the experiment. A further claim that more inputs improved welfare would require profit, consumption and risk outcomes, including premium and delivery costs.

To distinguish distrust from perfectly reasonable concern about basis risk, a further experiment could independently vary credible payment guarantees and the correlation between payouts and individual losses. If improving credibility did nothing but better loss matching raised demand, the trust explanation would weaken. The original results justify taking risk seriously; they do not establish a single psychological account of everyone who declined the product.

## AGR-C07 — Cooking systems: Odisha's stove trial and a later supported LPG contrast

The Odisha experiment offered an inexpensive improved biomass stove through phased random assignment and followed households for up to four years. The full 2012 working paper records 2,651 households in 44 villages and evaluates a real programme with training and household responsibility for continued operation. Primary cooks' carbon-monoxide exposure initially declined, but the effect disappeared by the following year; the final 2016 published abstract reports the same central result (AGR-K17). [Hanna, Duflo and Greenstone 2012, sections II and VI](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/centers/cid/files/publications/faculty-working-papers/241_HannaDufloGreenstone.pdf); [2016 published abstract](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20140008).

Continued use of older stoves, breakage, maintenance and use practices mediated the result. The working paper found neither the expected fuel savings nor clear health improvements and recorded additional repair time. That does not license blaming users: fitting meals into a device, obtaining suitable fuel and maintaining a chimney are parts of the technology's actual service cost (AGR-K18). [Hanna, Duflo and Greenstone 2012, sections V–VII](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/centers/cid/files/publications/faculty-working-papers/241_HannaDufloGreenstone.pdf).

A contrasting intervention helps delimit the lesson. The HAPIN trial supplied free LPG stoves, continuing home-delivered fuel, instruction and adherence support to pregnant women in selected areas of Guatemala, India, Peru and Rwanda. It obtained high adherence and markedly lower measured personal particulate exposure. Yet the adjusted birth-weight difference was 19.6 grams, with a 95% confidence interval from −10.1 to 49.2 grams (AGR-K19–20). This is a null finding for one specified outcome, not proof of no health benefits whatsoever. [Clasen et al. 2022, Methods, Results and Discussion; author-manuscript pp. 6–11](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/124935/cdc_124935_DS1.pdf).

These studies change different links in the chain from equipment to use, exposure and health. They are not a head-to-head trial of two stove types. HAPIN's supported uptake does not establish willingness to pay after free fuel ends, while its birth-weight result cannot be explained simply by the low adherence observed in Odisha. Residual pollution, intervention timing and other determinants of fetal growth remain rival explanations discussed by its authors. A discriminating follow-up would change one such factor while preserving measured exposure and adherence; an ownership count alone cannot settle the issue.

## AGR-C08 — United States household appliances and time use, 1900–2005

Cowan's history follows middle-class American household routines, especially during the 1920s, using magazines, advertisements and contemporary investigations. Early washing machines still required attendance and manual handling; replacing physical exertion did not imply unattended operation. Her account also traces changing domestic service and increasing expectations around childcare and consumption. She explicitly treats some proposed advertising and changing-standard mechanisms as hypotheses, rather than demonstrated causal estimates (AGR-K21). [Cowan 1976, pp. 4–10, 13–15 and 21–23](https://paperzz.com/doc/9496641/-industrial-revolution--in-the-home).

Ramey's reconstructed time series supplies a broader, differently measured check. In the inspected 2008 working-paper version, home-production time for women aged 18–64 falls from about 46.8 hours weekly in 1900 to 40.9 in 1965 and 29.3 in 2005. Men's time rises; aggregate conclusions change with population composition and the denominator. The early series is reconstructed from heterogeneous studies and extrapolations, whereas later surveys are more representative (AGR-K22). [Ramey 2008, sections V–VIII and table 6](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w13985/w13985.pdf).

The comparison rejects both “appliances automatically liberated a fixed number of hours” and “appliances accomplished nothing because housework persisted.” Household output and standards could change, work could move from servants to household members, and the strenuousness of an hour could fall. Neither study isolates the causal effect of a particular appliance on women's employment (AGR-K23). [Cowan 1976, pp. 21–23](https://paperzz.com/doc/9496641/-industrial-revolution--in-the-home); [Ramey 2008, introduction and sections VII–VIII](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w13985/w13985.pdf).

The relevant choice set included hired services and purchased goods as well as doing a task manually or mechanically at home. Income, infrastructure and social expectations could affect both appliance purchase and time allocation. To distinguish these mechanisms would require comparable households facing plausibly independent changes in appliance access, with measures of service output, purchased help, workload and who performs it. Cowan's mainly middle-class documentary sample should not stand in for all American women, domestic workers or households without utilities. Ramey's national reconstruction broadens coverage but cannot retroactively turn every historical component into a nationally representative survey.

## What the comparison establishes, and what it leaves open

The repeated pattern is a reason to investigate complements and distribution, not a universal adoption law (AGR-K24). Three distinctions survive these contrasts. First, the technically demonstrated unit is often smaller than the unit that must keep working: a plot versus a rotation, a rice field versus a watershed, or a stove versus a household's daily cooking system. Second, benefits must reach the person whose continued cooperation is required. Third, an observed increase in adoption, effort or output needs its own welfare assessment. [Bandy 2005, pp. 280–288](https://www.academia.edu/6455173/Energetic_Efficiency_and_Political_Expediency_in_Titicaca_Basin_Raised_Field_Agriculture); [Carney 1992, pp. 8–18](https://media.odi.org/documents/8169.pdf); [Karlan et al. 2014, pp. 648–650](https://www.atai-research.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/Agricultural-Decisions-after-Relaxing-Credit-and-Risk-Constraints.pdf).

The cases also discipline inference from non-adoption. Refusal may preserve control or avoid badly insured risk; it may instead reflect a repair obstacle, unsuitable infrastructure or a poor product. No outcome alone identifies which mechanism applies. Likewise, observed use can reflect subsidy, contractual pressure or limited alternatives. Persistence is evidence that a system continued, not proof that all participants preferred it.

The graph therefore links scoped practices, institutions, resources and devices. It does not draw a single ascent from “traditional” to “modern,” infer prerequisites from dates, or treat lower throughput as technological regression. Its uncertainty is deliberate: modelling can clarify a plausible mechanism without establishing historical origin, and random assignment can identify a local treatment effect without identifying long-run market demand.

Coverage remains uneven. These materials do not adequately represent Chinese household and agricultural history, African precolonial irrigation, the Islamic world, Pacific food systems, crop transfers, commercial cold chains or tractor-service markets. Contemporary studies are much better measured than ancient labour allocation. English-language accessibility and highly cited debates influenced selection. Those omissions prevent prevalence claims and belong in the next research wave, not in an imagined universal explanation.
