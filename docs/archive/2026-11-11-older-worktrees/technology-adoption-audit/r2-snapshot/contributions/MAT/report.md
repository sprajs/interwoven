**Material technologies: branching processes, selective use, and the limits of replacement narratives**  
Research edition 1 — 5 November 2026. Contribution MAT.

The evidence does not support a universal progression in which each harder material replaced an inferior predecessor. Pottery occurred before farming; arsenical bronze could offer desirable working properties beside tin bronze; iron’s spread need not await superior heat treatment; and local glass manufacture could coexist with imported glass. The useful historical unit is a particular production process serving particular users, with access to particular resources and skills. “Bronze”, “iron”, and “glass” each conceal consequential differences in composition, manufacture, finishing, intended use, and repair.

Seven purposively chosen cases test those distinctions. The strongest evidence identifies material transformation and operating constraints. Evidence for motives, political organisation, prices and household access is usually weaker. This contribution therefore does not infer welfare gains from technical success, identify inventors from the earliest surviving object, or calculate adoption rates from excavation totals. Its 16-source ledger records 13 full-text accesses, one partial text and two abstracts. Full-text access means that relevant sections of an available article were inspected, not that every cited underlying excavation or supplementary dataset was independently verified. The West Mexican comparison has the most serious access limitation. Claim IDs identify bounded propositions; speculative mechanisms remain labelled as hypotheses. The v2 records also preserve actor and labour unknowns, discriminating tests, scoped graph relations, source lineages and four explicit comparisons.

**MAT-C01 — Forager pottery in China and Japan: occurrence is not diffusion**

At Xianrendong, Wu and colleagues associated pottery-bearing deposits with dates of approximately 20,000–19,000 calibrated years BP. BP is measured from 1950, not from the present research date. Their contextual argument combines radiocarbon samples with sediment micromorphology. Sherds show forming, tempering and firing, while exterior burning makes cooking plausible. These are observations of forager vessel use, not identification of an inventor or particular meal. Sampling reopened excavation profiles rather than discovering a complete pottery workshop; no entire vessels could be reconstructed. Farming is therefore unnecessary as a general prerequisite, but the precise first invention remains unknown. [Wu et al., 2012, pp. 1697–1699, Tables 1–2](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228089260_Early_Pottery_at_20000_Years_Ago_in_Xianrendong_Cave_China). **MAT-K01–K02.**

A separate Japanese comparison addresses use during expansion. Lucquin and colleagues studied a combined corpus of 801 ceramic/residue samples from 46 sites. Among lipid-bearing samples, conservative aquatic-food assignments were 73/156 for the Incipient and 256/566 for the Initial Jōmon groups. Strong aquatic associations persisted across environmental settings and the Pleistocene–Holocene transition. The results challenge the proposed shift toward pottery chiefly for newly available terrestrial foods. They do not show that every pot contained fish: plant processing and poorly preserved contents remain harder to detect. [Lucquin et al., 2018, Results and Discussion, Table 1 and Conclusion](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.1803782115). **MAT-K03.**

These observations separate feasibility, deployment and scaling. Making fired containers was feasible among foragers; more extensive use later need not represent a new ceramic invention or a new basic function. A mobile group might find a pot useful for repeated visits to one location without carrying it everywhere. That is a testable possibility, not a reconstructed behaviour established by these papers. Comparing vessel locations, breakage, rebuilding episodes and other container technologies would help distinguish portable household equipment from equipment kept at repeatedly used places.

The rival explanations are resource processing, social convention and changing mobility. The residue evidence constrains the first; it does not independently identify a psychological attachment to fish cooking. A stronger cultural explanation would require persistence after controlling for food availability, alternative cooking methods and residue survival. Nor does the Chinese chronological lead establish a transmission chain to Japan: securely dated intermediate sequences and shared manufacturing details would be needed. The case is an explicit refusal to draw a necessary agriculture-to-pottery edge in the dependency graph.

**MAT-C02 — Canaan: iron gained ground while bronze remained viable**

Yahalom-Mack and Eliyahu-Behar distinguish Iron I utilitarian use from Iron IIA dominance among the surveyed metal tools and weapons, approximately the tenth–ninth centuries BCE. Bronze manufacture continued. Their evidence weakens a general input-shortage explanation: Arabah copper production persisted, while they report stable average tin contents in 95 copper-alloy objects. The latter dataset was unpublished in the article. Their metallography also fails to identify a chronological heat-treatment breakthrough explaining iron’s predominance. Carbon absorption could occur during smelting, and corrosion can erase evidence needed to distinguish deliberate later treatment. These findings constrain universal scarcity and superiority stories without disproving local shortages or all performance advantages. [Yahalom-Mack and Eliyahu-Behar, 2015, pp. 286–300, especially 295–300](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/275250407_The_Transition_from_Bronze_to_Iron_in_Canaan_Chronology_Technology_and_Context). **MAT-K04–K06.**

The same study proposes continuity between bronze-working locations and iron production, with administrative involvement suggested by public architecture. This is a credible route from existing craft capacity to systematic iron production, but workshop proximity does not establish ownership, compulsion or funding. **MAT-K07.**

A contemporary adopter’s choice cannot be reduced to hardness measured on a surviving blade. Relevant but incompletely observed variables include the reliability of replacement supply, available repair work, which objects a smith could produce with existing equipment, and the cost of keeping several material streams. A material can gain use because it makes an acceptable object available, even without dominating every property. Conversely, continued bronze manufacture is positive evidence of coexistence, not necessarily evidence that artisans resisted improvement.

This case also distinguishes evidence of objects from evidence of local production. The proposed Iron I craft transition is less secure than later workshop evidence because direct early production debris is missing. An imported iron blade attached locally to bronze fittings would not demonstrate local ore smelting. To discriminate between imported semi-products, migrant specialists and local retraining, future work needs securely phased slag, furnace ceramics, ore signatures and production debris, not only finished-object similarity.

Administrative demand remains a rival to dispersed commercial demand. It would gain support from controlled access to workshops, stores of raw materials, standardised orders and production peaks linked to institutions. Independently operated domestic workshops preceding administrative installations would weaken a claim that state coordination was necessary. Neither pattern alone supplies a welfare verdict: privileged demand could increase output while restricting ordinary access. The available study does not provide the prices, labour conditions or consumption distribution needed to decide that question.

**MAT-C03 — Chinese iron: multiple routes, then selective fuel changes**

Wagner’s account is useful partly because it documents revision. Earlier interpretations of a southern, cast-iron beginning became untenable when other evidence was published. Meteoritic prestige objects, smelted bloomery iron, cast metal and practical cast implements require separate histories. His proposed diffusion and southern development mechanisms should not be converted into a definitive origin map. [Wagner, 1999, sections 1, 3.4 and 4](https://wuecampus.uni-wuerzburg.de/moodle/pluginfile.php/4671985/mod_resource/content/1/Wagner%2C%20Donald%20B.%20The%20earliest%20use%20of%20iron%20in%20China.pdf). **MAT-K08.**

Liu and Wood’s recent comparison proposes the integration of bloomery-derived knowledge, bronze casting and ceramic/kiln expertise. Cast iron’s brittleness meant that useful applications could depend on subsequent treatment; repeated forms also made repeatable treatment practicable. Their discussion highlights a division of labour and infrastructure, rather than merely raising furnace temperature. But their broader explanation, in which political control suppresses competing routes, rests on much less direct evidence. Aggregating “China” and “the West” risks making regional diversity disappear before it can test the theory. [Liu and Wood, 2025, Table 1 and sections on processing and access to technologies](https://intarch.ac.uk/journal/issue69/14/full-text.html). **MAT-K09.**

A later, deliberately separate comparison concerns Song–Yuan mineral fuel. Wagner combines texts, sulphur observations and anomalously old carbon dates in iron objects to support its use. Carbon introduced during remelting complicates identification of the exact furnace stage. He also marks product allocation and desulphurisation as uncertain. His footnote explicitly rejects the reliability of a famous extrapolation of approximately 115,000 metric tonnes of annual Song iron output, despite considering its magnitude plausible. [Wagner, 2001, p. 42 n. 6; pp. 51–53 and 58–61](https://donwagner.dk/arch-iron/resources/Wagner2001.pdf). **MAT-K10–K11.**

The defensible mechanism is complementarity: changing fuel can change blast requirements, furnace behaviour and acceptable output. A new resource need not replace charcoal uniformly or support identical products. The source does not establish a national date on which everyone changed to coke. Nor may the disputed output estimate be treated as observed productivity and then used to explain success.

Two falsification strategies follow. The cross-craft account predicts a meaningful sequence and association of casting, mould-making and treatment facilities. Workshops lacking those proposed complements, yet producing comparable objects reliably, would require revising its necessity claims. The political account predicts restrictions or differential access, not merely the eventual dominance of one process. Excavated alternative production under the supposed monopoly, together with evidence of its institutional status, is particularly valuable. Transferred objects also do not demonstrate transferred operating knowledge: familiarity with a finished casting does not disclose its ore preparation, thermal history or workshop organisation.

**MAT-C04 — Meroe: a furnace is not a complete recipe**

Humphris and colleagues’ replica campaigns moved through failures and low yields before finding more workable combinations of ore, air delivery, charging and furnace operation. Later changes included different bellows arrangements and better control of tuyères. Their interpretation of workshop space also changed through use. The authors explicitly describe some later results as anecdotal and acknowledge that they explored only a small portion of possible practice. The reconstruction concerns later and post-Meroitic workshops, not one invariant technology covering the entire production history. [Humphris et al., 2018, Tables 2–4; Lessons Learned and Conclusion, pp. 412–415](https://api.repository.cam.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/58594bce-8624-430b-822e-bdec34bf76e6/content). **MAT-K12.**

Charcoal analysis supplies a different kind of constraint. Humphris and Eichhorn examined 1,754 fragments across metallurgical and comparison contexts at Meroe and Hamadab. Metallurgical deposits show persistent selection of Acacia type nilotica; the smaller non-metallurgical comparison is more varied. Their conclusion does not demonstrate smelting-driven deforestation. Continued access could reflect management, lower production intensity, efficient operation or imported fuel. [Humphris and Eichhorn, 2019, Tables 1–2; pp. 45–50](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/332221733_Fuel_selection_during_long-term_ancient_iron_production_in_Sudan). **MAT-K13–K14.**

This case makes tacit knowledge concrete without mystifying it. An excavated furnace preserves geometry imperfectly and preserves its working rhythm less well. Operators must coordinate observable changes and material handling; an experiment that alters several variables simultaneously establishes a successful combination more readily than it isolates the contribution of each component. Modern experts also bring assumptions from their own furnace traditions. Experimental success should therefore narrow hypotheses, not retrospectively certify one ancient procedure.

The sustainability claim requires comparable discipline. Stable use of one fuel species could coexist with depletion near the workshop and an expanding procurement radius. Conversely, a large slag heap accumulated over centuries need not imply catastrophic annual consumption. To choose between those possibilities requires independently dated vegetation evidence, procurement geography, estimates of production intensity and plausible regrowth. The charcoal study neither supplies those missing measurements nor warrants a romantic assumption of perfect woodland management.

Political demand is also underdetermined. Proximity to a royal centre makes institutions relevant, but slag alone cannot reveal whether production served tribute, exchange, agricultural replacement, military supply or several purposes. Distribution of products, provenance, settlements and changing demand would test those alternatives. This contribution does not use Meroe to adjudicate the earliest invention of African iron smelting or to infer that all African iron traditions descended from one centre.

**MAT-C05 — Andean bronzes: material properties depend on the job**

Lechtman’s controlled tests compare copper–arsenic and copper–tin alloys after different working treatments. Tin bronze can reach higher hardness, while arsenical bronze can retain valuable ductility and sheet-forming performance. Archaeological applications therefore require more than a single hardness ranking. Her historical discussion describes arsenical bronze continuing alongside Inka tin bronze, with regional ore geography and imperial distribution shaping the comparison. The political account is less directly established here than the experimental properties. [Lechtman, 1996, pp. 477–479 and 500–508, Tables 4–6](https://www.academia.edu/31356952/Lechtman_Arsenic_Bronze_Dirty_Copper_1996). **MAT-K15, MAT-K17.**

Lechtman and Klein’s cosmelting experiments used Peruvian ores and archaeologically informed furnace forms. Mixtures of copper oxides with arsenic-bearing sulphides produced alloys without preliminary roasting or added flux in the tested runs. This demonstrates a feasible route, including routes through naturally mixed or deliberately combined ores. It does not identify the procedure at every ancient workshop. [Lechtman and Klein, 1999, pp. 497–499 and 523–526, Table 11](https://www.scribd.com/document/374679205/Lechtman-H-The-Production-of-Copper-Arsenic-Alloys-by-Co-Smelting). **MAT-K16.**

The alternative to a “dirty copper” narrative is not to presume that every composition was deliberately optimised. A smith could select useful ingots after smelting rather than precisely control every charge. Geological co-occurrence can produce an alloy without a separate invention of alloying; repeated task-specific selection can subsequently make that material part of a stable craft tradition. Distinguishing those mechanisms requires the relationship between ore, production residues, intermediate stock and completed objects.

Practical alternatives also differ by task. An alloy suited to thin, resilient sheet need not be the best blade material; a blade’s potential hardness need not be valuable in a small implement whose actual loading is modest. Neither “ornament” nor “tool” is a sufficient performance description. Repair frequency, fabrication losses and failure modes would help compare working systems, but those quantities are not supplied here.

Political distribution can likewise alter availability without proving universal technical superiority. A strong imperial-standardisation explanation would predict changes following incorporation, access to controlled inputs, and differences between official and local products. Persistent local alloys in equivalent tasks would test how complete that control was. The inspected sources do not establish production-wide health exposure or demonstrate that concern for workers caused substitution. Experimental reduction of a particular emission pathway is not evidence of ancient awareness, measured dose or motive.

**MAT-C06 — West Mexico: a deliberately access-limited comparison**

Hosler describes copper alloys used for tools and status objects, while sound in bells and metallic colour were significant technical objectives. Her interpretation connects those properties with ritual and war symbolism. Only the publisher abstract of this article was inspected, so no detailed prevalence estimate or ethnohistoric passage is claimed. [Hosler, 1995, abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00438243.1995.9980295). **MAT-K18.**

An earlier paper proposes maritime transmission from Central and South America, followed by regional reinterpretation and additional inputs. Again, access is abstract-only. Its approximately AD 800 beginning differs from the approximately AD 600 framing of the later paper; these are not silently harmonised into a precisely dated origin. [Hosler, 1988, abstract](https://anthrosource.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1525/aa.1988.90.4.02a00040). **MAT-K19.**

The bounded inference is that the criteria for a successful material could include controllable sound and appearance. It would be mistaken to treat that as an absence of function or to define military usefulness only as a stronger cutting edge. Equally, symbolic interpretation must not absorb every object or obscure ordinary work. The full object-level catalogue and the underlying historical testimony remain necessary to test how widely particular meanings were shared.

The diffusion and independent-development accounts require symmetrical scrutiny. Similar forms can move without the complete production sequence; shared alloys can arise where ores and desired properties are similar. A stronger transfer argument would combine chronology, diagnostic manufacturing sequences and plausible contacts. A stronger local-invention argument would need a developmental sequence rather than merely differences from external objects. This case records a researcher's proposed selective transfer; it does not turn that proposal into a proven voyage or assume that no contact occurred. Wider adoption and colonial displacement are left unresolved because the inspected evidence cannot establish them.

**MAT-C07 — Ile-Ife: primary glassmaking is stronger evidence than an origin slogan**

Chemical analysis of 52 Igbo Olokun beads identifies distinctive glass groups compatible with locally available ingredients. The selected beads represent visible diversity rather than a random population sample. Related glass elsewhere supports exchange, while imported soda-lime glass at other Ile-Ife sites indicates a connected setting. Disturbed deposits complicate the production sequence; the excavated units did not contain an intact furnace. [Babalola et al., 2018, sections 2, 3.4, 4.1–4.3 and 5](https://www.academia.edu/35714341/Chemical_analysis_of_glass_beads_from_Igbo_Olokun_Ile_Ife_SW_Nigeria_New_light_on_raw_materials_production_and_interregional_interactions_Babalola_et_al_2018_JAS_90_). **MAT-K20.**

The crucible paper reports specialised refractory clay and estimated usable capacities of 1–7 litres. Only its opening pages were successfully inspected; detailed tables were not. These capacities indicate batch capability, not annual output. [Babalola et al., 2018, pp. 31–32](https://repository.cyi.ac.cy/bitstream/CyI/1939/1/Babalola%20et%20al%202018%20Glassmaking%20crucibles%20Ile-Ife%20JAA%2016.pdf). **MAT-K21.**

The later study of incompletely molten batch adds direct evidence of transformation from raw materials. Residual quartz and feldspar, reaction products and heterogeneous glass go beyond evidence of simply reheating imported finished glass. The fragments were recovered from museum storage with recorded site attribution, rather than newly excavated sealed contexts. [Babalola, Ogunfolakan and Rehren, 2020, manuscript pp. 2–4](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10104101/3/Rehren_Babalola%20et%20al%20Antiquity%202020%20final%20version.pdf). **MAT-K22.**

That evidential sequence matters. Finished beads alone leave several production locations possible; distinctive composition narrows comparisons; crucibles locate working; partially reacted raw batch supports primary manufacture. None identifies the first person to discover the process. The authors argue for autonomous development, but a novel recipe can still incorporate knowledge about glass learned through contact. Conversely, external glass imports do not prove that local makers lacked the ability to transform local materials. “Local manufacture” and “independent invention” are different propositions.

Scaling also requires separate evidence. Many recovered beads can reflect sustained manufacture, accumulated stock, losses or later reworking of deposits. Estimating throughput needs firing frequency, successful yields, workshop duration and the number of working installations. Technical ceramics are a material complement, not an incidental container: their compatibility with heat and melt must be maintained as output grows. Exact fuel procurement and labour organisation remain open. The causes of cessation, relocation or later redevelopment are not resolved by finding a related recipe at another site.

**Comparison, repair and tests that survive the cases**

Repair and recycling make simple replacement stories still less secure. Bentley, Gilmour and Pollard discuss how iron reuse can overlap with normal composite fabrication and how selected metallographic examples suggest recycling without providing a representative rate. A Roman knife from Yarnton is discussed as incorporating an earlier blade with further metal: a concrete example of extended material use, not a prevalence estimate. Such examples are methodological controls here, not evidence for a particular recycling percentage in Canaan or early China. [Bentley et al., 2023, Introduction, production/recycling discussion and Conclusions](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/arcm.12892). **MAT-K23.** An object census therefore needs a life-history model: new feedstock, retained stock, reuse, breakage, deposition and preservation can all change what survives. A durable material that is repeatedly recovered may become less visible archaeologically, not less important economically.

The cross-case inference is conditional. Reliable output depends on interacting operations; demand specifies which properties are valuable; and institutions can support or restrict access without being necessary everywhere. **MAT-K24.** A useful graph consequently links a scoped process to its documented complements and alternatives. It does not draw ancestry from chronology or impose farming before pottery, bronze before every iron tradition, or isolation before local innovation.

Three broad explanations remain worth testing rather than declaring victorious. Resource constraint should predict changes in procurement, prices or substitutions at the relevant place and time. Skill coordination should predict learning sequences, specialised facilities and reproducibility limits. Political or symbolic demand should predict differentiated products, access and consumption, not simply a successful workshop near an important settlement. Evidence against each explanation must be admissible before its outcome is known.

The largest gaps are equally explicit. This contribution does not resolve the earliest African iron chronologies, reconstruct South Asian crucible steel, or measure household adoption across any prehistoric region. Women’s labour, coerced work, apprenticeship, occupational harms and consumers without prestigious grave goods are poorly represented in the inspected record. Those omissions prevent an account of technical capability from standing in for an account of social benefit. They are priorities for additional evidence, not invitations to invent a universal motive or forecast a future winner.
