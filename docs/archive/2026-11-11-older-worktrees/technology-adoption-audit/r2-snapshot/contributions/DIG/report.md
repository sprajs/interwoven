# Digital systems: adoption is a choice among tasks and institutions

Research edition: 5 November 2026. This contribution compares seven bounded cases; the latest observations used are from 2024. It is not a claim about the current global market. Claim IDs refer to `claims.json`; source IDs refer to `sources.json`.

The cases support a conditional explanation: a digital device spreads when a usable service, an affordable means of reaching it, and a distribution arrangement meet particular users' tasks. More computing power is sometimes useful, but it is not the same outcome as useful access. Nor does adoption show that a technology is cheapest, safest, comfortable for every task, or beneficial to every affected person. People can adopt an inconvenient interface because an alternative is unavailable, keep several devices, or use a service whose institutional requirements exclude their neighbours.

The evidence is deliberately heterogeneous. Household surveys measure access or reported behaviour; telecommunications accounts measure subscriptions and revenue; experiments compare selected users under controlled conditions; company filings describe sales and business performance. These measures cannot be stacked into a single adoption ranking. The comparisons below retain their populations, dates and units. They also distinguish a mechanism supported by observations from a mechanism that the available design can identify causally.

| Case | Population and interval | Principal comparison |
|---|---|---|
| DIG-C01 | CERN's information environment, 1989–1994 | Distributed links and reusable software versus existing documentation arrangements |
| DIG-C02 | US households, 1984–2015 | Computer diffusion, internet access and coexistence with handheld devices |
| DIG-C03 | UK adults, 2015 and 2023; US adults, 2011–2014 | Smartphones, computers and tablets across tasks and access constraints |
| DIG-C04 | India, 2014–2024; rural schoolchildren, 2018–2021 | Cheap data and household devices versus usable educational access |
| DIG-C05 | Kenyan households, 2007–2014; adults, 2024 | Mobile money versus physical transfers and existing financial services |
| DIG-C06 | UK adults, 2023; German university workers, 2022 study; Meta, FY2023 | VR gaming and prolonged desktop work versus conventional devices |

## DIG-C01 — The Web addressed an organisational problem before it was a consumer product

Berners-Lee's 1989 proposal starts with information loss as people and projects changed at CERN. Its design requirements included remote access, heterogeneous computers, linking existing information and decentralised growth. It explicitly considered text on 24-by-80 displays sufficient initially; sophisticated graphics were optional. That is evidence of the proposed problem and design priorities, not evidence that graphics were irrelevant to later diffusion. It also treated sophisticated authorisation and accounting as secondary within CERN's information-sharing environment. Security was therefore a situated trade-off, not an invariant prerequisite for adoption. [DIG-S01, “Losing Information at CERN,” “CERN Requirements” and “Non requirements”](https://www.w3.org/History/1989/proposal.html). **DIG-K01.**

A separate institutional step concerned reuse. CERN's account records the public-domain release of three software components on 30 April 1993 and a permissive, royalty-free licensing approach for a subsequent release in 1994. Permission to use and modify working code reduced a legal and transaction-cost barrier for potential implementers. It did not supply their computers, connectivity, information, maintenance or audiences. The source is a retrospective account by CERN participants, not an independent estimate of the licence's effect. [DIG-S02, “Public releases of Web software” through “The first CERN Open Source software licence”](https://home.cern/science/computing/the-birth-of-the-web/licensing-web/). **DIG-K02.**

The mechanism worth carrying forward is compatibility with existing practices and information. Requiring every participating organisation to replace its entire information system would impose a different coordination problem from linking material it already held. This is an inference from the design, not a measured explanation of worldwide take-up. Rival explanations include interface improvements, computing capacity, network reach and institutional sponsorship. To rank them would require comparative deployment records and adoption timing, including sites that had the same connectivity but chose other systems. These sources do not establish that open licensing was necessary, sufficient, or the dominant cause of the Web's expansion. Existing hierarchical documentation was an operational alternative, not an irrational predecessor awaiting inevitable replacement.

## DIG-C02 — US household computing expanded without producing a simple replacement sequence

The Census Bureau's historical CPS series reports computers in about 8% of US households in 1984, 51% in 2000 and 79% in 2015. Reported household internet use rose from 18% in 1997 to 73% in 2015. The report warns that CPS and ACS estimates differ because of survey procedures and question wording; their levels should not be joined into one seamless series. [DIG-S03, pp. 2–3, Figure 1 and comparison box](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2017/acs/acs-37.pdf). **DIG-K03.**

The 2015 ACS measured desktop/laptop access at 78%, handheld computers at 75%, and broadband subscriptions at 77% of households. Its combined category—having all three—covered 62%. Broadband here includes mobile broadband, while handheld computers include more than a narrowly defined contemporary smartphone. These are household possessions or access, not minutes of individual use. [DIG-S03, p. 2 and Table 2](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2017/acs/acs-37.pdf). **DIG-K04.**

This is a negative case against wholesale displacement. A household can retain a computer for sustained composition or specialist software while members use phones for communication away from a desk. Co-ownership alone does not prove those motives: unused legacy equipment, different household members' preferences and wealth also predict multiple devices. Consequently the graph records coexistence, not a causal claim that owning a phone creates demand for a laptop.

**DIG-K25.** The historical rates establish diffusion in this population; they do not identify what proportion came from falling equipment prices, expanding services, workplace learning, education or network availability. To test a task-complement explanation, follow the same households through device acquisitions and disposals while measuring actual tasks. It would weaken if retained computers were predominantly unused or if comparable work moved fully to phones after access constraints were removed. A hardware shipment decline alone would not settle that question, since replacement cycles and existing stocks also matter.

## DIG-C03 — Pocket access and seated work solve different problems

Among UK internet users aged 16 and over in early 2015, 33% selected a smartphone as their most important internet device and 30% selected a laptop. This is reported importance among internet users, not smartphone ownership or a share of all internet activity. Tablets were present in 54% of households. Among internet users aged 55 and over, the most important device was more often a laptop, desktop or tablet than a phone. [DIG-S04, pp. 341–347, Figures 5.14, 5.17 and 5.20](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/research-and-data/cmr/cmr15/uk_5.pdf?v=322209). **DIG-K05–K06.**

The choice set is therefore task- and population-specific. A phone can be available at the moment a person needs directions, a message or a transaction; a desk-based computer's greater capacity cannot help at that moment unless it is also available. This makes availability a plausible rival to performance as an explanation of preference. Yet the survey did not experimentally vary pocketability, processor speed, app availability, tariffs or carrier distribution. The evidence supports investigating the bundle, not declaring one component the universal winner.

The US comparison also complicates voluntary preference. Pew's late-2014 surveys found smartphone ownership among 64% of adults, against 35% in 2011; 7% of adults had both no home broadband and few alternative ways online. Within that narrowly defined dependent group, 48% reported a previous service suspension because of financial hardship. [DIG-S05, pp. 2–4 and 19; methodology pp. 44–48](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2015/03/PI_Smartphones_0401151.pdf). **DIG-K07–K08.** Owning a device did not guarantee uninterrupted connectivity. Observed reliance can reflect constrained options as well as enthusiasm.

For a direct task contrast, Ofcom's September–November 2023 survey found 68% of UK smartphone users agreed that forms and documents were harder on their phone than on a laptop or desktop. This is perceived difficulty, not a timed performance experiment or a finding that every respondent could afford a computer. [DIG-S15, Table 70, M6A, printed pp. 292–294](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/research-and-data/data/statistics/2024/adults-media-literacy-tracker/adults-media-literacy-core-survey-2023-data-tables?v=330749). **DIG-K09.** The finding is compatible with phones being valuable overall and awkward for particular inputs.

A stronger causal comparison would hold connectivity, software, training and total cost constant, then randomise access to portable and seated interfaces for different tasks. Accessibility requires its own measurements: larger screens or physical keys may help some users; voice input or a personal device may help others. Status, privacy, habit and the need to communicate through other people's chosen apps remain plausible but undermeasured here. Their importance cannot be read directly from device counts. Nor do these sources establish that most laptops cost less than most smartphones: that proposition needs a dated, geographically defined price distribution and a decision about sales weighting, used devices, subsidies and recurring service costs. **DIG-K24.**

## DIG-C04 — India's lower data unit costs did not eliminate access bottlenecks

India's Department of Telecommunications reports average wireless-data revenue realisation of ₹268.97 per GB in calendar 2014 and ₹9.12 in financial year 2023–24. In the same table, average monthly wireless-data revenue per data subscriber rose from ₹71.25 to ₹211.36. These are nominal sector averages and differently bounded annual periods, not advertised tariffs or a constant household's bill. [DIG-S06, Table 6.2, p. 61](https://www.dot.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/07/f44e3a674c85aa98cd6fa881e4462b57.pdf). **DIG-K10.** Lower revenue per unit can coexist with more spending and consumption. It does not establish affordability relative to a poor household's income or the price of obtaining a capable handset.

The same compilation reports 111.75 urban and 44.16 rural internet subscriptions per 100 inhabitants at March 2024. The urban value above 100 is a useful warning: subscriptions are not unique individuals. The gap measures subscription density, not a direct population coverage map, signal quality or independent ability to use a service. [DIG-S06, Table 1.10, p. 14](https://www.dot.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/07/f44e3a674c85aa98cd6fa881e4462b57.pdf). **DIG-K11.**

ASER provides a different unit of analysis. In its rural education comparison, the proportion of enrolled children with a smartphone at home rose from 36.5% in 2018 to 67.6% in 2021. The 2018 comparison excludes households without a mobile phone; 2021 used a telephone survey, so this must not be presented as an unrestricted census of all Indian children. Among children with a smartphone at home in 2021, 26.1% could not access it for studies, and only 27% could access it at all times. [DIG-S07, sample design pp. 15–16 and Tables 8–12, pp. 28–31](https://img.asercentre.org/docs/aser2021forweb.pdf). **DIG-K12–K13.**

The mechanism is control over a shared complement. The household purchaser and the pupil are different actors; availability somewhere in the household need not coincide with lesson time. School closures changed the value of acquiring equipment, but these repeated observations do not isolate that demand shock from income changes, prices or the general expansion of connectivity. A survey reached by phone also risks underrepresenting precisely those most excluded.

A falsifier of a simple device-scarcity explanation would be persistently low participation after reliable personal devices, suitable content and connectivity were supplied. Conversely, improved participation when shared-access constraints are removed would support control and scheduling as mechanisms. Literacy, language, electricity, teacher support, data limits and screen/input design should be measured separately. This case does not show that digital teaching is inferior to classrooms or that smartphones cannot support learning; it shows why counting household devices cannot establish either proposition.

## DIG-C05 — Kenya's mobile money was a service network built around ordinary phones

Jack and Suri describe M-PESA's 2007 deployment as an SMS-based deposit, transfer and withdrawal system. The architecture joined mobile connectivity to customer registration, identification, agents with cash and electronic balances, and bank-held funds. Advanced smartphone computing was not a prerequisite for that service. [DIG-S08, sections II–III, PDF pp. 6–11](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16721/w16721.pdf). **DIG-K14.** The relevant alternative was often getting value to another person through existing physical or financial channels, not running a desktop banking application.

The network had operational limits. In the researchers' 2008 and 2009 survey rounds, 20% and 16% of user households respectively reported withdrawal delays. Agent cash shortages and network outages were reported causes. These are reports of encountering delays, not failed-transaction rates. [DIG-S08, Tables 14a–14b, printed pp. 25–26](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16721/w16721.pdf). **DIG-K15.** A functioning handset therefore did not by itself make value immediately redeemable. Human agents' liquidity management remained part of the technology's practical operation.

The longer-run welfare evidence is more ambitious but conditional. Suri and Jack estimated that increased access raised about 194,000 households out of extreme poverty. Their design related 2014 outcomes to agent-density changes during 2008–2010, which they argued were not systematically related to relevant pre-existing characteristics. It was not random assignment of adoption. They excluded Nairobi from the later panel, experienced substantial attrition, and acknowledged that mechanisms were not all definitively established. [DIG-S09, pp. 1288–1292, equations 1–2 and Table 1](https://www.findevgateway.org/sites/default/files/publications/files/new_jack_and_suri_paper_1.pdf). **DIG-K16.** The estimate is an inference under those assumptions, not a directly counted list of beneficiary households.

A later national survey provides a separate adoption observation: 82.3% of Kenyan adults aged 18 and over reported current use of mobile money in 2024. This covers mobile money generally, not M-PESA alone; it is not a monthly-active-account measure. The report identifies phone ownership and identity-document eligibility among barriers to non-use. [DIG-S10, methodology pp. 3–5, Figure 2.3 p. 12 and usage p. 26](https://www.centralbank.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/2024-FINACCESS-HOUSEHOLD-SURVEY-MAIN-REPORT.pdf). **DIG-K17.** Its adult denominator cannot be compared directly with an earlier household measure.

This case supports remittance coordination and accessible cash conversion as mechanisms. It does not establish that every transaction became cheaper or safer, or that every expansion of mobile credit improves welfare. Alternative explanations include selective agent placement and changes in local economic opportunities. Pre-trends, independently measured access changes and results robust to attrition would strengthen the welfare interpretation; unexplained differential pre-trends would weaken it. The 2011 working paper acknowledges collaboration with Safaricom and Vodafone, and the development organisations involved have an inclusion mission. These interests do not invalidate observations but make independently collected outcomes and attention to exclusions particularly valuable.

## DIG-C06 — VR is a bounded use case, not a single adoption verdict

For a matched comparison, Ofcom's original 2023 Core Survey reports gaming on VR headsets among 4% of UK adults aged 16 and over, versus 34% on phones, 22% on consoles, 14% on tablets, 10% on laptops/netbooks and 8% on desktops. Responses are non-exclusive. The question specifies no weekly or monthly threshold. The mixed face-to-face/online quota sample contained 3,643 adults, with fieldwork from 27 September to 12 November 2023. [DIG-S15, Table 74, G1, pp. 307–308](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/research-and-data/data/statistics/2024/adults-media-literacy-tracker/adults-media-literacy-core-survey-2023-data-tables?v=330749); [DIG-S16, pp. 7–10](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/research-and-data/data/statistics/2024/adults-media-literacy-tracker/adults-media-literacy-tracker-2023-technical-report?v=330751). **DIG-K18.**

Thus VR gaming was a minority activity relative to these comparators in this population and period. The figures establish neither headset ownership nor purchase volumes, recent active usage, repeat-use retention, or the global prevalence of industrial and educational VR. Ofcom's later summary contains an inconsistent comparison-year parenthesis; the underlying table is used here. [DIG-S11, p. 68](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/research-and-data/online-research/online-nation/2024/online-nation-2024-report.pdf?v=386238). **DIG-K21.** Claims that VR has “little mass adoption” require this sort of explicit boundary.

Task duration supplies one plausible mechanism. Biener and colleagues compared a week of ordinary work in a Quest 2 virtual desktop with a week in a corresponding physical setup. Sixteen university employees or researchers completed both; two further participants stopped during their first VR day. The counterbalanced study found worse subjective ratings on several measures, including perceived productivity, and substantial discomfort. It deliberately matched a conventional display arrangement rather than giving VR every possible spatial advantage. [DIG-S12, sections 3.1–3.4, 3.6–3.7 and Discussion](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.03189). **DIG-K19.** It is evidence about this prolonged-work setup, not proof that immersive games, short training sessions or later hardware lack value. The author team includes Microsoft Research affiliation, and the small university sample limits transfer to other workers.

Financial scale is another distinct measure. Meta reported FY2023 Reality Labs revenue of $1.896 billion and an operating loss of $16.120 billion. Reality Labs includes AR and VR work, hardware, software and research; these amounts are not VR user counts or an estimate of per-headset subsidy. [DIG-S13, Note 16, pp. 124–125](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1326801/000132680124000012/meta-20231231.htm). **DIG-K20.** The filing establishes investment and commercial exposure, while management's adoption ambitions remain interested statements.

Price, wearing comfort, visibility of one's surroundings, input precision, available content, social acceptance and friends' device choices could all matter. The inspected evidence does not rank them. A useful test would vary task duration, device fit and content while following both purchasers and people who return or abandon headsets. Sustained use for tasks with a clear spatial advantage would challenge a blanket failure narrative; low repeat use even after those constraints were relieved would challenge a simple hardware-friction explanation.

## DIG-C07 — Microsoft's phone retrenchment shows why sales are not business success

Microsoft reported selling 36.8 million Lumia phones and 126.8 million other non-Lumia phones in FY2015, while recognising $7.5 billion of phone-business goodwill and asset impairments. Its shareholder letter described a narrower phone strategy. The FY2014 sales comparison covers only the post-acquisition period, so the year-on-year change is not a like-for-like demand growth rate. Non-Lumia sales must not all be treated as Windows smartphone sales. [DIG-S14, “Phone Hardware,” shareholder letter and impairment discussion](https://www.microsoft.com/investor/reports/ar15/index.html). **DIG-K22.**

This was already a deployed business with substantial sales; an unsuccessful expansion strategy is a different outcome from technical infeasibility or zero adoption. The same report describes a strategy of common applications and a unified device ecosystem. That demonstrates management's intended mechanism, not that a shortage of apps uniquely caused the impairment. [DIG-S14, “Create more personal computing”](https://www.microsoft.com/investor/reports/ar15/index.html). **DIG-K23.**

Possible competing explanations include ecosystem coordination, distribution incentives, timing, expected margins, acquisition valuation and execution. Buyers need services that work with their correspondents and institutions; developers need a reachable audience; distributors need a reason to stock and promote products. These interests need not converge simply because hardware is capable or discounted. A persuasive explanation would compare app availability, carrier support, local pricing and retention across markets and episodes. It would also distinguish an accounting reassessment of future profits from immediate user abandonment. This case ends with the documented 2015 retrenchment; it does not manufacture an unsupported terminal date for the platform.

## What survives comparison, and what remains unproved

Three distinctions are especially useful. First, adoption has several margins: acquiring a device, obtaining a connection, controlling access when needed, completing a task, and continuing to use it. Different actors can control each margin. Second, devices are often complements. A cash agent complements a digital account; a phone and a computer can serve one household; a headset can supplement an existing gaming arrangement. Third, institutional access is part of practical feasibility. Licence permissions, account identification and the distribution of shared equipment can block an otherwise functioning system.

Cheapness should therefore be tested as total cost for a specified task and person. Include equipment, service, finance, travel, time, repair, learning and switching. Subsidies can lower an upfront payment while changing later obligations; this contribution does not estimate their historical contribution. Similarly, “safer” needs an outcome and a bearer of risk. A system may reduce cash-carrying risks while creating different account, privacy or operational risks. Preference is not evidence that such costs disappear.

Pocket mobile internet plausibly expands the set of moments in which useful computing is available. The historical evidence here is consistent with that explanation and with simultaneous advantages for larger screens and physical input. It cannot establish a universal causal ordering between portability, performance and ergonomics. Status and habit should remain explicit rival hypotheses rather than convenient residual explanations for behaviour that appears puzzling.

The inspected sample is geographically narrow within the digital world. It lacks primary adoption studies from China, Latin America, the Middle East and much of Africa; it also lacks systematic price distributions, accessibility comparisons and long-term VR retention. The dependency graph is consequently a set of scoped, evidenced relationships, not a complete genealogy or a forecast. Further research should seek cases where these mechanisms fail, particularly comparable mobile-money deployments with weaker adoption and useful devices rejected despite affordable access.
