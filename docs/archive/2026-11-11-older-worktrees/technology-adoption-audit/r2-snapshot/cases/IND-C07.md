# IND-C07 — Chinese household weaving and factory-yarn hybrids

**Period:** c. 1870–1949. **Region:** China; Dingxian; Lower Yangzi; North China; Central and western China. **Domain:** cotton textiles; household production; gender and labour.

Factory yarn could displace hand spinning and support handloom weaving; household work also carried gendered meanings and constraints.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/IND/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Neither factory yarn nor the handloom is a new invention in this interval.

**feasibility:** Different yarn characteristics supported selective combination of factory warp and hand-spun weft.

**deployment:** Named districts and historical accounts describe hybrid use; exact household installations are not enumerated.

**diffusion:** Eyferth reports continuing handloom output and spread with yarn access; underlying original surveys were not audited.

**scaling:** Machine intermediates could extend hand production geographically, rather than only displace it.

**non-adoption:** Cash, alternative earnings, product requirements and gender arrangements are candidate constraints; preferences cannot be inferred from persistence alone.

**displacement:** Spinning declined faster than hand weaving; reported 1934–1936 handloom output share remains substantial.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Household hand weaving with machine-spun warp and/or hand-spun weft; regional factory-yarn access and gendered work classification.

**task:** Provide cloth with practical and social uses while sustaining household livelihoods.

**population:** Selected Chinese weaving districts and national estimates reported through earlier scholarship; household cases are not uniformly represented.

**interval:** c. 1870–1949; quantitative benchmark 1934–1936

**contemporary alternatives:** Hand-spun yarn and hand weaving; Purchased factory yarn with hand weaving; Purchased factory cloth; Other paid work where available; Different fibres or reduced clothing consumption

**adoption measure:** Reported cotton-output share for handloom cloth and qualitative hybrid-use accounts; not a census of technology preferences.

**observation limits:** Pre-1949 evidence is background in a mainly post-1949 study; underlying earlier studies not independently inspected; unpaid work is often poorly recorded.

## Actors

**chooser:** Household decision-makers, merchants and women producing cloth, with authority distributed unequally and not fully observed.

**payer:** Households supply cash/materials and unpaid or undercounted labour; merchants may mediate inputs and sales.

**operator maintainer:** Predominantly women doing spinning, weaving and household textile work, with actual regional divisions more diverse than ideals.

**affected nonchooser:** Women and girls whose time can be allocated by household norms; dependants requiring clothing.

**ability to refuse:** Persistence does not demonstrate freely chosen preference; gender prescriptions and limited cash or outside work may constrain refusal. Exact household bargaining is unknown.

## Labour

**tasks:** Hand spinning contracted unevenly; weaving could persist using industrial inputs; paid market and unpaid household production overlapped.

**entry barriers:** Embodied textile skills, access to cotton/yarn and looms, gendered expectations and alternative employment access.

**bargaining ownership:** Household and merchant relations shape resource access; the article’s discourse evidence does not identify each woman’s control of proceeds.

**transition:** Regional coexistence and interwar shares observed; no tracked cohort of spinners transitioning to factory employment.

**distribution:** Women’s work could be economically consequential yet omitted from reformers’ definitions; no full pre-1949 time or wage distribution established.

**evidence limit:** Post-1949 rationing findings cannot establish pre-1949 causation; national output estimates cannot settle household welfare.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: modular input substitution; fabric properties; market access; household cash and labour allocation; gender norms; measurement exclusion

Main source studies a later period and uses earlier scholarship for this background. National quantities are estimates; post-1949 mechanisms are not projected backward.

## Evidence links

- [IND-K20](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k20): In late Qing and Republican China, some handweavers combined machine-spun warp with hand-spun weft, and cheaper factory yarn also supported handloom weaving in new districts.
- [IND-K21](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k21): Eyferth reports that handloom cloth accounted for about 70 percent of China’s cotton textile output in 1934–1936, despite the expansion of factory production.
- [IND-K22](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k22): Eyferth documents moral and gendered classifications of textile work in late imperial and Republican China, including reform rhetoric that rendered household female production economically invisible.
