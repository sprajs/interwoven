# ANC-C03 — Rome's aqueduct service and the 537 milling emergency

**Period:** Subepisode A: c. 97–early second century CE maintenance administration; subepisode B: siege of 537 CE. **Region:** Rome, Italy. **Domain:** water supply; public infrastructure; milling; state finance.

Administrative evidence makes maintenance, funding and allocation visible; a later siege narrative describes different outcomes for drinking, bathing and milling.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/ANC/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** No invention claim.

**feasibility:** Established urban conduits and mills, not just technical descriptions.

**deployment:** Frontinus describes Rome's services and workforce; Procopius describes mills dependent on an aqueduct.

**diffusion:** No inference from Rome to every Roman city.

**scaling:** Maintenance and distribution arrangements support a large service; exact consumption not reconstructed.

**non-adoption/niche:** Animal-driven substitution reportedly restricted by fodder shortage in 537; wells remained useful.

**displacement/reversal:** Siege interruption and reported boatmill replacement; not proof of permanent universal aqueduct loss.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** A: aqueduct maintenance/distribution at Rome. B: aqueduct-fed Janiculum mills and reported Tiber boatmills during siege.

**task:** A: reliable water delivery and allocation. B: grinding during blockade, distinguished from drinking and bathing.

**population:** A: officials, enslaved work gangs and entitled water users. B: siege population and military organisers; exact coverage unknown.

**interval:** Two explicitly separate episodes, c. 100 and 537 CE.

**contemporary alternatives:** Wells and river for drinking, attested by Procopius.; Animal milling was a known but fodder-constrained option in 537.; Tiber boatmills reportedly substituted during siege.

**adoption measure:** Textual staffing and procedural account; narrative emergency sequence. No quantified diffusion or service-coverage denominator.

**observation limits:** Reported rules differ from enforcement; staffing is not employment trend; narrative sufficiency is not throughput.

## Actors

**chooser:** A: water commissioner, emperor and other authorities in defined legal roles. B: Belisarius according to Procopius.

**payer:** A: public treasury, imperial purse and some water-right revenue. B: funds and requisition arrangements not specified.

**operator maintainer:** A: public/imperial enslaved specialists and contractors. B: boatmill builders/operators not individually identified.

**affected nonchooser:** Enslaved workers; water users without decision authority; households dependent on distributions; siege residents.

**ability to refuse:** Enslaved maintenance workers did not have ordinary free-labour choice; precise coercion and resistance not measured. Household alternatives varied; siege constrained choice.

## Labour

**tasks:** Inspection, cleaning, masonry, piping and administration; boatmill assembly and tending in 537. Enslaved labour explicitly present in A.

**entry barriers:** Specialised work roles and controlled access to assets; actual training pathways unknown.

**bargaining ownership:** Public/imperial ownership and grants shape control; individual worker bargaining power unmeasured.

**transition:** No linked worker cohorts across the two widely separated episodes.

**distribution:** Access was differentiated legally; no quantified welfare or wage change by group.

**evidence limit:** Do not infer freedom, consent or secure livelihoods from useful infrastructure; no employment multiplier calculated.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: Recurrent finance, workforce, access and repair schedules complement physical assets.; Allocation rights shape who benefits.; Emergency substitution depends on resources required by each alternative.

Frontinus is prescriptive and self-presentational; Procopius is a single later narrative. The two subepisodes cannot be treated as one continuous panel.

## Evidence links

- [ANC-K07](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#anc-k07): Roman productive waterpower and enslaved hydraulic maintenance contradict an absolute incompatibility between slavery and complex machinery; the effect of labour costs on marginal adoption remains unidentified.
- [ANC-K08](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#anc-k08): Frontinus reports about 240 publicly owned and 460 imperial enslaved aqueduct workers organised into specialised maintenance functions.
- [ANC-K09](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#anc-k09): Frontinus describes recurrent funding and timed, sequential shutdowns as arrangements for maintaining Rome's aqueduct service.
- [ANC-K10](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#anc-k10): Bannon's evidence distinguishes civic aqueduct grants from other water rights and shows roles for local control, payment and status; infrastructure did not imply a single empire-wide administration or equal access.
- [ANC-K11](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#anc-k11): Procopius reports aqueduct disruption stopping Rome's mills in 537, fodder scarcity limiting animal substitution, and Tiber boatmills restoring grinding while wells still supplied drinking water.
- [ANC-K22](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#anc-k22): These cases show why cessation of an installation, substitution of its service, and loss of technical knowledge must be tested separately; survival of a named system likewise does not establish unchanged operation.
