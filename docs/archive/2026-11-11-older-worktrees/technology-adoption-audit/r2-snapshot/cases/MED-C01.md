# MED-C01 — Variolation expertise across Ottoman, African and British networks

**Period:** c. 1700–1723. **Region:** Ottoman Empire; North Africa (reported testimony); Britain; colonial Boston. **Domain:** health; preventive practice; knowledge transmission.

Documented channels expose non-European and non-elite expertise, while leaving uptake denominators and origins uncertain.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/MED/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Not established; older origin traditions are disputed.

**feasibility:** Contemporary reports describe induced infection and subsequent protection, with selection and follow-up limitations.

**deployment:** Reported Ottoman and African use; specified British and colonial encounters.

**diffusion:** Qualitative correspondence and institutional discussion; no population diffusion curve.

**scaling:** Not quantified.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Variolation using infectious smallpox material, distinct from later vaccination; methods were heterogeneous.

**task:** Reduce risk of later natural smallpox, while incurring an immediate intervention risk.

**population:** Practitioners, recipient families and named informants in surviving reports; not representative Ottoman/African/British populations.

**interval:** c. 1700–1723, with older histories treated as retrospective context.

**contemporary alternatives:** Natural infection risk without variolation; Locally varying inoculation practices

**adoption measure:** Qualitative reports of use and knowledge transfer; numerator and eligible-population denominator unavailable.

**observation limits:** Elite and colonial mediation; informant motives and precise identities cannot be fully reconstructed; no valid common risk ratio.

## Actors

**chooser:** Parents, practitioners and patrons in documented encounters; children were frequently not the decision-makers.

**payer:** Family or patron payments appear in reports; systematic cost evidence absent.

**operator maintainer:** Experienced local practitioners including women; household aftercare not quantified.

**affected nonchooser:** Children and dependent people subjected to others' choices; potential contacts were not enumerated.

**ability to refuse:** No representative consent record. Onesimus's enslavement constrains interpreting testimony as an autonomous market transaction; household authority over children is evident, adult refusal rates unknown.

## Labour

**tasks:** Hands-on inoculation and knowledge brokerage; some healers were remunerated, unpaid care unmeasured.

**entry barriers:** Experiential skill and trusted access; formal entry requirements varied and are not established here.

**bargaining ownership:** Elite writers controlled much surviving attribution; practitioner bargaining power and earnings unknown.

**transition:** No individual employment-transition series; persistence of practice inferred only from reports.

**distribution:** Knowledge contributors span gender/status groups; no income gains established.

**evidence limit:** Cannot estimate occupational growth, remuneration or changes in caregivers' total workload.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: practitioner skill; brokerage of credibility; epidemic risk perception; record and status bias

Prior local dissemination and epidemic timing could explain uptake independently of elite brokers; surviving reports are selective.
