# LBR-C01 — US telephone switching: delayed automation, displaced incumbents and later entrants

**Period:** 1880s–1940 (main labour study 1910–1940). **Region:** United States. **Domain:** communications; labour; organisation.

Mechanical switching required a service system to change, while the labour consequences differed between incumbent operators and subsequent entrants. The study is a bounded counterexample to inferring individual opportunity from aggregate employment resilience.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/LBR/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Mechanical switching existed by the 1880s in the author-summary account.

**feasibility:** Feasible apparatus did not establish viability across AT&T’s interdependent service system.

**deployment:** AT&T began automating in the late 1910s; local cutovers provide study timing.

**diffusion:** Over half the US network was converted during 1920–1940 according to the study; not every exchange at once.

**scaling:** Technical change, system adaptation and market scale enter the authors’ account; their relative weights are not adjudicated here.

**non-adoption:** Smaller/manual exchanges persisted; exact service-area comparison requires full adoption-paper audit.

**displacement:** Operators’ tasks were displaced, with separate outcomes for incumbents and future cohorts.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Mechanical local call switching replacing manual operator switching in US exchanges

**task:** Connect telephone calls within an interdependent service network

**population:** Adopting exchanges; main later-cohort results for white American-born women aged 16–25 in smaller US cities; separate linked incumbent sample

**interval:** Main labour observation 1910–1940; adoption context 1880s–1940

**contemporary alternatives:** Manual switching; Hybrid/manual service in other exchanges or tasks

**adoption measure:** Exchange cutover timing and network penetration; census employment/occupation and selected wage outcomes. Cohort and incumbent denominators differ.

**observation limits:** Newspaper coverage and genealogical linkage are selective; census decade intervals obscure short transitions; occupational scores are not each individual’s observed pay.

## Actors

**chooser:** Telephone company management selected system adoption and exchange cutovers.

**payer:** Company investment funded through business revenues/capital; precise incidence on subscribers not estimated here.

**operator maintainer:** Operators, switching technicians and service staff; task balance changes.

**affected nonchooser:** Displaced operators, prospective entrants and subscribers.

**ability to refuse:** Workers did not individually control equipment replacement; ability to move into alternative employment varied. No comprehensive measure of refusal rights or bargaining acquired.

## Labour

**tasks:** Manual switching displaced; other clerical/service work expanded for later entrants.

**entry barriers:** Gender/racial hiring boundaries and occupational skills matter; main sample excludes many groups, so equal accessibility is not established.

**bargaining ownership:** Company ownership controlled automation; causal effect of bargaining arrangements not established.

**transition:** Incumbents tracked a decade later; future cohorts observed separately. Short unemployment spells incompletely captured.

**distribution:** Incumbents versus later cohorts diverge; racial/immigrant populations cannot be collapsed into main estimates.

**evidence limit:** No proof that every displaced worker could enter the growing roles, or that aggregate gains compensated losses.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: organisational complementarity; scale economics; task displacement; cohort-specific adjustment

No overall employment decline for later cohorts does not erase incumbent loss; limited-power wage tests cannot establish unchanged earnings.

## Evidence links

- [LBR-K01](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#lbr-k01): The AT&T historical study attributes delayed switching automation to organisational interdependencies and market-scale economics, rather than treating invention as immediate deployability.
- [LBR-K02](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#lbr-k02): In the telephone-automation study, incumbent operators and later young female cohorts experienced different labour-market outcomes; stable later-cohort employment did not mean incumbent workers were unharmed.
- [LBR-K03](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#lbr-k03): The main later-cohort comparison focuses on white American-born women aged 16–25, while incumbent operators are followed through a separate linked census sample; neither sample directly represents all affected workers.
- [LBR-K04](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#lbr-k04): The authors find no clear overall wage effect for later young female cohorts, but the relevant tests cannot exclude wage changes around plus or minus 10 percent; no detected effect is not proof of unchanged pay.
