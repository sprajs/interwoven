# MOB-C01 — Roman freight: complementary modes and the cost of an entire journey

**Period:** Roman imperial era, with empirical pottery subepisode c. 250–400 CE. **Region:** Roman Mediterranean; North Africa; Western Asia; Lowland Britain. **Domain:** transport; shipping; roads; logistics.

An imperial-scale route model and a sharply bounded British pottery study expose why cheaper sea carriage per segment does not determine all door-to-door freight choices.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/MOB/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** No single invention investigated; roads, carts, ships and river transport predate the episode.

**feasibility:** Established land/water transport is documented; ORBIS reconstructs possible routes under stated assumptions.

**deployment:** Road, river and coastal networks existed; actual lowland British pottery distributions supply the empirical subepisode.

**diffusion:** No temporal adoption curve. Artifact distributions indicate movement; modeled availability is not measured use.

**scaling:** Roman-scale connectivity is a reconstructed system property, not an independently identified expansion effect.

**non-adoption/niche:** Water routes were geographically and seasonally restricted; road and transfer legs remained relevant.

**displacement/reversal:** No empire-wide displacement or post-Roman collapse is established by this case.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Subepisode A: ORBIS v1 road/river/sea network reconstruction. Subepisode B: distribution of 14 identified late Roman pottery industries in lowland Britain.

**task:** Move goods between production, distribution and consumption sites; avoid conflating imperial communication speed with freight cost.

**population:** Modeled travelers and grain shipments in ORBIS; excavated pottery at sampled British settlements in the archaeological study. These are different observational populations.

**interval:** Model combines Roman-era evidence; pottery evidence c. 250–400 CE.

**contemporary alternatives:** land carriage by pack animals/carts; river carriage; coastal or overseas sailing; local production rather than distant purchase (not directly measured)

**adoption measure:** No adopter denominator. Pottery occurrence/proportion by weight and modeled route costs are spatial proxies, not annual transport shares.

**observation limits:** Surviving records concentrate on selected places and commodities. Pottery sample is predominantly rural and excludes most military transport; the model treats some nonsynchronous features as contemporaneous.

## Actors

**chooser:** Merchants, carriers and officials plausibly made route choices, but their individual choices are not observed here.

**payer:** Freight purchasers and state redistribution appear in the source framing; actual incidence by class is unknown.

**operator maintainer:** Sailors, port workers, carters and road/river maintainers; labour status and repair financing are not measured in this corpus.

**affected nonchooser:** Producers, consumers and road/port-adjacent communities; burdens on enslaved or requisitioned workers are not quantified.

**ability to refuse:** Unknown by actor. A reconstructed route does not establish freely chosen use; geography restricted alternatives, but this is not evidence of legal compulsion for a named journey.

## Labour

**tasks:** Carriage, loading, unloading, warehousing and transfers are treated as distinct cost-generating tasks; paid/unpaid division unknown.

**entry barriers:** Ships, animals and local route knowledge were complements; access to ownership and training not measured.

**bargaining ownership:** No direct bargaining or ownership distributions reconstructed.

**transition:** No individual or cohort labour transition data.

**distribution:** No group-level income or harm estimates.

**evidence limit:** This case tests modal-cost and measurement propositions, not occupational opportunity or living standards.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: full-journey cost; seasonality; material and geographic constraints; multimodal complementarity; measurement sensitivity

Britain is not the Mediterranean; rural pottery is not army supply or grain. Transport-cost and social-distribution explanations overlap.

## Evidence links

- [MOB-K01](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mob-k01): ORBIS models Roman connectivity as a seasonally variable combination of road, river and sea routes; its route outputs are modeled opportunities rather than observed adoption or actual journeys.
- [MOB-K02](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mob-k02): For pottery movement in lowland Britain c. 250–400 CE, Wiseman and colleagues infer best-fit road costs roughly three times river and four times sea costs, with wide uncertainty and regional river variation.
- [MOB-K03](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mob-k03): Segment-level freight prices can misstate door-to-door modal advantages when transshipment, storage, port charges and access legs are omitted.
