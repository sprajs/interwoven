# ENE-C01 — Crystalline-silicon PV: changing markets and production capabilities

**Period:** 1970–2012. **Region:** United States; Japan; Germany; China; International supply chain. **Domain:** energy; industrial manufacturing.

A linked set of episodes distinguishes early terrestrial-market formation, later module process improvements, and Chinese producer entry from domestic deployment. The evidence tests learning-only and cheap-labour-only explanations.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/ENE/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Prehistory; no single invention date established here.

**feasibility:** Repeatable crystalline-silicon cell/module production existed before the sampled expansion; subsequent yield and material improvements measured in reconstructed models.

**deployment:** US space and off-grid terrestrial tasks in the 1970s; later policy-supported grid markets in Japan/Europe.

**diffusion:** Dated Chinese production/export shares and qualitative supplier/worker knowledge circulation; no household adopter census.

**scaling:** Factory size, efficiency, material use/prices and process yield changed during 1980–2012.

**non-adoption/niche:** Early terrestrial niches and very small Chinese domestic-demand share despite major export manufacturing.

**displacement/reversal:** No general displacement of fossil generation or quantification of net welfare established.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Crystalline-silicon modules; subepisodes: US terrestrial market formation (1970s), worldwide production-cost changes (1980–2012), Chinese downstream entry (2003–2009).

**task:** Provide electrical energy in remote/space tasks and later grid-connected installations; produce saleable modules meeting market quality.

**population:** Module manufacturers/industry averages in cost studies; selected Chinese firms and equipment suppliers in interview/patent study; installations are only indirectly observed.

**interval:** 1970–2012, with nonidentical subepisode windows explicitly separated.

**contemporary alternatives:** Spacecraft primary batteries for finite missions; Remote diesel or extending electricity connections where feasible; Conventional grid electricity; Other PV chemistries; not cost-equivalent in every sampled period

**adoption measure:** Module production/cost series; 2008 Chinese world-production/export shares; separate 2009 global-demand share. No common adopter denominator across subepisodes.

**observation limits:** Module manufacture is not installed-system service; nominal output is not lifetime energy; model variables mix sources and interviews select producers.

## Actors

**chooser:** Space/remote-system purchasers, later system owners under national support regimes, and firms choosing production investment.

**payer:** Government procurement/public research; private firm capital; tariff/tax-supported customers in later grid markets; individual financing shares not identified.

**operator maintainer:** Manufacturing engineers, technicians and production workers; system installers and owners. Vendor training is documented.

**affected nonchooser:** Ratepayers/taxpayers, production communities and workers; their net burdens/benefits were not measured in these studies.

**ability to refuse:** Producer entry is a firm choice; downstream buyers face task and connection constraints. Foreign recruitment/no-poach arrangements are reported, but worker consent, legal enforceability and household ability to decline support costs are not established.

## Labour

**tasks:** Vendor training, line operation/adaptation, quality control, manual/automated module assembly, recruitment of experienced managers.

**entry barriers:** Skilled process knowledge and capital; accessible downstream turnkey equipment reduced some firm-entry barriers, while upstream secrecy and limited suppliers impeded entry.

**bargaining ownership:** Firms controlled equipment and hiring. Interviews report poaching disputes and agreements not to recruit rivals’ skilled staff; wage/bargaining effects unmeasured.

**transition:** Some executive migration histories documented; no longitudinal worker transition or retraining cohort.

**distribution:** Distinct opportunities for internationally trained managers versus assembly tasks; no reliable wages, gender, working-condition or displaced-worker distribution.

**evidence limit:** Reduced labour cost per module cannot establish fewer total jobs, better livelihoods or who retained gains.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: Procurement and demand composition; R&D and process control; Equipment trade plus tacit skill; Investment risk and factory scale; Supply-chain entry barriers

Decomposition allocations differ strongly by model; low wages, energy cost, finance, foreign incentives and technology transfer covary.

## Evidence links

- [ENE-K01](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ene-k01): For US PV in the 1970s, the shift from a space-program buyer toward terrestrial users changed product requirements, standardization and market structure; the early fall in module prices cannot be read wholly as factory learning.
- [ENE-K02](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ene-k02): PV cost decomposition is model-sensitive: for 1980–2001 Nemet assigns 43% of decline to plant size, whereas Kavlak et al. assign 8%; the latter assigns 36% for 2001–2012 and separates efficiency, material use, prices and yield. Their reconstructions also identify early silicon supply/process benefits inherited from the semiconductor industry rather than generated entirely by PV output.
- [ENE-K03](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ene-k03): China’s emergence as a PV producer in the late 2000s did not imply similarly large domestic deployment: the inspected 2010 study reports over one-third of world production in 2008 with over 95% exported, and about 2.2% of global demand in 2009.
- [ENE-K04](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ene-k04): Interviews and supply-chain evidence from early Chinese PV entry support imported production equipment plus skilled personnel as important channels; upstream process secrecy and sparse equipment markets constrained entry despite downstream success.
