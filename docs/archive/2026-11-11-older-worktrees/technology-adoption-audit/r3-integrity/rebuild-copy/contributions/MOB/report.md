# Transport adoption: systems, power and uneven consequences

Research contribution MOB · 5 November 2026 · seven bounded cases

Transport adoption is often explained as replacing a slow or expensive vehicle with a faster or cheaper one. These cases support a more specific proposition: a useful vehicle must fit a journey, a supply and repair system, an investment arrangement and a distribution of decision-making power. Different people can experience the same change as improved access, a tax liability, lost work or loss of control over land. Neither construction nor increasing freight volume establishes their consent.

This is a purposive comparison, not a representative sample of transport history. It follows seven episodes from Roman freight to Singapore container terminals. The main non-European evidence concerns Andean roads, colonial India, Ghana and Singapore. Fourteen sources were available as full text and their relevant sections were inspected; several are explicitly dated author manuscripts, one is author-posted OCR, and one is a German scholarly article. “Full text” describes access, not a claim that every page or cited original was independently audited. The accompanying ledgers distinguish original archaeological or quantitative research, inherited historical narratives and primary judicial or official records. They also record unsuccessful access to important further leads in `questions.md`.

## 1. Roman freight: the complete journey changes the comparison

The Roman case tests a measurement assumption before telling an adoption story. ORBIS represents communication through roads, rivers and sea routes, with time and expense changing by mode and season. Its authors explicitly distinguish modeled opportunities from actual travelers’ experiences. The model combines some features documented at different Roman dates; it is not a photograph of a network in one year, much less a census of its users. Its routes therefore establish neither voluntary uptake nor how often Romans chose each mode. **MOB-K01.** [MOB-S01, pp. 7–11 and 19](https://orbis.stanford.edu/orbis2012/ORBIS_v1paper_20120501.pdf)

This matters because “water was cheaper” can compare different things. A sailing price omits the cart trip to a port, storage, loading, unloading, charges and the onward journey. A customer needs the complete delivery. Wiseman, Ortman and Bulik infer transport costs from the distribution of pottery in lowland Britain rather than applying only surviving Mediterranean freight prices. Their best-fit figures put road costs around three times river and four times sea costs, with wide uncertainty and variation between river systems. They challenge very large, universalized water advantages; they do not claim that water carriage had no advantage. The sample is mainly rural, poorly covers large towns and largely excludes military transport. **MOB-K02–03.** [MOB-S02, §1.1, Table 3 and §5](https://api.repository.cam.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/7291f9ad-f581-44cb-9e21-a63889ffd40b/content)

The contemporary alternatives were consequently complementary as well as competitive. A road could connect a producer to a water route; it could also avoid the cost of making several transfers. Geography, season, cargo quantity and available endpoints condition that choice. This is a synthesis of the two studies, not an observed Roman preference ranking. A model that finds an inexpensive route cannot tell us whether the trader possessed the required animals, information, credit or permission.

The strongest rival explanation for the archaeological pattern is that social supply arrangements or product preferences created distance decay that the model attributes to costs. Independent shipment accounts or comparable evidence for other commodities would discriminate better. Road-building labour, slavery, requisition, maintenance finance and household gains remain underexamined here. The case establishes a reason to investigate those complements; it does not establish their distribution from the road network’s impressive size.

## 2. Inka roads: imperial integration did not exhaust local uses

Inka expansion was not the invention of Andean movement from nothing. The ICOMOS evaluation describes incorporation of earlier routes, improvements and new connections, with stores, shelters, drainage, bridges and other engineering adjusted to terrain. The unit that scaled was a linked logistical system. The evaluation also records later adaptation and community maintenance. Those later observations show continued use of parts of the infrastructure, not survival of an unchanged imperial organization. **MOB-K04.** [MOB-S03, printed pp. 321 and 324–325](https://whc.unesco.org/document/152728)

A particularly useful corrective comes from Francisco Garrido’s survey and excavations around Cachiyuyo de Llampos in the Atacama. His fieldwork covers a 58-square-kilometre area and twelve kilometres of road. Scarce water and forage lead him to infer predominantly pedestrian traffic on that segment, in contrast with more suitable Andean caravan routes. More importantly, mining camps shifted toward the road and their craft, domestic and roadside remains differ from those at the formal Inka outpost, Tambo Medanoso. He interprets the pattern as local miners exploiting new exchange opportunities beyond directly administered production. **MOB-K05–06.** [MOB-S04, §§4–6, pp. 98–106](https://www.academia.edu/26536064/Rethinking_Imperial_Infrastructure_A_bottom_up_perspective_on_the_Inca_Road)

That interpretation qualifies both a triumphal infrastructure story and an equally totalizing account of state control. Imperial conquest, labour obligations and provisioning arrangements are part of the wider system. Yet evidence of these institutions elsewhere does not establish that every miner on this segment worked as a directly commanded labourer. Conversely, household-looking architecture cannot prove freedom from taxation, permission requirements or informal supervision. The rival of indirect imperial control remains plausible.

The adoption measure is archaeological: settlement positions, assemblages and differentiated facilities. It is not the proportion of all subjects enjoying road access. The people who decided to connect a territory were not necessarily those contributing labour, carrying provisions or changing their local livelihood. Some miners may have gained an exchange opportunity while other subjects bore costs. Direct evidence of permission, tribute and household production would be needed to resolve that distribution.

The relevant negative comparison is ecological rather than an imagined contest between a “primitive” and an “advanced” vehicle. A pack-animal configuration suitable on one route need not be suitable across a dry segment without forage. Evidence for corrals, feed provision or transport-specific animal use could weaken the pedestrian interpretation. Absence of such evidence must first be evaluated against excavation and preservation conditions.

## 3. Indian railways: three different populations, three different outcomes

Indian railway expansion combined transferred engineering with colonial decisions about finance and territory. Early concessions guaranteed returns on invested capital, exposing Indian revenues to shortfalls. Government route planning included military control alongside commercial and humanitarian aims. A line’s construction therefore cannot be interpreted simply as consumers choosing a superior service. The contemporary choice set also included bullock carriage, river and coastal shipping, alternative alignments and different financing arrangements. **MOB-K07.** [MOB-S06, §2, pp. 6–8](https://idei.fr/sites/default/files/medias/doc/conf/icied/papers_2011/bogartbis.pdf), [MOB-S05, §I.B–C](https://dave-donaldson.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/Donaldson_RRRaj_AER.pdf)

Donaldson’s published study provides substantial evidence of economic gains. It links lower transport costs to price convergence and trade, and reports a rail-access coefficient of 0.164 log points for real agricultural income per unit area in its district panel. The paper describes this as about 16 percent; exponentiating the coefficient gives about 18 percent. Its outcome is crop output valued at local prices, deflated and divided by land area—not each resident’s disposable income. Planned-but-unbuilt lines provide a useful disconfirmation test: they do not show the same apparent effects in the reported placebo exercises. Selection and model assumptions remain material. **MOB-K08.** [MOB-S05, §§III–V and Table 4](https://dave-donaldson.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/Donaldson_RRRaj_AER.pdf)

Company outcomes tell a different story. Bogart and Chaudhary find lower working expenses after state purchases at contractually determined dates. Changes in expense categories and employment support a labour-cost channel. They lack direct railway wage data, so reduced wage rates cannot be asserted. Selected quality and timing checks weaken explanations based solely on reporting changes or service deterioration. But lower employment expenditure still cannot be translated into a gain for every worker, particularly without evidence about subsequent jobs. The inspected source is their April 2011 manuscript; its numerical result is not silently substituted for the later publication. **MOB-K09.** [MOB-S06, pp. 22–25 and Tables 7–9](https://idei.fr/sites/default/files/medias/doc/conf/icied/papers_2011/bogartbis.pdf)

A third population comprises financial investors. The authors’ April 2019 accepted manuscript estimates an average annual total return of 3.7 percent on guaranteed securities over 1880–1908 and no average abnormal return in the three years before government purchase. This challenges a particular hypothesis of exceptionally profitable securities. It does not cancel taxpayer exposure, prove fair land acquisition or rule out other colonial transfers. A guarantee on original capital and a return to a later purchaser at a market price are also different quantities. **MOB-K10.** [MOB-S07, pp. 2–3 and Table 2a](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~dbogart/extractive_copyacceptedJIE-March2019.pdf)

The unresolved comparison is not simply public versus private competence. The colonial state’s fiscal incentives and limited accountability to workers shaped what “lower cost” meant. Trade gains, fiscal costs, worker displacement and coercion can coexist; the available studies do not reduce them to a common welfare measure.

## 4. Ghana: a railway can lose traffic while its towns retain importance

The Ghana case separates the persistence of a technology from the persistence of a geography. Jedwab and Moradi reconstruct the western line’s mining and military purposes and the eastern route’s different politics. Construction and opening were not instantaneous adoption: the western route reached Kumasi in 1903, while the eastern route reached it in 1923 after wartime delays. Planned alternatives were not technically identical to an actually operating railway. **MOB-K11.** [MOB-S08, §2.1, pp. 5–6](https://alexandermoradi.org/research/rail.pdf)

Their spatial study connects railway access with cocoa expansion and settlement growth, using geographic controls, proposed but unbuilt routes and instruments to probe selection. It also follows later decline: rail traffic fell sharply after 1974 as roads expanded, while earlier railway towns retained relative importance through 2000. The authors discuss both inherited assets and the coordination advantages of established settlement. Their historical account adds a service–revenue–maintenance feedback and a changing cocoa frontier involving forest conversion. **MOB-K12–14.** [MOB-S08, §§2.2, 4–6 and Table 3](https://alexandermoradi.org/research/rail.pdf)

This is evidence against assuming that a newer mode redistributes activity uniformly. It is not evidence that old railway places could prosper without transport: roads often preserved their absolute access. Nor does concentration by itself establish an optimal national arrangement. People and investment could remain in a town because other people were there, because amenities persisted, because roads served it, or because some relevant geographical advantage was omitted. The paper’s controls support persistence more strongly than a uniquely identified explanation for it.

The contrast with India concerns both inference and actors. African farmers were responsive economic agents in the account, not merely passive recipients of colonial rail. That does not make the initial route priorities consensual or eliminate questions about migrant labour, tenure, forest users and bypassed regions. A farmer’s decision to plant an export crop is different from a community’s ability to refuse a railway, land claim or tax.

For occupational transition, the missing information is consequential. The study supplies spatial and aggregate evidence, not linked careers showing whether displaced head-loaders or railway employees gained better work. A maintenance failure may be a public-finance problem, an organizational problem, a demand problem or a feedback among them. More reliable vehicles alone would not identify which constraint dominated on a particular line. Line-level accounts and repair histories would be a useful falsification exercise; they have not been reconstructed here.

## 5. Early electric mobility: feasibility, enterprise survival and niche use

The electric-cab episode makes stage distinctions unusually clear. In 1897 Morris and Salom’s New York venture operated electric vehicles while retaining ownership and specialist responsibility. It sold journeys rather than asking every passenger to buy and maintain a machine. Kirsch’s reconstruction nevertheless suggests the first months were unprofitable. Demonstrated operation established an alternative configuration, not a successful business at scale. Regional EVC enterprises subsequently failed; the parent company’s default in December 1907 was a later event. Production delays, fires, litigation and financial organization appear alongside technical problems. **MOB-K15–16.** [MOB-S09, pp. 306–307](https://thebhc.org/sites/default/files/beh/BEHprint/v026n2/p0304-p0310.pdf)

Mom’s comparative history further separates early battery failures from later applications with suitable duty cycles and disciplined maintenance. Commercial and specialized electric fleets could function even as the general private-car market moved toward gasoline. His account rejects a single universal turning point at the electric starter: its significance differed by segment, and the Model T received a starter only in 1919. Battery upkeep, purchase cost and the usefulness of a vehicle for multiple kinds of trips remain substantive explanations. A useful local fleet is not evidence that the same configuration could serve general touring at comparable cost. **MOB-K17.** [MOB-S10, pp. 281–283](https://zeithistorische-forschungen.de/sites/default/files/medien/material/2017-3/Mom_1997.pdf)

Complement-building also had a history. The Electric Vehicle Association of America, formed in 1909, promoted utility fleets, charging garages, off-peak tariffs and a common charging plug. These were organized attempts to improve the surrounding system. Kirsch’s suggestion that earlier utility coordination might have preserved a larger electric role is a counterfactual, not an observed result. The evidence establishes that the effort occurred and that coordination was incomplete; it does not measure what an earlier association would have achieved. **MOB-K18.** [MOB-S09, pp. 308–309](https://thebhc.org/sites/default/files/beh/BEHprint/v026n2/p0304-p0310.pdf)

The rivals are therefore not “physics” and “society” as mutually exclusive explanations. Poor batteries can increase maintenance needs; ownership arrangements determine who performs that maintenance; finance determines whether a service can survive while solving problems. Later fleet success weakens a timeless claim of intrinsic uselessness, but improved batteries and different tasks prevent it from being a clean counterfactual for the original EVC.

The labour evidence is thinner than the system history. Specialist driving, charging, repair and dispatch were necessary tasks in the service configuration, but the inspected sources do not trace workers from failed electric firms into later occupations. They also do not establish residents’ consent to the external effects of competing urban transport systems.

## 6. Containers: compatibility and work relocation, with a causal warning

Postwar containerization joined boxes to compatible ships, lifting equipment, terminals and inland transport. Bernhofen, El-Sahli and Kneller distinguish 1956 US deployment from their international diffusion period beginning in 1966. Their historical account describes early proprietary systems, reluctant ports, standards compromises, US subsidy specifications and military logistics demand. These were contributions to scaling; none establishes that a particular dimension, war or institutional bargain was universally necessary. First national facilities also differ from widespread utilization. **MOB-K19–20.** [MOB-S11, §§2.1–2.3, pp. 38–40](https://qn1-next.xuetangonline.com/16800654811573.pdf)

The published econometrics contain a warning that is easily lost in summaries. Very large estimates based only on adoption dates do not convincingly identify causal effects when pre-adoption trade trends differ. Product-containerizability comparisons provide stronger, smaller evidence: the benchmark North–North differential is 17.4 percent, while the world comparison accumulates roughly 14 percent with delayed effects. These are particular bilateral product-trade comparisons; robustness and classification choices change magnitudes. They are not a universal port return or an established percentage contribution to world GDP. Older working-paper headlines must not replace the published qualification. **MOB-K21.** [MOB-S11, p. 37, Tables 2–3 and pp. 44–46](https://qn1-next.xuetangonline.com/16800654811573.pdf)

Primary court records reveal an institutional transition that aggregate trade cannot represent. The 1960 US West Coast agreement accepted labour-saving changes in exchange for a $29 million mechanization fund over 1961–1966. Even the fund’s allocation was contested: Volkswagen challenged the incidence of assessments on automobiles. Thus an agreement to share gains did not remove disputes about who should finance adjustment. **MOB-K22.** [MOB-S12, opening paragraphs 2–4](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/390/261)

Later litigation recounts larger-than-anticipated losses of dock work and movement of stuffing and unstuffing to off-dock freight stations or cargo owners’ premises. Longshore workers and Teamsters contested jurisdiction. This discriminates between disappearance of a task and relocation of it across employers and worker groups. New equipment jobs existed, but the records do not establish that each displaced worker acquired one, or that working conditions and bargaining power were equivalent. **MOB-K23.** [MOB-S13, part I](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/822/1203/373792/)

The case consequently supports a conditional efficiency mechanism while rejecting a simple distributional inference. The shipper’s complete delivery may improve even when one port loses activity, a worker loses a protected task or an unrepresented community bears a burden. These sources do not measure a complete balance of land, environmental or livelihood effects.

## 7. Singapore: an adopter’s account exposes land and planning choices

Singapore is kept separate because its evidence is different. In his 2000 Pasir Panjang opening speech, Goh Chok Tong dates the first container berths at Tanjong Pagar to 1972 and describes subsequent conversion and expansion. He explicitly presents the new terminal as a long planning process involving limited waterfront space, competing land uses, workforce upgrading and information services. This is primary evidence of an official rationale and deployment chronology. It is not an independent experiment identifying why Singapore succeeded. **MOB-K24.** [MOB-S14, paragraphs 3–6](https://www.nas.gov.sg/archivesonline/data/pdfdoc/2000033003/gct20000330l.pdf)

The meaningful adoption choice was therefore wider than purchasing cranes. It concerned the use of urban space and the service organization around the terminal. A comparison restricted to ship turnaround would exclude much of what authorities had to decide. Equally, the authorities’ selection of a site does not establish that residents or other waterfront users had an effective ability to refuse its transformation.

This source’s silence must remain visible. It celebrates successful upgrading but does not provide worker cohorts, access to training, displacement compensation or a complete environmental account. Those are unknowns, not zero costs. It also cannot demonstrate that the chosen site and timing were uniquely optimal: alternative locations, staged investment and continued conventional handling would require contemporary appraisal records. No forecast in the speech is used as evidence of a realized later outcome.

## What these comparisons establish—and what they do not

Four comparisons are specified in `comparisons.json`. Roman freight and containers identify an analogous measurement problem at interfaces, without claiming identical institutions. Inka, Indian and Ghanaian cases show why local economic responses do not establish political consent. Ghana and early electric vehicles distinguish persistent consequences or niches from continued dominance. India, US ports and Singapore expose different levels of evidence about occupational transitions.

Across these cases, the strongest defensible inference is procedural: evaluate a configuration doing a specified task for named actors, and follow the complete chain. Trace the distinction between an available route, actual use, profitable operation, sustained maintenance and the distribution of gains. An investment can create an opportunity without solving the conditions for everyone to take it. A declining technology can leave useful practices or geography without remaining the best current means of transport.

Several appealing generalizations fail this evidence standard. Superior speed does not by itself explain deployment; the decisive buyer may be a state pursuing control. Low operating costs do not establish favourable worker transitions. Infrastructure persistence does not establish an optimal original choice. A universal “network effect” is too vague: compatibility across firms, concentrated settlement and maintenance finance are different mechanisms with different counterfactuals.

The graph consequently contains scoped contributions and hypotheses, not a technological family tree or a set of universal prerequisites. It makes no `depends-on` claims. No transitive causal inference should be drawn across its episodes, and multiple incoming links do not establish jointly necessary inputs. The test ledger is strongest where sources supply actual contrasts—unbuilt railway plans, task-specific electric survival or container pre-trend tests—and weakest where we possess only a retrospective success narrative.

The principal omissions are aviation, bicycles, urban public transport, East Asian inland systems, maritime labour outside North America and direct accounts of land refusal. Those gaps matter to any proposed general history of mobility. This contribution supplies neither a complete social accounting nor forecasts of future winners; it supplies bounded mechanisms, negative comparisons and inspectable limits for the wider programme.
