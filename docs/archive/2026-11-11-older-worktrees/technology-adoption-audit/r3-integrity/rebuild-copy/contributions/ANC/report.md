# Ancient engineering: operating systems, upkeep, and bounded reversals

This investigation compares six bounded cases, from Bronze Age Gujarat to Roman Gaul and early imperial China. Its strongest finding is that a technical principle, an operating installation, and a durable service are different achievements. The evidence frequently reveals the labour, access, water supply, and repair arrangements that connected them. It rarely supplies prices, comprehensive installation counts, or the experience of ordinary users. Consequently, the cases support mechanisms and challenges to universal propositions; they do not rank civilizations or estimate the proportion of ancient production that was mechanised.

The comparison is deliberately asymmetrical. Hero supplies a transmitted technical text; Barbegal supplies material traces of operation; Frontinus supplies an administrator's account; the Arabian galleries combine excavation with disputed reconstruction; Dujiangyan combines inscriptions interpreted by a historian with a modern hydraulic model; Dholavira combines excavated structures, an institutional archaeological synthesis, and geophysical hypotheses. Calling all these simply “evidence of invention” would erase the distinctions on which the explanation depends. Source access and claim-specific locators are recorded in the accompanying ledgers. Statements about unknown payers, workers, or diffusion are limits of this investigation, not assertions that nobody can know them.

## ANC-C01 — Hero's rotating steam sphere: what exactly was demonstrated?

The object described in *Pneumatica* II.11, numbered 50 in the 1851 English edition, consists of a heated water vessel feeding a pivoted hollow sphere through a tube; steam exits bent outlets and produces rotation. The passage describes no attached productive load, output measurement, fuel bill, or manufacturing programme. This establishes a written mechanism, not its prevalence, its inventor, or an ancient commercial steam industry (ANC-K01). The edition's heading “The Steam-Engine” also belongs to a nineteenth-century presentation whose preface explicitly seeks antecedents of contemporary machinery. [Hero, *Pneumatics*, §50, p. 72; editor's preface, pp. vii–viii (ANC-S01)](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/77400/77400-h/77400-h.htm).

Courtney Roby's introduction challenges readings that treat the sphere as a failed approximation of later industrial machinery. She discusses a philosophical interpretation, associated with Paul Keyser, concerning motion, and stresses the corpus's complex transmission and uncertain biography. That is a serious rival to the familiar “toy that should have become a factory engine,” not a definitive reconstruction of every ancient user's intention (ANC-K02). Only the publisher's ten-page introductory excerpt was inspected; Keyser's original article was not. [Roby, *The Mechanical Tradition of Hero of Alexandria*, pp. 1–4 (ANC-S02)](https://assets.cambridge.org/97810090/14052/excerpt/9781009014052_excerpt.pdf).

The relevant contemporary choice set must follow the task. For explaining motion, teaching construction, attracting attention, or performing a display, visible rotation may already accomplish the objective. For grinding flour, the comparison is delivered grinding service against available muscle or water power. The latter comparison would require evidence about sustained power under load, durable couplings, manufacture, fuel supply, operators, repairs, and buyers. This is an analytical list of missing comparisons, not a claim that every item was technically impossible in antiquity (ANC-K03).

Two rival explanations therefore remain open: the device served an intellectual or performative purpose, or it belonged to a practical development path whose deployment evidence has not survived. An ancient workshop installation with a credible working connection to a productive load would weaken the first explanation. A historically constrained reconstruction could test capability and resource consumption, but would not itself establish ancient adoption. Conversely, absence of a load in one text cannot prove that nobody ever tried one. The statement that “Rome nearly industrialised but slavery stopped steam” moves across several unsupported steps: from description to commercial feasibility, from feasibility to demand, and from non-observation to a specific social cause.

## ANC-C02 — Barbegal: substantial capacity with an uncertain market

At Barbegal near Arles, the second-century CE complex contained two trains of eight waterwheels. Carbonate casts preserve information lost with the wooden machinery, including traces interpreted as successive maintenance interventions. This is evidence of a working production installation, far stronger for deployment than Hero's isolated description (ANC-K04). Stable-isotope sequences also indicate recurrent interruptions, particularly in late summer and autumn. The 2018 investigators proposed ship's biscuit production because its storage and seasonal demand could fit that schedule. The product destination remains an inference: seasonal pauses do not uniquely identify hardtack (ANC-K05). [Sürmelihindi et al., “The second century CE Roman watermills of Barbegal,” Results, especially Fig. 4, and Discussion (ANC-S03)](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aar3620).

The 2024 study narrows the terminal history to three flumes from the western train. Their carbonate records indicate different operating histories, replacement or adjustment of equipment, and cessation in stages while the aqueduct still carried water. Some material was subsequently reused. Its conclusion therefore modifies a picture of one instantaneous, site-wide collapse; it does not identify a single political or commercial cause (ANC-K06). [Passchier et al., “Operation and decline of the Barbegal mill complex,” §§5–6, pp. 604–607 (ANC-S04)](https://doi.org/10.1002/gea.22016).

What was being economised was repeated grinding work. What had to be assembled was a water supply, a suitable fall, machinery, building fabric, maintenance, grain deliveries, and an outlet for flour. The aqueduct and mill were complementary capital, but water alone did not keep milling going. A fall in customers, disrupted grain supply, a changed product, or inability to organise repairs could each break the service while leaving substantial hydraulic infrastructure intact. These are rival pathways, not discoveries about the final proprietor's decision. Distinguishing them would require independently dated consumption, transport, repair, and ownership evidence.

Large size also does not establish empire-wide prevalence. Counting wheel positions answers a site question; counting excavated mills answers a research-history question unless the surveyed population and detection probability are known. The record preserves a selected installation and selected carbonate fragments. Neither an output estimate nor a claim that Barbegal fed all of Arles is adopted here.

Barbegal is nevertheless a decisive objection to an absolute claim that Roman slavery made productive mechanisation impossible. Frontinus's enslaved maintenance workforce also shows that coerced labour could support sophisticated hydraulic services. Neither observation establishes the labour status of Barbegal's millers, or proves that relative labour costs never discouraged machinery elsewhere (ANC-K07; ANC-S03, ANC-S05). The meaningful economic proposition is conditional: for a specified task and purchaser, did buying, feeding, supervising, and replacing labour change the return on a particular machine? This investigation has no wage, slave-price, or capital-cost series with which to identify that effect.

## ANC-C03 — Rome's aqueduct service: construction, allocation, and interruption

Frontinus, writing around the end of the first century CE, describes approximately 240 publicly owned and 460 imperial enslaved workers, divided among functions including inspection and repair. He identifies treasury and imperial funding, with water-right revenues contributing to public expenses. He also prescribes advance preparation, seasonal timing, and taking only one aqueduct out of service for channel repairs at a time (ANC-K08–K09). These details make upkeep visible as an organised activity. They remain an administrator's account of arrangements and proposed discipline; the text does not verify daily compliance or an audited budget. [Frontinus, *De aquaeductu*, §§116–124 (ANC-S05)](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Frontinus/De_Aquis/Bennett/2%2A.html).

Cynthia Bannon's analysis complicates an account in which “the Roman state” uniformly supplied water. Civic aqueduct rights differed from rights in natural sources; local management mattered; private grants could depend on payment or status. A functioning conduit did not imply equal access or a single imperial water administration (ANC-K10). [Bannon, “Fresh Water in Roman Law,” §III, “Civic Aqueducts,” and §V, “Conclusion” (ANC-S06)](https://doi.org/10.1017/S007543581700079X).

The explanatory distinction is between a stock and a flow of service. Building an aqueduct creates an asset; delivering water tomorrow also requires accessible channels, working distribution, people, materials, and authority to intervene. Frontinus's arrangements suggest mechanisms for keeping those complements together. They do not demonstrate that centralisation was always best. Concentrated authority might mobilise resources, but it could also privilege favoured recipients or redirect workers. More local control might improve knowledge and accountability while complicating coordination across an entire route. Both are testable propositions, rather than institutional virtues inferred from survival.

A later interruption sharpens the contemporary alternatives. Procopius reports that the Gothic siege of 537 cut Rome's aqueducts, stopping the mills; fodder scarcity made animal substitution difficult. Belisarius arranged boat-mounted watermills on the Tiber, while wells and river water remained available and bathing ceased (ANC-K11). The narrative credits a commander and may exaggerate completeness, but the reported constraint is unusually specific: an energy alternative depended on feed that siege conditions denied. [Procopius, *Wars* V.19.8–28, also called *Gothic War* I.19 (ANC-S07)](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Procopius/Wars/5C%2A.html).

This is a bounded example of disruption and substitution, not evidence that every aqueduct permanently failed in 537 or that hydraulic knowledge disappeared. It also prevents treating all water uses as one service: drinking, bathing, and grinding could experience different outcomes. Stronger causal testing would align repair inscriptions and channel deposits with changes in particular districts, rather than infer water quality, public health, or universal deprivation from a broken monumental conduit.

## ANC-C04 — Arabian falaj and Iranian galleries: local water, uncertain origins

This case centres on southeast Arabian galleries of the first millennium BCE, with northern Iran in Polybius as a textual contrast. Rémy Boucharlat distinguishes a broad family of shafts-and-gallery groundwater works from a single deep-aquifer type. Excavated Arabian examples linked to Iron Age settlements challenge an uncomplicated Iranian invention followed by radial diffusion. His polycentric interpretation is plausible, but sparse and uneven dating cannot prove independent invention at every site (ANC-K12). [Boucharlat, “Qanāt and Falaj,” §§17.1–17.2 and 17.4, pp. 280–288, 293–296 (ANC-S08)](https://www.academia.edu/124000172/Boucharlat_R_2017_Ancient_qan%C4%81t_and_falaj_polycentric_and_multi_period_innovations_Iran_and_the_United_Arab_Emirates_as_case_studies_in_A_N_Angelakis_E_Chiotis_S_Eslamian_H_Weingartner_eds_Underground_Aqueducts_Handbook_CRC_Press_Boca_Raton_FL_279_301).

At al-Madam, deepening and subsequent silting are interpreted as responses to declining water availability followed by abandonment. That makes adjustment and reversal part of the case, although environmental depletion is not independently isolated from maintenance or settlement change here (ANC-K13; ANC-S08, §17.2.2). A working gallery could expand cultivation while conditions allowed flow, yet later require investment merely to preserve the earlier service. Observing a successful adaptation at one date therefore does not demonstrate sustainability over an indefinite period.

The physical comparison is with obtaining water by other local means, including wells or surface channels, not with a universal ladder from primitive to advanced irrigation. A gravity gallery requires water at an elevation from which a maintained channel can reach the outlet. Once built under favourable conditions, it can avoid repeated lifting at that outlet; it does not remove excavation, cleaning, collapse risk, or the dependence on groundwater. This mechanism follows from the identified geometry, but historical operating costs and extraction volumes remain unmeasured (ANC-K15).

Polybius describes underground channels in the context of Antiochus III's campaign. He reports a local story that Persian rulers granted five generations of cultivation rights to those bringing water to previously unirrigated land, connecting expensive construction to an extended benefit (ANC-K14). The inspected Paton translation does **not** explicitly say “tax exemption,” a stronger description often attached to the passage. Nor does this report establish a complete contract, universal royal policy, or deep-aquifer engineering. [Polybius, *Histories* X.28.1–7 (ANC-S09)](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Polybius/10%2A.html).

The institutional hypothesis is that durable claims to benefits could encourage costly construction. A rival is that political command, inherited local arrangements, or labour coercion did the organising, with the royal story retrospectively legitimising access. Neither is identified by a report of cultivation rights alone. Ancient allocation contracts, disputes, and excavated construction sequences could discriminate. Secure early Iranian installations would revise the chronology; distinctive shared construction practices and intervening sites would strengthen diffusion. Similar forms alone would not.

## ANC-C05 — Dujiangyan: maintenance as work, with a discontinuous evidentiary record

Dujiangyan's diversion system on the Min River provides a substantial comparison with Roman upkeep. The bounded ancient question is what can be established about organised maintenance by the late Han period, not whether every modern structure dates to its conventional third-century BCE foundation. Peng Bang-ben discusses inscriptions dated 168 and 199 CE identifying water officials and repair activity. He interprets the latter episode as evidence of customary winter work and relates the workers to labour obligations (ANC-K16). This is inspected through a substantial Chinese reproduction of the scholarly article, not independently checked rubbings. [Peng, “Dujiangyan's Annual Repair System in Ancient Times,” §§1 and 4 (ANC-S10)](https://www.sohu.com/a/255708893_523159).

Peng also explicitly acknowledges that the inference of a maintenance system from the project's beginning lacks decisive early evidence. The date of a much-cited six-character rule is disputed, and later accounts cannot simply be made contemporary with Li Bing. These qualifications matter more than the article's celebratory language of uninterrupted vitality (ANC-K17; ANC-S10, §§1–2). Institutional continuity is a proposition requiring dated links, not a property automatically conferred by a surviving place-name.

A modern numerical study helps specify the physical mechanism while adding its own limits. Zheng and colleagues model how channel geometry, shoals, and discharge conditions distribute water and sediment; their discussion identifies annual dredging as necessary. They exclude the modern upstream dam's influence, and their conclusion acknowledges further model validation needs. These results support examination of sediment management, not a claim that the ancient arrangement was identical, self-maintaining, or immune to floods (ANC-K18). [Zheng et al., “Sustainability of the Dujiangyan Irrigation System,” §§1, 4–6, especially pp. 12–13 (ANC-S11)](https://doi.org/10.3390/su12062431).

The comparison with Rome therefore concerns coordinating recurrent work around physical constraints. Sediment must go somewhere, an intake must still admit water, and a workforce must be available at an appropriate season. Who gains irrigated crops and who supplies labour need not coincide. The Han evidence supports state involvement; it does not quantify the burden on households, prove consent, or show that every contribution was unpaid. Nor does ritual associated with water officials negate technical competence. Measuring a channel and maintaining sacred authority can coexist within an institution.

Two rivals must remain visible. Geometry may have reduced the burden of maintenance compared with feasible alternatives; alternatively, sustained political mobilisation may have permitted a demanding system to persist. Both can operate together. A comparison would need contemporary channel configurations, repair volumes, failures, and competing irrigation works in similar terrain. Modern simulations without those historical inputs cannot separate the effects. Demonstrating a Qin foundation likewise would not demonstrate twenty-three centuries of uninterrupted operation at a constant service level.

## ANC-C06 — Dholavira: storage capacity, changing settlement, and uncertain damage

Dholavira on Khadir Island used reservoirs, drains, rainwater collection, and a dam associated with seasonal streams. The ICOMOS evaluation places major reservoir construction in an expansion phase and reports damaged water structures left unrestored during a later phase of decline. Its chronology also contains occupation gaps; it should not be flattened into fifteen centuries of continuous urban service (ANC-K19, ANC-K21). The evaluation is an institutional synthesis of excavation and nomination evidence, not the excavation archive itself. [ICOMOS, *Dholavira: A Harappan City*, “Description and history,” printed pp. 14–15 (ANC-S13)](https://whc.unesco.org/document/189324).

Agrawal and colleagues' GPR study investigated an unexcavated area near the East Reservoir. They proposed additional reservoirs, bunds, and flood damage, but allowed alternative structural interpretations and explicitly required excavation for confirmation (ANC-K20). Its abstract and conclusion give inconsistent surveyed-area totals, so no exact area is used here. [Agrawal et al., “Archaeological studies at Dholavira using GPR,” pp. 879–880 and 884–886 (ANC-S12)](https://doi.org/10.18520/cs/v114/i04/879-887).

For residents and producers, the relevant service was access to water across variable inflows. Storing episodic runoff and diverting floodwater solved a different problem from a perennial aqueduct or groundwater gallery. A reservoir could bridge a dry interval, but its usable capacity depended on water arriving, retained storage, and access. That is a physical mechanism and a research question about reliability, not a calculated ancient household supply. No per-capita capacity or drought-survival duration is reconstructed in this contribution.

The site also demonstrates why monumental accomplishment cannot identify political arrangements by itself. Large reservoirs require coordinated effort, but they do not specify whether rulers, neighbourhood groups, specialist custodians, or combinations organised the work. The inspected sources do not recover construction accounts, everyday allocation rules, or a distribution of labour burdens. An argument for hydraulic despotism would need evidence of the direction of dependence: did command create the system, did system management create command, or did both arise from a third process?

Decline does not reverse that standard. Earthquake, floods, reduced inflow, changed exchange networks, and reorganisation of settlement are distinguishable possibilities. The institutional evaluation's historical narrative and the GPR authors' possible flood traces are different kinds of evidence and must not be merged into one identified cause. Damaged structures not restored could indicate lost resources, changed priorities, reduced demand, or migration. Stratigraphic synchronisation of damage, repairs, settlement change, and independent environmental records could falsify some explanations. A single impressive reconstruction cannot.

## What the comparison changes

The cases reject several universal formulations without replacing them with another. Described steam rotation is insufficient evidence of an industrial opportunity declined. Productive Roman waterpower contradicts the categorical impossibility of machinery under slavery, while leaving conditional labour-cost effects open. An abandoned installation does not by itself establish loss of the knowledge needed to build it. A long-lived named system can depend on recurrent reconstruction, altered institutions, and changing service boundaries. Barbegal's staggered cessation, Rome's reported emergency substitution, and the Arabian gallery adjustments are especially useful because they interrupt a simple sequence from invention to diffusion to permanent improvement (ANC-K22).

Three different resources dominate the comparisons: usable energy for grinding, appropriately located water for cultivation or consumption, and recurrent capacity to maintain the connection between them and users. The same material, water, may be an energy carrier, a consumed input, or a destructive sediment-bearing flow. That difference explains why the graph distinguishes technology, resource, practice, and institution, and why its edges are scoped to particular cases. It contains no unsupported arrow from Persian qanats to Roman aqueducts, or from Hero to later steam engines.

Finally, private benefit and social benefit must be separated. Avoided grinding effort, protected crops, and convenient water could coexist with compulsory labour, privileged access, and burdens imposed beyond the beneficiary group. The inspected evidence is much better at identifying installations and official arrangements than measuring those distributions. The strongest next investigations would recover ordinary repair decisions, failed alternatives, and unequal access within the same locality. Famous surviving structures are an informative starting sample; they cannot supply the denominator for ancient technological opportunity.
