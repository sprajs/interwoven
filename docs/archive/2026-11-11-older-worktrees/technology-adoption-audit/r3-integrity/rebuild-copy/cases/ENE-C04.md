# ENE-C04 — Lithium-ion cells: feasible chemistry, qualification and manufacturing improvement

**Period:** 1981–1991; manufacturing comparison 1995–2000 versus 2010–2015. **Region:** Japan; International battery manufacturing and research. **Domain:** energy storage; materials; industrial manufacturing.

A first-person development account and later fixed-format cell model distinguish rechargeable chemistry from saleable qualification and from the sources of cost improvement. No universal battery-replacement or safety claim follows.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/ENE/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Several electrode discoveries and configurations; Yoshino’s carbon/lithiated-cathode patent in 1985 is one development, not a sole-inventor story.

**feasibility:** Laboratory rechargeable operation faced heat, volume and abuse-performance constraints; paired material selection and testing mattered.

**deployment:** Commercial manufacture/sales in Japan in 1991 reported by Yoshino.

**diffusion:** Portable-electronics use is described qualitatively; no independently audited adopter count.

**scaling:** Cost and component changes for representative high-energy 18650 cells between two multi-year windows.

**non-adoption/niche:** Rejected PA and metallic-lithium configurations in the described pathway; application-specific needs persist.

**displacement/reversal:** No assertion that Li-ion displaced every rechargeable chemistry; ENE-C05 records continued lead-acid use.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Episode A: carbon/LiCoO2 nonaqueous rechargeable configuration in Yoshino’s development account. Episode B: representative high-energy cylindrical 18650 cells, not every chemistry.

**task:** Store rechargeable energy in small/light packages for portable devices; lower manufacturing cost per energy capacity while meeting application requirements.

**population:** One corporate research pathway; separate reconstructed representative cell datasets assembled from publications/reports.

**interval:** 1981–1991; 1995–2000 and 2010–2015 comparison windows.

**contemporary alternatives:** Nickel-cadmium and nickel-metal-hydride rechargeables; Lead-acid batteries where mass/volume tolerable; Primary lithium batteries; Rejected metallic-lithium/PA rechargeable designs in the studied pathway

**adoption measure:** Commercialization date and qualitative use; modelled cost per Wh of representative cells, not a complete production or user adoption census.

**observation limits:** Retrospective inventor report; sparse early components/process data; choice of high-energy 18650 excludes applications optimized for other characteristics.

## Actors

**chooser:** Corporate research and manufacturing teams, device designers and end purchasers with different constraints.

**payer:** Company research/manufacturing investment and downstream device buyers; public research contributions acknowledged but exact incidence not reconstructed.

**operator maintainer:** Materials researchers, process/quality engineers and factory workers; testing drew on another corporate division’s facilities.

**affected nonchooser:** Workers and communities involved in materials supply and disposal; mining/waste distribution not investigated by these two sources.

**ability to refuse:** Research teams could reject material routes; consumers faced device offerings. Worker and supply-community refusal evidence unknown.

## Labour

**tasks:** Electrode/material development, cell assembly and formation, inspection, manufacturing-process optimization and qualification testing.

**entry barriers:** Materials knowledge, controlled processes, equipment and quality/safety capability; exact skill credentials and employment access not established.

**bargaining ownership:** Corporate patents/assets and proprietary process formulations matter; ownership of gains and worker bargaining not measured.

**transition:** No longitudinal labour or mining-community evidence; dates describe technology work, not individual retraining duration.

**distribution:** Modeled labour/overhead costs aggregated; no supported distribution of compensation, health risk or returns.

**evidence limit:** A small assigned learning-by-doing share is not an estimate of factory labour’s value; battery-cost decline is not a worker welfare estimate.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: Complementary electrode chemistry; Qualification and reliability; Interdivision knowledge transfer; Energy density and manufacturing scale; R&D/process classification

Inventor selection and incomplete process records; cell format, power, safety and cycle-life trade-offs limit aggregation.

## Evidence links

- [ENE-K13](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ene-k13): Yoshino’s account separates a rechargeable laboratory configuration from saleable batteries: polyacetylene’s heat/volume limitations motivated a carbon anode, and comparative impact testing supported continued development before Japanese commercialization in 1991.
- [ENE-K14](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ene-k14): For representative high-energy-density 18650 cells, Ziegler et al. compare 1995–2000 with 2010–2015 and allocate 38% of modeled cost decline to charge-density improvements; reduced material prices and nonmaterial costs also contribute.
- [ENE-K15](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ene-k15): The battery model’s baseline assigns about 54% of cost decline to broadly defined R&D, 30% to scale and 2% to learning-by-doing; these shares depend on classifications that include pilot-line and process work within R&D.
