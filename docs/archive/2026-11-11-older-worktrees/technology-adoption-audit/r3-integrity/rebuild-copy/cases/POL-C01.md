# POL-C01 — Tokugawa firearms: regulated persistence and changing tasks

**Period:** 1603–1853 (selected local observations). **Region:** Japan; Shiiba mountain villages; Matsumoto domain; Edo hinterland. **Domain:** military; agriculture; regulation.

Firearms persisted in documented agricultural/hunting and elite niches; regulation and changing social meanings cannot be collapsed into national abandonment.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/POL/report.md).

## Stages

**deployment:** Already established technology at the episode’s start; no invention claim.

**diffusion:** Local ownership registers and qualitative evidence; no national curve.

**non-adoption/niche:** Continued farming/hunting and elite uses under regulation.

**displacement/reversal:** Reported shift toward security meanings in the Edo hinterland in the 1840s; does not establish wholesale technological replacement.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Locally owned matchlock firearms in Tokugawa villages; elite marksmanship considered as a distinct associated task, not the same user population.

**task:** Protect crops, hunt, display elite martial skill; later reported local-security use.

**population:** Selected village households and domains represented in historians’ records, especially Shiiba/Matsumoto; Edo-hinterland interpretive subepisode.

**interval:** 1603–1853; numerical anchors 1745 and 1836, Edo change from 1840s.

**contemporary alternatives:** Nonfirearm crop protection/hunting methods: not evaluated in inspected sources; Swords, bows and spears continued in other tasks; not equivalent substitutes for every use; Abstention from firearm possession where permitted or necessary: individual constraints unknown

**adoption measure:** 436 recorded firearms/955 Shiiba households in 1745; 586 firearms in 1836 with no same-year household denominator. Qualitative use and licensing evidence elsewhere.

**observation limits:** Registers may miss unauthorized guns; stocks do not measure shooters or skill. No representative sample; original records accessed through scholarship.

## Actors

**chooser:** Domain and shogunal authorities classify permissible uses; village households seek/hold firearms; elite practitioners choose within status institutions.

**payer:** Village owners and domain/elite institutions supply resources; prices and fiscal incidence not established.

**operator maintainer:** Hunters/cultivators and elite users; gunsmith/repair chain not directly measured.

**affected nonchooser:** Other villagers, people subject to armed authority, crops and wildlife; outcomes not systematically counted.

**ability to refuse:** Permission and restrictions vary by class, domain, time and task; legal possession is not proof of free political consent. No individual refusal cohort.

## Labour

**tasks:** Hunting/crop protection and elite marksmanship persist; reported later reclassification toward security.

**entry barriers:** Permission/status and practical skill; precise cost, training and apprenticeship thresholds unknown.

**bargaining ownership:** Household possession and authority over lawful use differ; gains from saved crops and gunsmith income not quantified.

**transition:** Local stocks separated by decades, not linked individuals; 1840s meaning change known only from Howell abstract.

**distribution:** Class/domain differences in access documented qualitatively; no wage, household-income or violence-incidence distribution.

**evidence limit:** Cannot infer occupational job creation, welfare or modern gun-policy effects from persistence.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: task-specific permission; hierarchy and licensing; peacetime demand; changing security concerns

No national gun-stock, firing-rate or military-effectiveness series; inherited local records and overlapping scholarly lineage.

## Evidence links

- [POL-K01](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#pol-k01): Enomoto reports 436 hunting firearms among 955 Shiiba-mountain households in 1745 and 586 firearms in 1836; these are local recorded stocks, not a national ownership rate.
- [POL-K02](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#pol-k02): Legal crop-protection and hunting use, together with recorded commoner ownership, contradicts a general account of Tokugawa Japan eliminating guns.
- [POL-K03](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#pol-k03): Task-specific permission and peacetime elite appropriation helped accommodate continued firearm use within Tokugawa hierarchies, rather than requiring universal rejection of the technology.
- [POL-K04](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#pol-k04): Howell interprets a shift in Edo-hinterland gun meanings from farm implements toward weapons in the 1840s as responding to anxiety about rural disorder.
