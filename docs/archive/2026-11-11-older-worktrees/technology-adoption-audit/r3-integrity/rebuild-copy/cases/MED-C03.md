# MED-C03 — Antiseptic surgery as a changing clinical practice

**Period:** 1867–1890. **Region:** Britain; Glasgow; Edinburgh; London. **Domain:** health; surgery; professional knowledge.

Clinical demonstrations, changing protocols and disputed comparisons complicate a simple discovery-to-acceptance narrative.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/MED/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** No unique-inventor claim; earlier clinical and cleanliness traditions remain relevant.

**feasibility:** Lister's cases and procedures support particular clinical observations, not an invariant protocol.

**deployment:** Use by Lister and other surgeons with varying combinations and adaptations.

**diffusion:** Professional demonstrations, publications and trainees; qualitative, not a surgeon-level diffusion rate.

**displacement/reversal:** Selective adoption and modification undermine a clean antisepsis-to-asepsis sequence.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Evolving carbolic-based wound management, dressings and operative routines rather than a single chemical purchase.

**task:** Reduce wound complications and expand feasible surgery.

**population:** Specified surgeons/wards and published case groups; no representative British surgeon sample.

**interval:** 1867–1890 debate; Lister's earlier treated cases are background to published claims.

**contemporary alternatives:** Cleanliness methods; Selective combinations of antiseptic measures; Existing wound care

**adoption measure:** Published case reports, small before/after series and documented teaching/adaptation; no general adoption denominator.

**observation limits:** Clinical selection, changing case mix and routines, historical reinterpretation and non-independent source reuse.

## Actors

**chooser:** Surgeons and hospital leadership selected methods; patients' influence varied and is not systematically documented.

**payer:** Hospitals, practitioners and patients through varied arrangements; comparative treatment costs not measured.

**operator maintainer:** Surgeons, assistants, dressers and caregivers performed procedures and aftercare.

**affected nonchooser:** Patients exposed to choices and execution quality; nursing/dressing labour often not foregrounded.

**ability to refuse:** One case narrative notes patient reluctance; no broad consent or refusal series. Treatment alternatives constrained by local hospital practice.

## Labour

**tasks:** More attention to dressings, materials, handling and repeatable routines; demonstration and teaching.

**entry barriers:** Hands-on skills and access to teaching/equipment; social/professional barriers not quantified.

**bargaining ownership:** Professional authority shaped accepted evidence and practice; worker bargaining and ownership not measured.

**transition:** Practice changed across decades; no measured displacement of occupations or wage effect.

**distribution:** Patient outcomes vary by procedure/ward; no credible occupational distribution estimates.

**evidence limit:** Cannot convert a skills transition into a net employment or income claim.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: tacit skill; evidence comparability; professional networks; protocol adaptation

Epidemic cycles, case selection and general improvements complicate outcome attribution; status or material access may also shape adoption.

## Evidence links

- [MED-K06](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#med-k06): Lister’s 1867 address presented an evolving wound-treatment practice through cases and claimed ward improvements, rather than a controlled trial of a fixed protocol.
- [MED-K07](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#med-k07): Contemporary objections to Lister’s aggregate outcome comparisons included small samples, case comparability and hospital epidemic cycles, making simple before/after attribution contestable.
- [MED-K08](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#med-k08): Demonstration, detailed instruction and embodied operative skills contributed to communicating Listerian practice; adoption was not reducible to assent to germ theory.
