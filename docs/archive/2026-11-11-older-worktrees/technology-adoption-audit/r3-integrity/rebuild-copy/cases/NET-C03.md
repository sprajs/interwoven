# NET-C03 — Korean metal printing, manuscripts and rental circulation

**Period:** 1377–early nineteenth century; Jikji and later Chosŏn subepisodes kept distinct. **Region:** Korea. **Domain:** printing; manuscripts; reading networks.

The 1377 metal-type Jikji and 1378 woodblock version establish adjacent production methods. Later Chosŏn manuscripts served rental, imported-text access and selective literary circulation. State resources could sometimes support private publishing, so neither invention nor a state/market binary predicts a single trajectory.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/NET/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** No first-ever metal-type claim: BnF describes the surviving second volume of the 1377 Jikji.

**feasibility:** The Jikji artifact attests actual metal-type production at Heungdeok-sa; a 1378 woodblock counterpart is a separate production.

**deployment:** Later examples include elite female/palace novel circulation, rental manuscripts, literary collections and private use of official publishing resources (NET-K10–K12).

**diffusion:** Commercial rental manuscript circulation from the mid-eighteenth century is documented qualitatively, without an all-reader denominator.

**scaling:** Rental businesses and copying can circulate texts without identical mechanical editions; output volume and affordability are not measured.

**non-adoption/niche:** Print and copying serve overlapping but different audiences and purposes; selected/variant manuscripts are not merely failed printing attempts.

**displacement/reversal:** A general replacement of manuscripts by print is not established by the inspected evidence.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Temple metal-type and woodblock Jikji editions (1377/1378), followed separately by Chosŏn manuscript rental/literary production and selected state/private resource transfers.

**task:** Transmit Buddhist texts, vernacular fiction, imported Chinese works or tailored literary collections; these are distinct tasks with different users.

**population:** One surviving edition tradition; later elite/palace women, literati, rental readers and publishing actors represented in Park's source cases, not all Koreans.

**interval:** 1377–1378 artifacts; late seventeenth-century elite vernacular manuscript circulation; mid-eighteenth-century rental expansion and later literary practices.

**contemporary alternatives:** Metal-type printing; Woodblock editions; Hand copying and selective compilation; Renting or borrowing rather than owning books; Acquiring imported printed texts where available

**adoption measure:** Surviving objects and qualitative descriptions of circulation practices. No common print-run, ownership, rental or literacy denominator.

**observation limits:** Later manuscript practices cannot measure fourteenth-century diffusion. Genre/language and class differ; copying survival and official catalogues select what can be observed.

## Actors

**chooser:** Temple patrons, court/official producers, literati, family readers and rental businesses make different text/form decisions.

**payer:** Patrons, public resources, households, publishers and rental customers; specific fees, subsidies and cost shares are not compiled.

**operator maintainer:** Type makers/printers, block cutters, scribes and rental custodians; copying may involve paid or unpaid work, not consistently distinguished in inspected passages.

**affected nonchooser:** Potential readers excluded by cost, language or education and labourers whose work is controlled by patrons; group-level effects unknown.

**ability to refuse:** Reader/publisher alternatives vary by access and social position. The existence of rental or private production does not establish universal choice or free entry.

## Labour

**tasks:** Casting/composing, carving, copying, selecting and changing texts, editorial work and lending. Manuscript publication sustains work distinct from serial reproduction.

**entry barriers:** Literacy, access to texts and official equipment, craft expertise, social networks and capital; no comparable entry-cost data.

**bargaining ownership:** Some actors reuse state expertise/type privately, but who could obtain such access and on what terms is only partly documented.

**transition:** No artisan cohort or earnings record ties the Jikji episode to later rental businesses; the long interval is explicitly not a continuous labour transition.

**distribution:** Park identifies differentiated literary and vernacular settings, including some women readers, without implying equal female access or population-wide gains.

**evidence limit:** No net occupational displacement or wage claim; manuscript persistence is not direct evidence of preference, efficient choice or commercial freedom.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: textual selection and variation; rental distribution; unequal education and access; public/private resource overlap; genre-specific demand

Expense or shortage of printed books can still explain particular copying. State support coexists with restrictions; a few private uses of official resources do not measure net openness. Prestigious surviving editions are not a representative readership sample.
