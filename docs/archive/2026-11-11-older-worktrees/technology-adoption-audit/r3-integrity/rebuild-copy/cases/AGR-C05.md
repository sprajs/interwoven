# AGR-C05 — Fallow investment and practical land security in Akwapim, Ghana

**Period:** Late twentieth-century two-year survey; exact calendar dates not established in inspected description. **Region:** Akwapim; Ghana; West Africa. **Domain:** agriculture; land tenure; soil management; gender and labour.

Fallowing is an investment through waiting. Plot histories and within-household/cultivator contrasts connect practical security, political position and fallow decisions, while leaving causal exclusion restrictions and the best remedy open.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/AGR/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** No invention date is relevant or inferred; fallowing is an existing practice.

**feasibility:** Soil recovery requires leaving land unused for a period, with subsequent access sufficiently secure to capture returns.

**deployment:** The survey observes cultivation and fallow histories in four Akwapim clusters.

**diffusion:** The study compares investment intensity/security, not national spread of a purchased device or a new practice.

**scaling:** More investment may require land/time security rather than simply supplying equipment or knowledge; no scale-up programme is evaluated.

**non-adoption:** Shorter fallow can be a response to expropriation risk or other constraints, not a failure to understand agronomy.

**displacement:** Earlier recultivation is the relevant competing practice; no wholesale historical displacement trajectory is reconstructed.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Fallow–cultivation rotations under locally negotiated land-control arrangements; waiting is the productive investment.

**task:** Restore soil productivity while retaining the ability to return and harvest the investment's payoff.

**population:** Cultivators and plots in Goldstein and Udry's repeated survey of four Akwapim village clusters; not all Ghanaian farmers and not the northern Ghana insurance sample.

**interval:** Two-year survey with retrospective plot histories, late twentieth century; the inspected survey description does not fix calendar endpoints.

**contemporary alternatives:** Cultivate again sooner to maintain a claim; Longer fallow where control is secure; Other soil-improvement inputs, whose equivalent feasibility is not established by this case

**adoption measure:** Fallow duration, plot use, tenure/political position and estimated agricultural returns; no binary new-technology adoption share.

**observation limits:** Observational and instrumental-variable identification relies on restrictions. Plot histories and practical security are imperfect; a legal document and effective control are distinct.

## Actors

**chooser:** Individual cultivators choose timing subject to household, lineage and local authority over land; choices can differ within the same household.

**payer:** Cultivators bear foregone current production and risk of losing access; no purchased-equipment payer is required for the central investment.

**operator maintainer:** Cultivators manage the rotation and later crop work. Waiting may reduce current plot labour while imposing a longer return horizon.

**affected nonchooser:** Household members dependent on output and people with competing claims to land; gendered authority changes who can retain control.

**ability to refuse:** A farmer can shorten fallow but may not be able to secure the land through waiting. Women generally have less local political power in the study; formal legal freedom does not establish equal feasible options.

## Labour

**tasks:** Cultivation is rescheduled over longer or shorter rotations; the central choice is land/time allocation, not an observed new occupation.

**entry barriers:** Secure practical access to land and local political standing; foregone current output can matter alongside cash and agronomic knowledge.

**bargaining ownership:** Control during non-use affects who captures delayed productivity gains; a household-level owner label can hide distinct cultivator rights.

**transition:** Repeated observation and plot histories identify investment patterns, not a worker reskilling or occupational-transition sequence.

**distribution:** Women are less often politically powerful and face shorter effective investment horizons. Estimates do not prove all gender differences arise through one channel.

**evidence limit:** No universal effect of titling, no comparison of every soil technology, and no causal national gender-welfare estimate.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: capturing delayed returns; practical tenure security; political power; gendered access; plot-specific comparison

Political status may carry wealth, labour and other advantages. Within-person/plot-provenance contrasts address some rivals but do not randomly assign power or establish formal title as the only solution.

## Evidence links

- [AGR-K14](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#agr-k14): Goldstein and Udry's within-household and within-cultivator contrasts support an institutional interpretation of the observed gender investment gap: women less often occupy powerful positions and face weaker practical security, rather than the difference directly revealing skill or taste.
