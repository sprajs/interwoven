# IND-C06 — Rings, mules and adaptation in Japan, India and Britain

**Period:** 1878–1933. **Region:** Japan; India; Britain; United States (technology origin). **Domain:** cotton spinning; technology transfer; labour organisation.

Imported spinning equipment was recombined with local cotton preparation, labour and skills; machine rankings changed over time.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/IND/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Ring and mule lineages predate the main comparison; high drafting later changed fibre control.

**feasibility:** Product count, fibre length, preparation and operatives affected workable use.

**deployment:** Supplier orders establish purchases; installation and realised utilisation are incompletely observed.

**diffusion:** The study follows major British exporters’ records across countries, with defined coverage limits.

**scaling:** Japanese mixed-cotton preparation expanded ring capability; supplier improvements widened the range of adopters.

**non-adoption:** Mule purchases continued where skill and flexibility conferred advantages; this need not indicate stagnation.

**displacement:** Ring gains and high drafting eroded mule applications in the 1920s; specialised persistence continued and is not reduced to a single switch date.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Rings and mules in international orders, Japanese cotton-mixing package in the 1890s, and Indian high-draft orders in 1929–1933 are distinct subepisodes.

**task:** Spin desired yarn counts reliably from available cotton at viable cost.

**population:** Customers of major British spinning-machinery exporters; country aggregates of specified orders, not every mill or spindle.

**interval:** 1878–1933

**contemporary alternatives:** Mule spinning with specific operative skills; Ring spinning; Different fibre mixtures and preparation work; Later high-draft systems; Continuing existing machinery

**adoption measure:** Supplier order types and specifications; selected national installed-spindle tables are a different measure. No direct profit or worker-welfare measure.

**observation limits:** Orders may differ from completed installations or usage; cotton specification is missing for some orders; buyers unable to finance any machine are underobserved.

## Actors

**chooser:** Mill management chooses equipment with machinery suppliers’ advice; recruitment and cotton buying are coordinated managerial decisions.

**payer:** Mill owners/investors; precise national financing constraints not reconstructed.

**operator maintainer:** Mule spinners with maintenance skills, ring attendants, cotton-preparation workers and mechanics; gender composition differs across the episodes.

**affected nonchooser:** Workers allocated machines, pace and tasks; families supplying young workers.

**ability to refuse:** Purchasing records establish employer choice, not workers’ consent. Source indicates organised/skill-based mule labour and young female recruitment, but does not supply a comparative freedom-to-exit audit.

## Labour

**tasks:** Mixing and fibre preparation could use more labour while machine tending changed; mule skills included adjustment and maintenance.

**entry barriers:** Tacit mule skills and apprenticeship-like learning versus less experienced ring workforces; access to mill capital and imports remains necessary.

**bargaining ownership:** Management and supplier choices interact with organised skilled labour and workforce composition; Indian explanation remains tentative.

**transition:** Country-level technical change is observed for decades, but individual occupational mobility is not.

**distribution:** Output potential and labour use are studied; no reliable comparative wages, hours or health outcomes for the affected groups established here.

**evidence limit:** Technical progress in machine orders must not be translated into freedom, welfare or an inherent national labour quality.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: cotton preparation; imported capital and technical services; learning and prior experience; skill specificity; workforce organisation; changing engineering frontier

Orders and specifications differ from output; adoption regressions are not exogenous identification; Indian labour-relations explanation is explicitly tentative.

## Evidence links

- [IND-K17](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k17): Japanese mills in the 1890s adapted ring spinning through a package involving cotton imports, mixed fibres, more preparation labour and a changed labour force, rather than merely copying American operating conditions.
- [IND-K18](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k18): Between 1878 and 1914 mule and ring spinning both continued to improve; Saxonhouse and Wright interpret British mule persistence as a choice supported by skills and product flexibility, not simply stagnant technology.
- [IND-K19](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k19): India supplied the largest number of high-draft machinery orders in Saxonhouse and Wright’s 1929–1933 dataset, a bounded counterexample to equating low wages with non-adoption of labour-saving equipment.
