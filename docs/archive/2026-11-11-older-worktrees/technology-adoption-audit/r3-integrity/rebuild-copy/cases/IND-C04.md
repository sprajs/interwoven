# IND-C04 — British spinning, factory organisation and machine-tool complements

**Period:** c. 1760–1850. **Region:** Britain; Lancashire; English textile districts. **Domain:** textiles; machine tools; labour organisation.

Task-specific pay, product demands, power, precision machinery and control over work need separation when explaining mechanisation.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/IND/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Jennies, water frames and later mules/tools are distinct inventions; exact firsts and all development costs not reconstructed.

**feasibility:** Repeatable operation required appropriate craft and engineering capabilities; small devices and complex machinery differed.

**deployment:** Factory machinery and outwork were contemporary alternatives; Ure records managerial advocacy rather than a deployment census.

**diffusion:** Displacement of hand spinning is established qualitatively in scholarship, with different fibre and regional trajectories.

**scaling:** Machine tools and replacement parts supported repeated machine supply; labour recruitment and organisation remained necessary.

**non-adoption:** Extending outwork or importing yarn could address capacity needs without immediate factory concentration.

**displacement:** Hand-spinning income contracted while engineering skill demand expanded; complete deskilling is not inferred.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Hand spinning/outwork versus mechanised spinning and factory organisation, with later machinery production and repair as a linked engineering subepisode.

**task:** Produce yarn of suitable quantity and quality, and reproducibly supply the machinery that does so.

**population:** British spinners across several fibres in archival pay records; advocates and engineering accounts concern partly different cotton-factory and machinery populations.

**interval:** c. 1760–1850; wage reconstruction also reaches earlier years

**contemporary alternatives:** Extended putting-out networks; Imported yarn; Hand or small-machine spinning; Concentrated powered spinning; Craft-built versus machine-tooled components

**adoption measure:** Pay observations, factory/engineering descriptions and qualitative displacement; no common national adoption denominator.

**observation limits:** Cotton observations are scarce in wage data dominated by wool; retrospective machinery histories and pro-factory testimony are not representative worker surveys.

## Actors

**chooser:** Yarn masters, factory owners and machine producers; some household producers make smaller-scale choices.

**payer:** Owners and investors finance equipment/materials; households contribute labour and sometimes tools.

**operator maintainer:** Women and children in hand and factory tasks, skilled spinners, mechanics and instrument/tool makers; composition varies with process.

**affected nonchooser:** Dependent hand-spinning households and children recruited under institutional arrangements; these are not equivalent to free investor purchasers.

**ability to refuse:** Poor-law, prison and household dependence qualify voluntariness; Ure’s aspiration for tractable labour does not establish actual refusal rights for all factories.

## Labour

**tasks:** Hand yarn work displaced or reorganised; machine tending, fitting, precision metalwork and repair expanded. Paid and household work coexist.

**entry barriers:** Engineering and skilled operative training, machinery assets and gendered access to occupations; no complete transition matrix.

**bargaining ownership:** Yarn masters often controlled fibre and wages; factory owners controlled capital; mechanics could retain skills and organisation despite deskilling aspirations.

**transition:** A multi-decade change with different fibres and tasks; no evidence that the same displaced spinners became mechanics.

**distribution:** Evidence documents low spinners’ pay and managerial substitution motives; aggregate output gains cannot establish compensation of displaced women.

**evidence limit:** The sources do not estimate lifetime losses, child welfare or a causal skill premium; pay comparisons require careful time and task units.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: relative wages contested; female and child labour; quality and productivity; labour control; precision engineering; maintenance supply; capital organisation

Most hand-spinning pay observations concern wool; cotton time-use data are scarce. An anti-high-wage finding does not identify one alternative motive.

## Evidence links

- [IND-K09](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k09): Allen’s explanation assigns British mechanisation to labour being expensive relative to capital and energy, and subsequent wider diffusion to improvements that reduced other input requirements.
- [IND-K10](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k10): Humphries and Schneider’s reconstructed British hand-spinning pay does not show a general wage surge immediately before the spinning inventions; it challenges including these workers in an undifferentiated high-wage economy.
- [IND-K11](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k11): In 1835 Ure publicly advocated factory mechanisation partly as a means to substitute cheaper labour and reduce dependence on independent skilled workers.
- [IND-K12](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k12): Kelly and Ó Gráda trace a pathway from instrument-making skill and precision measurement to complex textile machinery and the machine tools needed for repeated production and replacement parts.
- [IND-K15](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k15): Inikori argues that Atlantic demand linked to slavery-based commodity production helped form the markets and regional expansion within which British cotton mechanisation developed.
