# IND-C08 — US manufacturing electrification and factory redesign

**Period:** c. 1880–1930. **Region:** United States. **Domain:** electricity; factory organisation; power transmission.

Line-shaft replacement, group drive and unit drive were distinct arrangements; electricity service and machine redesign shaped their value.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/IND/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Electric motors and generation preceded broad industrial reorganisation; exact first-use claims are not needed here.

**feasibility:** Reliable motor operation did not establish machine compatibility or economic superiority in every factory.

**deployment:** Early motors often retained existing shafts; group and unit configurations followed and coexisted.

**diffusion:** Motor horsepower shares are documented; unit-drive prevalence is inferred from technical sources, not comprehensively counted.

**scaling:** Utility networks, equipment suppliers, installation advice and new factory design helped expansion.

**non-adoption:** Existing onsite generation and group operations could make immediate conversion unattractive.

**displacement:** Mechanical distribution was reduced over time; electric motors could still rely on steam-generated electricity.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Electric line-shaft retrofits, group drive and unit drive in US factories, with onsite versus utility generation separately identified.

**task:** Deliver controllable mechanical work and organise production economically.

**population:** US manufacturing mechanical-drive capacity and selected factories described in technical literature; no comprehensive unit-drive census.

**interval:** c. 1880–1930; horsepower comparison 1909–1919

**contemporary alternatives:** Steam/water prime movers with shafts; Electric line-shaft drive; Electric group drive; Electric unit drive; Onsite versus purchased generation

**adoption measure:** Electric motor horsepower share, approximately 25% in 1909 and over 53% in 1919; unit-drive prevalence inferred qualitatively from design and technical literature.

**observation limits:** Capacity is not actual utilisation, output or establishment count; engineering sources select projects and suppliers’ promotional successes.

## Actors

**chooser:** Factory owners/managers, production engineers and utility/supplier advisers.

**payer:** Manufacturers finance equipment; utilities finance networks and sometimes lend motors or installation assistance.

**operator maintainer:** Machine operatives, electricians, mechanics, utility operators and production planners.

**affected nonchooser:** Workers whose pace and discretion change; communities affected by power generation; neither outcome well measured here.

**ability to refuse:** Owners can retain installed equipment; workers’ consent to changed work organisation is not established. Customer choice among power arrangements differs from operator autonomy.

## Labour

**tasks:** Belt adjustment and mechanical distribution maintenance reduced; motor installation, electrical maintenance and production reorganisation expanded.

**entry barriers:** Electrical/engineering competence and factory investment; credential and demographic access not reconstructed.

**bargaining ownership:** Owners and utilities control capital and power service; evidence does not quantify workers’ share of productivity gains.

**transition:** Multiple configurations coexist through decades; no linked worker-career dataset.

**distribution:** Potential efficiency and control gains are documented; no inference of improved wages, pace, safety or autonomy for every worker.

**evidence limit:** Engineering and horsepower data cannot identify livelihoods or allocate all productivity gains to electrification.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: distributed control; machine compatibility; factory layout; utility economies and services; sunk capital; maintenance and downtime

Engineering-focused sources underrepresent workers; aggregate productivity gains are not all independently assigned to electrification.

## Evidence links

- [IND-K23](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k23): American factory electrification included distinct line-shaft, group-drive and unit-drive arrangements; replacing a prime mover with a motor did not automatically reorganise factory power distribution.
- [IND-K24](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k24): Devine links effective unit-drive use to compatible machinery, production redesign, utility expansion and application services, with sunk onsite equipment differentiating retrofits from new plants.
- [IND-K25](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k25): Devine reports that electric motors represented roughly one-quarter of US manufacturing mechanical-drive horsepower in 1909 and over half in 1919; he separately infers unit-drive diffusion because comprehensive configuration statistics were unavailable.
