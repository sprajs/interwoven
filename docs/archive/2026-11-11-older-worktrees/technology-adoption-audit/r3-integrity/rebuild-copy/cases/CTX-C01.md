# CTX-C01 — Guanzhong household textiles under collective agriculture and restricted cloth access

**Period:** 1949–1980; strongest mechanism evidence from mid-1950s procurement/collectivisation through collective-era recollections. **Region:** China; Shaanxi; Guanzhong; Gedatou (Zhouzhi county); Danbei and Zhangli (Xingping county). **Domain:** household textiles; state procurement; gender and unpaid labour.

Hand production continued amid valued cloth services, scarce cash and rationed substitutes, while women combined household clothing duties with collective work. Local alternatives and agency varied; persistence is not a measure of voluntary preference or occupational opportunity.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/CTX/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** No claim of invention; hand textile processes and factory substitutes already existed.

**feasibility:** Household production supplied clothing; technical feasibility did not imply adequate inputs, time, consent or remuneration.

**deployment:** Retrospective interviews and archival interpretation document continued work in three named villages.

**diffusion:** No adoption curve or national denominator. Author’s extension to other cotton-growing areas is a hypothesis.

**scaling:** National factory output and local household use are different measures; this case estimates neither a scaling rate nor a national household-output share.

**non-adoption:** Factory cloth entitlement could fail to become actual purchase because cash and other needs constrained redemption; qualities and social uses also differed.

**displacement:** Later photo captions report the end of hand spinning and continued niche weaving, but no household exit panel or causal timing is established here.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Household spinning, weaving and clothing work using retained or informally obtained cotton, alongside collective field labour and coupon-and-cash access to factory cloth. Do not assume the pre-1949 mixed-yarn configuration for every later household.

**task:** Clothe family members and fulfil locally valued textile exchanges; state authorities also sought cotton procurement and agricultural labour, creating conflicting purposes.

**population:** Primary local evidence: >80 villagers, mostly men and women born 1920–1945, in Gedatou (Zhouzhi), Danbei and Zhangli (Xingping), interviewed about 200 times over 14 weeks in five visits, 2005–2011. Broader policy context in CTX-S03 has a different, nonmatched population.

**interval:** 1949–1980, separating early PRC years from procurement in 1954 and collectivisation in 1955–1956. Main work/consumption accounts concern the subsequent collective period; later photographs are a limited coda.

**contemporary alternatives:** Purchase rationed factory cloth when coupons, cash and supplies permit; entitlement alone is not access.; Use retained cotton, glean or acquire informally, and produce cloth at home.; Sell coupons or cloth and exchange output for cotton or other necessities; illegal or risky routes were not equally available.; Local gifts and collective cotton distributions; variable and not a guaranteed substitute.; Earlier textile cooperatives or attempted collective domestic services existed in broader histories; local reliability and coverage are not established here.; Reduce clothing consumption or divert time from collective work/rest; these are burdensome adjustments, not necessarily acceptable choices.

**adoption measure:** Qualitative continued-use and purchase/resale accounts in a bounded retrospective sample. No consistent person/household-year denominator, national prevalence or common pre/post output share.

**observation limits:** Oral history recorded decades later, selected villages and surviving interviewees, no acquired original recordings or archived files. Actor motives are reported interpretations. Macro output totals in pp.378–379 require denominator reconciliation and are excluded.

## Actors

**chooser:** State planners set procurement and cloth allocation; collective/team leaders allocate work and resources; households decide purchases and exchanges unevenly; women operate and negotiate within these constraints. No unitary household or state preference assumed.

**payer:** Households supply cash or coupons and cotton; women supply much unremunerated textile time. Collective work points and retained household resources differ from a wage for home production.

**operator maintainer:** Women performing hand spinning, weaving, sewing and related clothing work; detailed loom repair organisation is not observed and must not be inferred as a paid repair role.

**affected nonchooser:** Women bearing competing work demands, children and dependants needing clothing, and relatives whose access to cloth depends on household allocation.

**ability to refuse:** Neither compulsory hand weaving nor unrestricted choice is established. Agricultural obligations, scarce cash, household expectations and sanctions/risk around informal trade narrowed options. Some women negotiated, sold cloth, concealed inputs or held local office; extent of individual refusal rights is unmeasured.

## Labour

**tasks:** Collective field duties and cotton cultivation overlapped with unpaid home textile and care work; occasional sales provided a distinct cash route for some.

**entry barriers:** Textile skills, access to cotton/tools, time after collective duties, market contacts, risk of prohibited trade and household position. No credential or entry-cost series available.

**bargaining ownership:** State/collectives controlled important inputs, work allocation and legal channels; households exercised additional authority. Women’s operational skill and trading activity do not establish control of all proceeds or capacity to leave.

**transition:** Retrospective work histories span political change, but no tracked cohort transition from home textiles to a paid industrial or repair occupation. Later spinning cessation is not an identified transition rate.

**distribution:** Accounts locate unpaid work/rest burdens among women and document some public roles and commercial exceptions. They do not yield a net group welfare effect, an average individual income or a national gender comparison.

**evidence limit:** No audited time-use panel, wages for unpaid household work, original interview files, independent national sampling or measured value capture. This case cannot establish an attractive future occupation from task persistence.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: ration entitlement versus effective purchase; household cash constraints; gendered allocation of unpaid work; collective labour obligations; different cloth services; cotton retention and informal exchange

Some households used factory cloth; some women sold handwoven cloth or gained public standing. State policies varied and local leaders sometimes mediated informal cotton access. No single mechanism or uniform welfare outcome follows.

## Evidence links

- [CTX-K01](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ctx-k01): Eyferth’s post-1949 investigation records continued household textile work in three Guanzhong villages: Gedatou in Zhouzhi, and Danbei and Zhangli in Xingping. Its fieldwork comprised about 200 interviews with more than eighty villagers, mostly men and women born in 1920–1945, over fourteen weeks during five visits in 2005–2011.
- [CTX-K02](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ctx-k02): In Eyferth’s Zhouzhi account, cloth-ration entitlement did not ensure purchased factory cloth: informants attributed coupon resale and continued home production to scarce disposable cash, with village cadres and newly married couples described as exceptions to the prevailing practice.
- [CTX-K03](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ctx-k03): The Guanzhong accounts describe women meeting household clothing duties through unpaid textile work alongside collective agricultural obligations, often at the expense of rest. This supports a pathway in which continued household production was sustained by shifting time costs onto its women operators.
- [CTX-K04](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ctx-k04): Reported durability and the social uses of textiles complicate a comparison based on metres of cloth alone. These observations leave room for useful task advantages of homespun, but do not establish that ritual textiles had to be handmade or that operators freely preferred the work.
- [CTX-K05](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ctx-k05): Retained and diverted cotton, local distributions and some black-market cloth production offered routes around formal procurement constraints in the Guanzhong account. Reports of commercial weaving coexist with reports that its risk and required absence from collective work made it inaccessible to many interviewees.
- [CTX-K06](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ctx-k06): The evidence does not support a uniform loss-of-agency account: Eyferth records appreciation of changed marital status and some women’s public prominence, while Hershatter describes broadened rural social worlds and labour-model visibility alongside continued unequal burdens.
- [CTX-K07](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ctx-k07): Compared with IND-C07’s pre-1949 hybrid-yarn configuration, this post-1949 episode adds observed access and unpaid-labour pathways to persistence. Continued task performance alone cannot establish technical preference, voluntary participation or a desirable paid occupation.
- [CTX-K08](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ctx-k08): Hershatter’s account distinguishes early PRC support for some women’s textile cooperatives from the narrowing of household selling opportunities and the Great Leap’s attempted collective provision of domestic services. Policy towards women’s work was not one unchanging programme across 1949–1980.
