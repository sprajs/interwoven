# SKL-C04 — Tanzanian automotive workshops: learning, tool ownership and repair alternatives

**Period:** 1960s–1980s histories, investigated in 2011–2012. **Region:** Tanzania: Dar es Salaam, Morogoro, Tabora and other documented garages. **Domain:** automotive repair; apprenticeship; reuse.

Workshop learning and salvage could keep vehicles operating without purchasing new ones, while labour obligations, tool accumulation and disagreements over reliability shaped who could become an independent mechanic.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/SKL/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Specific repairs recombined existing parts and processes; no claim of a national original automobile invention.

**feasibility:** Observed repairs and interview histories identify working practices; no standardised lifetime or road-safety experiment.

**deployment:** Named garages and repairs documented through interviews, photographs and author participation.

**diffusion:** Pedagogic lineages and regional differences described qualitatively; no workshop census or national rate.

**scaling:** Tool accumulation and more customers sometimes enabled a larger workshop, but one career does not establish typical upward mobility.

**non-adoption/niche:** Mechanics sometimes preferred keeping modules original and specialising by model rather than maximising modification.

**displacement/reversal:** Repair extended older vehicles' use; no aggregate displacement of new vehicle sales or formal training is inferred.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Informal gereji bubu performing routine maintenance, part modification and modular reuse on heterogeneous motor vehicles.

**task:** Return existing vehicles to service and train mechanics under changing parts availability and cash constraints.

**population:** Grace's named mechanics and workshops; historical recollections of 1960s–1980s careers plus directly observed training/repairs in 2011–2012.

**interval:** Historical skill/parts trajectories and later field observation are separately identified; direct observation is not projected unchanged into the past.

**contemporary alternatives:** New or imported replacement parts; Whole replacement vehicle when affordable; Hand refinishing versus machine-tool-assisted work; Keep stock modules versus extensive modification; Leave vehicle out of service

**adoption measure:** Specific repair histories, table 2.1's interview-linked methods, workshop observations and one narrated shop expansion; no representative rates.

**observation limits:** Retrospective recall, one researcher's short apprenticeship, selected reputationally visible mechanics and no independently measured repair durability.

## Actors

**chooser:** Vehicle owners choose repairs subject to cost/parts; head mechanics choose methods and allocate apprentice access.

**payer:** Customers pay for jobs; apprentices contribute labour while receiving instruction and reported small shares of profits rather than a regular salary; occasional earnings from assisting repairs are also described.

**operator maintainer:** Head mechanics, specialists and apprentices; apprentices also cooked, cleaned and did household errands.

**affected nonchooser:** Passengers, other road users and youths allocated risky or menial work; outcomes not systematically recorded.

**ability to refuse:** Independent work became possible with skills/tools; cash and job scarcity constrained entry choices. No universal freedom or coercion finding for apprentices is warranted.

## Labour

**tasks:** Routine servicing, heavy lifting, grinding, diagnosis, fabrication, tool salvage, domestic support and teaching. Labour-for-learning exchange is visible, not a paid/unpaid binary.

**entry barriers:** Access to a supervising mechanic, years of participation, personal tools, bodily work and reputation; some accounts cite unaffordable formal schooling.

**bargaining ownership:** Apprentices' personal tool stocks increased independent-job options; senior mechanics controlled customer relationships and specialist-learning opportunities.

**transition:** Named life histories and observed hierarchy suggest long progression; no typical completion rate, earnings trajectory or cohort denominator.

**distribution:** Workshops offered entry but exposed youths to physical risk and menial work; gendered masculine authority is documented, women's full role not measured.

**evidence limit:** Practical access is not proof of secure income, safe repair, universal opportunity or positive aggregate welfare.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: supervised participation; tool ownership; salvage parts; regional machine access; reputation and reliability

Coastal mechanics contested some upcountry methods; makers themselves associated some improvisation with bad economic conditions. Successful survivors and male technical authority dominate the record.
