# ENE-C05 — Chinese electric two-wheelers: lead-acid persistence and shifted externalities

**Period:** 1999–2009, principally observations 2006–2007. **Region:** China; sampled urban markets and regional electricity systems. **Domain:** energy storage; transport manufacturing.

Lead-acid storage supported a growing electric vehicle category while newer batteries remained costly alternatives. City rules, modular component supply, actual substituted modes and upstream lead flows complicate a clean/newer-is-better story.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/ENE/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Lead-acid chemistry predates the episode; the innovation here is a configured vehicle/supply system.

**feasibility:** Standardized batteries, motors, controllers and chargers assembled into useful low-speed vehicles.

**deployment:** Observed use and sales expansion in Chinese cities during the 2000s.

**diffusion:** Industry sales estimates and limited city surveys; no nationally representative household diffusion panel.

**scaling:** Decentralized supplier/assembler networks; routine assembly observed at six plants.

**non-adoption/niche:** Li-ion/NiMH available but limited in domestic stores; lead-acid continued. Some cities restricted e-bikes.

**displacement/reversal:** Prior bicycle/bus use common in surveyed cities; not evidence of universally replacing cars or motorcycles.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Chinese bicycle-style and scooter-style electric two-wheelers, mostly VRLA batteries, with Li-ion/NiMH comparators.

**task:** Affordable urban/regional personal mobility under local road rules; assemble/maintain a saleable vehicle.

**population:** Users, dealers and manufacturers in selected cities/plants; national industry estimates and a separate partial emissions model.

**interval:** 1999–2009, field observations principally 2006–2007; some upstream lead inputs refer to 1999.

**contemporary alternatives:** Bicycles; Buses/public transport; Gasoline motorcycles where permitted; Cars for households able to afford them; Li-ion/NiMH electric two-wheelers, available but more costly

**adoption measure:** Industry annual-sales estimates, city roadside shares, prior-mode surveys; chemical-market shares do not cleanly distinguish exports from domestic use.

**observation limits:** Nonrandom cities/plants, shifting regulations and inconsistent estimates; emissions model uses borrowed factors and scenario service life/load assumptions.

## Actors

**chooser:** Users selecting among affordable/legal modes; city authorities setting access; assemblers selecting components.

**payer:** Purchasers and replacement-battery buyers; external pollution costs partly borne elsewhere and unpriced in purchase comparison.

**operator maintainer:** Riders/households charge vehicles, dealers/repairers replace components, assembly workers build vehicles and battery workers/recyclers handle materials.

**affected nonchooser:** Pedestrians, road users, workers and communities near electricity generation, lead production and recycling.

**ability to refuse:** Motorcycle/e-bike bans remove particular options in different cities; surveyed users retained bicycle/bus alternatives but access varied. Communities exposed to upstream pollution did not choose each vehicle; effective refusal rights unestablished.

## Labour

**tasks:** Prefabricated-component assembly using pneumatic tools, battery manufacture/replacement, charging and recycling; low machining requirement in visited assembly plants.

**entry barriers:** Modular components lowered some assembler technical requirements; capital, supplier access, legal registration and quality control remained. User purchase price and local legal access constrain uptake.

**bargaining ownership:** Many small assemblers and competitive supplier networks documented; wages, ownership profits and recycler bargaining not established.

**transition:** No tracked transitions from bicycle manufacture or displaced transport jobs; plant visits provide task snapshots.

**distribution:** Pollution shifted to supply/recycling locations; actual worker doses, health outcomes and income distribution not measured.

**evidence limit:** Low task-entry requirements are not proof of decent work, and cheap transport is not sufficient evidence of net social benefit.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: Upfront price and performance trade-offs; Standardized components and assembly entry; Policy-shaped transport alternatives; Supply-chain externalities

Battery choice motives not causally observed; advanced-battery production includes exports; environmental inventory omits important pathways and dose-response evidence.

## Evidence links

- [ENE-K16](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ene-k16): In China’s mid-2000s electric-two-wheeler market, lead-acid remained the dominant battery despite commercially available lithium-ion alternatives; a contemporary comparison estimated roughly fourfold upfront Li-ion battery cost with a longer assumed life.
- [ENE-K17](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ene-k17): E-bike adoption in the surveyed Chinese cities combined component-based assembly with a policy-shaped choice set: motorcycle restrictions favored some electric use, while other cities restricted e-bikes; available surveys mainly observed substitution from bicycles and public transport.
- [ENE-K18](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ene-k18): The 2009 partial lifecycle analysis finds that e-bike comparisons depend on regional electricity and the displaced mode; lead emissions arise upstream in battery production/recycling, so zero tailpipe emissions do not demonstrate zero pollution or a quantified health benefit.
