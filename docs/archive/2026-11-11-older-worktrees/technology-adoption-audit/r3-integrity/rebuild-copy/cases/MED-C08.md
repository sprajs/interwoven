# MED-C08 — Antibiotic prescribing and dispensing incentives in a Chinese city

**Period:** October 2011–June 2012. **Region:** One unnamed large city, China. **Domain:** health; prescribing; professional incentives.

Randomized encounter scripts show an interaction between expressed demand and expected dispensing revenue; downstream harms were not measured.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/MED/report.md).

## Stages

**deployment:** Established hospital dispensing/prescribing system studied, not invention of antibiotics.

**diffusion:** No historical diffusion series; standardized encounters measure inappropriate use in a bounded population.

**non-adoption/niche:** Lower prescribing under outside-pharmacy scripts provides a within-study negative comparison.

**displacement/reversal:** Potential prescribing reduction, not demonstrated durable reform or discontinuation of needed treatment.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Hospital outpatient prescribing linked to expected medicine sales, tested with standardized mild-illness presentations.

**task:** Authors evaluate appropriate prescribing; providers and patients may face different immediate objectives.

**population:** Experiment 1: 320 visits to 80 physicians. Experiment 2: 300 visits to 120 physicians; young healthy auditors in one city.

**interval:** October 2011–June 2012.

**contemporary alternatives:** No antibiotic prescription for study presentation; Prescription filled at outside pharmacy; Patient knowledge/demand scripts

**adoption measure:** Antibiotic prescriptions per audited encounter; experiment 1 rates compare assigned scripts, not drug ingestion or national use.

**observation limits:** Auditors knew study goals; limited patient/city scope; no resistance or long-term policy measurement.

## Actors

**chooser:** Physicians prescribed; auditors manipulated demand and expected purchase location; hospital finance shaped context.

**payer:** Patients ordinarily pay or use insurance; these audits record prescribed expenditure, not full system costs.

**operator maintainer:** Physicians, hospital/outside pharmacy staff; no production change investigated.

**affected nonchooser:** Patients bearing unnecessary expenditure/exposure; wider resistance costs are plausible but not observed here.

**ability to refuse:** Auditors could request outside purchase; real patients' ability to choose depends on access and information. No universal freedom or coercion claim.

## Labour

**tasks:** Prescribing and dispensing tasks allocated across hospital and outside pharmacies; quality may change with revenue incentives.

**entry barriers:** Medical authority and dispensing infrastructure; entry/credential history not researched.

**bargaining ownership:** Hospitals and compensation arrangements connect revenue to practitioner incentives; no causal wage-distribution estimate.

**transition:** Short audit observations only; no individual job transition or reform follow-up.

**distribution:** Young standardized patients only; no distribution by age, income, morbidity or clinician livelihood.

**evidence limit:** Inappropriate prescribing response cannot be converted into net employment or physician-welfare effects.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: provider revenue; demand-incentive interaction; agency; possible external costs

Scripts convey signals beyond money; young simulated patients in one city do not represent other conditions or real reform.

## Evidence links

- [MED-K22](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#med-k22): In experiment 1 of the 2011–2012 one-city Chinese audit, expected purchase outside the hospital reduced antibiotic prescribing from 55% to 10% without a request; with a request, the corresponding rates were 85% and 14%.
- [MED-K23](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#med-k23): The audit’s additional scripts support financial incentives as a major explanation for prescribing in its sampled encounters, while leaving general-equilibrium reform effects unresolved.
- [MED-K24](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#med-k24): This prescribing experiment illustrates a possible divergence between provider revenue and appropriate use, but does not quantify resulting antimicrobial resistance or total social harm.
