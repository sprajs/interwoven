# SKL-C02 — Japanese seasonal clocks and Tanaka's unscaled automatic clock

**Period:** c. 1800–1873; Tanaka's clock completed 1851. **Region:** Japan: Kyoto, Kurume, Saga and surviving museum collections. **Domain:** horology; craft learning; standards.

Japanese makers used adjustable rates or hour markers to serve seasonal hours. Tanaka's automatic variant was an exceptional artifact, not evidence that its complexity translated into sales or a replicable production system.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/SKL/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** European mechanical designs were antecedents; local seasonal-hour mechanisms included several distinct configurations.

**feasibility:** Surviving mechanisms and disassembly establish how adjustments were implemented; original rate stability is not remeasured here.

**deployment:** Seasonal clocks were used; Tanaka made one Myriad Year Clock in 1851.

**diffusion:** Surviving collection is not a population ownership curve; prototype's distribution stayed singular in S04's account.

**scaling:** Tanaka intended additional sales, but S04 reports one unsold clock; no unit cost series.

**non-adoption/niche:** Manually adjusted seasonal clocks remained the practical comparison to the elaborate automated variant.

**displacement/reversal:** Seiko describes gradual disappearance following fixed-hour reform in 1873, not immediate universal replacement.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Seasonal clocks with alternately operating foliots or manually shifted hour markers; Tanaka's 1851 automatic hour-marker arrangement is an explicitly distinguished subconfiguration.

**task:** Display locally meaningful hours that change with seasonal day/night duration, and in Tanaka's artifact combine several calendars/displays.

**population:** Documented makers, users of surviving clock types and prospective elite buyers of Tanaka's clock; not all Japanese households.

**interval:** Early/mid-nineteenth-century configurations, Tanaka's 1847 instruction and 1851 clock, contextual 1873 reform.

**contemporary alternatives:** Manual seasonal adjustment; Equal-hour imported clocks with conversion to local practice; Public time signals and nonmechanical timekeeping; comparative cost not established

**adoption measure:** Surviving mechanism types and single prototype history; qualitative post-reform decline. No sales/household denominator.

**observation limits:** Elite survival bias, uncertain tool reconstruction and anecdotal purchaser refusal. Later disassembly diagnoses construction, not historical user satisfaction.

## Actors

**chooser:** Makers designed for existing time conventions; owners/users chose clock variants; government later changed the legal time system.

**payer:** Customers and patrons in Tanaka's career; intended elite purchaser for the masterpiece was not secured in the source's account.

**operator maintainer:** Users or clockmakers periodically repositioned markers/adjusted foliots; identities and remuneration of routine winders are not established.

**affected nonchooser:** People following shared time signals and workers serving clock owners; specific effects not measured.

**ability to refuse:** The reported buyer could decline the clock. A national time-system change is not proof of every user's voluntary preference; enforcement and compliance are unmeasured.

## Labour

**tasks:** Design, fine metalworking, clock adjustment and calendrical interpretation; automatic markers could remove a recurring adjustment task for this configuration, but no labour saving was measured.

**entry barriers:** Craft ability, access to specialist calendar teaching, tools and patronage; actual tools for Tanaka's fine gears are incompletely known.

**bargaining ownership:** Tanaka ran a commercial shop and later served a feudal patron; no general guild ownership or wage structure inferred.

**transition:** Named career sequence across the 1840s–1850s, not a cohort of clockmakers after 1873.

**distribution:** An unusually successful maker is visible; ordinary makers, household staff and women are poorly represented.

**evidence limit:** Cannot establish widespread deskilling, unemployment after reform, average affordability or that tacit skills were uniquely necessary.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: task-specific design; calendar conventions; periodic adjustment; specialist instruction; patronage

The unsold-clock story is a biographical account with an anecdotal cost explanation; many records were destroyed. A masterpiece is selected precisely because it is unusual.

## Evidence links

- [SKL-K04](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#skl-k04): Japanese clockmakers served seasonal-hour conventions through different mechanisms, including alternating foliots and movable hour markers; many required periodic human adjustment rather than adoption of equal-hour time.
- [SKL-K05](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#skl-k05): Tanaka's 1851 Myriad Year Clock automated seasonal markers, yet Hashimoto reports only one completed clock and no buyer despite an intention to sell more; exceptional mechanical elaboration did not itself establish a scalable product.
- [SKL-K06](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#skl-k06): Tanaka's documented career combined commercial craft work, instruction in calendrical theory/practice from the Tsuchimikado family in 1847, and later Saga patronage; the exact tools used to make his clock's fine components remain uncertain.
