# NET-C01 — Paper manufacture, imports and acceptance around the Islamic Mediterranean

**Period:** ca. 750–1500; later material and craft evidence used only as qualified comparators. **Region:** West Asia; North Africa; Mediterranean Europe. **Domain:** paper; craft production; trade; knowledge media.

Papermaking and paper use crossed connected regions through uneven production and trade networks. Imported sheets could support writing without local manufacturing capability. Material suitability and negotiated acceptance are supported interactions; a quantified cost explanation is not established.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/NET/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Not located here: the case concerns transmission/adaptation of an older technique, without a unique inventor or battle claim.

**feasibility:** Broad evidence of manufacturing in Baghdad by the late eighth century and Mediterranean Christian production by the twelfth, mediated by Bloom's synthesis (NET-K01).

**deployment:** Manufacturing history is separated from the use of Italian watermarked paper in a Tunisian royal letter of 1350 (NET-K03).

**diffusion:** Qualitative chronology and traded-paper examples; no consistent mill, household or writing-volume denominator.

**scaling:** Craft inputs and finishing are examined, but no cross-regional productivity or price series is available.

**non-adoption/niche:** Religious concern about watermark imagery is documented alongside a permissive 1409 ruling, not a measured population refusal.

**displacement/reversal:** Local manufacturing and imported paper may change relative importance; no claim that paper use ceased where domestic production contracted.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Fibre-sheet manufacture and purchased writing paper; separate subepisodes are Islamic/Mediterranean transmission, 1350 Tunisian imported-paper use and 1409 permission. Barrett's European 1300–1800 workflow and later material samples are limited comparators.

**task:** Produce or obtain sheets suitable for writing/printing and acceptable to the intended user.

**population:** Historically documented producers and selected literate purchasers/users; the Tunisian court letter and juristic ruling do not sample ordinary households.

**interval:** ca. 750–1500 for transmission/use; later manufacturing descriptions and conservation samples are not backcast as uptake measures.

**contemporary alternatives:** Imported paper instead of local manufacture; Different paper grades and finishing; Parchment or papyrus for some earlier tasks, without a measured task-by-task choice set

**adoption measure:** Qualitative production chronology and a named watermarked letter; no regional denominator. The 228-paper laboratory collection, including 133 undated samples, measures material properties rather than adoption.

**observation limits:** Manuscript survival, uncertain paper provenance and later craft descriptions; text language does not establish where a sheet was made.

## Actors

**chooser:** Makers select production practices; courts, scribes and purchasers choose materials within available supplies; a jurist addresses permissibility in one recorded dispute.

**payer:** Patrons and purchasers fund texts/materials, and producers acquire equipment and inputs; cost shares and credit terms are not measured.

**operator maintainer:** Papermaking and finishing workers, traders and scribes; specific employment and unpaid household arrangements are not reconstructed.

**affected nonchooser:** Workers preparing materials and people reliant on patrons' documents; effects on competing material trades are not quantified.

**ability to refuse:** The 1409 question indicates concern about using certain imported sheets, but actual refusals and purchasers' alternatives are unknown. No free-choice inference from eventual use.

## Labour

**tasks:** Material procurement, fibre preparation, forming, drying, sizing/finishing and writing; the evidence identifies tasks, not net job gains.

**entry barriers:** Craft skill, usable water/materials, equipment and trading access; relative importance and exclusion rules unknown.

**bargaining ownership:** Control of workshops, water and supply routes is potentially consequential but not systematically documented in these sources.

**transition:** No individual worker or cohort series; broad manufacturing movement cannot establish retraining duration.

**distribution:** Evidence privileges surviving high-quality sheets and literate patrons; wages, ordinary consumers and downstream harms are not measured.

**evidence limit:** No occupational employment or real-income estimate, and no inference that imported supply improved every producer's livelihood.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: input quality and craft skill; trade substitutes for local production; task-specific material suitability; negotiated religious acceptability

Bloom's diffusion and cost interpretations are partly inherited; later surviving-paper collections are not medieval production samples. A ruling does not demonstrate a subsequent rise in purchases. Cheap imports, local input scarcity and political change remain rival explanations.

## Evidence links

- [NET-K01](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#net-k01): Bloom's historical synthesis places papermaking in Baghdad by the late eighth century and its transmission to Mediterranean Christians by the twelfth; this is a broad chronology of manufacture, not a unique invention event.
- [NET-K02](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#net-k02): For the high-quality European book/writing-paper workflow reconstructed for 1300–1800, water quality, fibre preparation and gelatin sizing affected usable sheet properties; a generic recipe for pulp did not capture the complete craft.
- [NET-K03](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#net-k03): Bloom cites Italian watermarked paper used in a Tunisian royal letter in 1350 and a 1409 Ibn Marzuq ruling permitting writing on Christian-made paper with objectionable watermark imagery; imported paper could support local writing without local manufacture.
- [NET-K04](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#net-k04): Mahgoub et al.'s 228-paper collection includes 133 undated samples and mostly eighteenth-/nineteenth-century dated examples; text language and finishing cannot by themselves establish paper's place of manufacture.
