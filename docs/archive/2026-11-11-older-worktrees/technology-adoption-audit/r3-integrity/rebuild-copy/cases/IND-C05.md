# IND-C05 — India–Lancashire competition, colonial markets and uneven displacement

**Period:** c. 1700–1900. **Region:** India; Bengal; Coromandel; Lancashire; Atlantic markets. **Domain:** cotton textiles; global trade; colonial institutions.

Productivity, cotton/freight prices, domestic political and agricultural shocks, slavery-linked demand and asymmetric policy power offer interacting explanations.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/IND/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** This case evaluates competitive diffusion and displacement rather than identifying a first machine.

**feasibility:** British machinery could be feasible without immediately undercutting every Indian product after input and delivery costs.

**deployment:** British mechanised output reached different export markets at different times; Indian manufacture predated this competition.

**diffusion:** Trade and price reconstructions track competitive shifts; they are not uniform adoption rates for Indian producers.

**scaling:** Expanding markets and raw-material supply interacted with machine productivity; colonial market access was politically shaped.

**non-adoption:** Low wages alone are not accepted as a sufficient explanation for Indian production choices; capital and policy constraints need further study.

**displacement:** Hand-spinning, export production, handloom weaving and later mill growth require separate series; universal destruction of handicrafts is not claimed.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Indian artisanal textile supply and Lancashire mechanised competition through different markets; early cost competition and later colonial import penetration are explicit subepisodes.

**task:** Sell cotton yarn/cloth of specified quality and obtain livelihood or merchant profit.

**population:** Reconstructed British/Indian trade, wages and prices; regional producers are aggregated imperfectly. India is not treated as one factory or a homogeneous chooser.

**interval:** c. 1700–1900, with model phase distinctions around 1810 and later mill growth

**contemporary alternatives:** Indian hand-produced export and local cloth; British mechanised imports; Other fibre/product/market specialisations; Later Indian mills; Protective policy where political authority permitted it

**adoption measure:** Trade flows, relative prices, employment reconstructions and qualitative market change; loss of export share is not a technology-adoption denominator.

**observation limits:** Quality, task and household labour units differ; aggregate shares can change because foreign output expands. Colonial records have administrative selection.

## Actors

**chooser:** British and Indian producers and merchants choose production/sourcing within constraints; British/Company political authorities shape trade rules.

**payer:** Producers, investors and trading firms fund production; enslaved producers and dependent households bear labour costs not reducible to voluntary capital supply.

**operator maintainer:** Spinners, weavers, dyers, factory workers and mechanics across distinct systems.

**affected nonchooser:** Indian textile households, enslaved Atlantic commodity producers, and subjects without equivalent tariff sovereignty.

**ability to refuse:** Colonial subjects lacked symmetric authority over trade policy; enslaved labour was coerced. Individual artisans’ practical exit options and contract restrictions require regional study.

## Labour

**tasks:** Spinning, weaving and finishing faced different technical shocks; some labour shifted activities while later mills created different jobs.

**entry barriers:** Machine finance, raw materials, technical services, merchant access and occupational institutions; their separate effects remain unmeasured.

**bargaining ownership:** Imperial authorities shaped protection; merchants and owners controlled market access. Household earnings cannot be equated with an individual male wage.

**transition:** Models distinguish phases, but lack sufficient person-level data to track displaced families into later occupations.

**distribution:** Decline differs by task and locality; no claim that later growth compensated earlier households or that imported cheap cloth settled the welfare balance.

**evidence limit:** Competing reconstructions do not identify a unique colonial, climate or wage contribution; original fiscal and household records remain necessary.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: relative productivity and wages; raw cotton and transport; colonial tariff power; Atlantic demand and coerced production; political/agricultural disruption; quality-specific markets

National aggregate estimates and wage units are fragile; a cost advantage does not identify market formation, and a coercive policy does not identify every product’s counterfactual.

## Evidence links

- [IND-K13](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k13): Broadberry and Gupta’s reconstruction makes the timing of Lancashire’s competitive gains depend on raw-cotton and transport costs as well as wages and uneven productivity advances across spinning and weaving.
- [IND-K14](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k14): Clingingsmith and Williamson propose that pre-1810 Indian textile decline involved domestic political/agricultural shocks, while later decline involved stronger international manufactured-price pressure.
- [IND-K15](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k15): Inikori argues that Atlantic demand linked to slavery-based commodity production helped form the markets and regional expansion within which British cotton mechanisation developed.
- [IND-K16](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k16): Inikori’s discussion of Charles Grant’s 1821 testimony identifies asymmetric British and Indian tariff power as a contemporary concern alongside improvements in machinery.
