# LBR-C02 — US new job specialties and innovation, 1940–2018

**Period:** 1940–2018. **Region:** United States. **Domain:** labour; measurement; digital and industrial systems.

A longitudinal title-based study documents new work and connects it with innovation and demand. Its measurement is informative but cannot be converted directly into a count of technology-created jobs or a forecast of opportunity.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/LBR/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Study uses patent-based exposure as one innovation measure, not a list of first inventions.

**feasibility:** Not directly measured for each technology.

**deployment:** Patent exposure is not equivalent to installed adoption.

**diffusion:** Occupational title emergence and employment composition are observed/imputed; no universal technology adoption rate.

**scaling:** Aggregate employment allocation changes over decades.

**non-adoption:** Inventions not patented and work not captured by title revisions remain partly unobserved.

**displacement:** Automation and new tasks can operate simultaneously and affect different occupations.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Multiple innovation exposures matched to US occupational tasks and outputs; a comparative data study rather than one device

**task:** Measure creation and reorganisation of job specialties

**population:** US census occupations and employed population, 1940–2018; micro-title counts do not directly enumerate workers

**interval:** 1940–2018, with 1940–1980 and 1980–2018 contrasts

**contemporary alternatives:** Existing job tasks/titles; Reorganisation within existing occupations

**adoption measure:** New Census Alphabetical Index titles; imputed employment shares; patent-to-task/output similarity exposures. These are distinct proxies.

**observation limits:** Title-based imputation, changing classifications and incomplete patent coverage; no universal count of new occupations or technology-caused employment.

## Actors

**chooser:** Firms, customers and workers jointly shape tasks; census classification is an observation institution.

**payer:** Employers and buyers fund paid work; unpaid work is outside the principal measurement.

**operator maintainer:** Workers in changing occupational task bundles.

**affected nonchooser:** Workers losing tasks or excluded from new specialties.

**ability to refuse:** Not identified at individual level in these aggregate data.

## Labour

**tasks:** Creation and displacement can coexist within and across occupations.

**entry barriers:** Education, skills, resources and discrimination are not fully identified by title counts.

**bargaining ownership:** Distribution of task value between owners and workers is not recovered from title emergence alone.

**transition:** Decadal aggregate patterns; individual mobility into new work not directly established.

**distribution:** Composition shifted across pay/skill groups; no guarantee of equal access.

**evidence limit:** US historical patterns do not forecast occupations, net employment or accessible gains for a present individual.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: task complementarity; demand shifts; occupational reclassification

New titles can reflect classification changes; patents miss innovation and expose the same occupation to both automation and augmentation.

## Evidence links

- [LBR-K05](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#lbr-k05): The roughly 60 percent estimate of 2018 US employment in job titles added since 1940 is an imputation from new-title shares within occupations, not a direct count of workers in new micro-titles.
- [LBR-K06](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#lbr-k06): The new-work study links title emergence to both output-complementing innovation and demand shifts; its patent-based categories may overlap and cannot exhaust technological change.
