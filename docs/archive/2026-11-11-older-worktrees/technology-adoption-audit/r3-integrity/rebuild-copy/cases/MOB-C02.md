# MOB-C02 — Inka roads and local appropriation in the Atacama

**Period:** c. 1400–1530, with earlier route inheritance and later reuse explicitly distinguished. **Region:** Andes; Peru; Bolivia; Chile; Argentina; Ecuador; Colombia. **Domain:** transport; imperial logistics; mining; road maintenance.

Inka integration combined inherited roads with logistical facilities, while archaeology in one Atacama district supports local mining exchange beyond a solely state-commanded road economy.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/MOB/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Earlier Andean routes were incorporated; no Inka invention of roads or sole origin is asserted.

**feasibility:** Roads, drainage, bridges, shelters and stores made travel possible in different environments; local water/forage constraints remained.

**deployment:** Imperial roads and formal wayside facilities operated alongside local camps and paths.

**diffusion:** Expansion of connected territory is qualitative; no fraction of subjects using roads is available.

**scaling:** Imperial incorporation, maintenance and new links expanded a network over c. fifteenth–early sixteenth centuries.

**non-adoption/niche:** Regular llama caravans are doubtful in the specific dry survey segment; pedestrian movement is inferred, not counted.

**displacement/reversal:** Regime change did not erase all road use; the 2014 evaluation observes later adaptation and community maintenance, not unchanged operation of the original empire.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** An Andean road-and-wayside-supply network; the causal microcase is a 12 km segment beside Cachiyuyo de Llampos mining camps.

**task:** Official travel/provisioning and possible local craft exchange; these purposes need not have identical users or permissions.

**population:** Imperial network components in heritage evaluation; camps, roadside sites and travelers inferred from archaeological remains in a 58 km² survey.

**interval:** Late pre-Hispanic/Inka episode, approximately fifteenth–early sixteenth centuries, with pre-Inka comparisons. Later road survival is a separate observation.

**contemporary alternatives:** pre-Inka paths and local exchanges; pedestrian carriage; llama caravans where water and forage permit; highland routes with pasture (not equivalent to the surveyed desert crossing)

**adoption measure:** Material distributions and settlement location changes; no census of road users or quantitative diffusion denominator.

**observation limits:** Ceramic chronology, surface visibility and selected test excavations; elite narratives and formal architecture disproportionately survive. Local permission and trading volumes are not directly recorded.

## Actors

**chooser:** Inka authorities selected integration and supply structures; local artisan-miner households appear to have situated activities near the road.

**payer:** Imperial and local resources; mit’a and state provisioning are documented in the broader scholarly account, but segment-specific labour budgets are unknown.

**operator maintainer:** Road officials, travelers, porters and local communities; exact construction and maintenance allocation at the surveyed segment is unknown.

**affected nonchooser:** Subjugated communities and labour contributors; local beneficiaries cannot be treated as representative of all subjects.

**ability to refuse:** Imperial conquest and labour obligations mean subject consent cannot be assumed. Garrido discusses chronicles restricting ordinary road use, but local archaeology suggests limits to control; actual refusal rights at these camps are unknown.

## Labour

**tasks:** Road building/maintenance, provisioning, carrying, mining, bead/pigment production and roadside exchange. Paid/unpaid distinction is not safely reducible to modern wages.

**entry barriers:** Access to scarce water, ores, paths, supplies and social permissions; specific household exclusion rules unknown.

**bargaining ownership:** Official facilities contrast with household production spaces; control of surplus and any tax burden at local camps are not directly observed.

**transition:** Settlement/craft change between archaeological periods; no tracked individuals or measured retraining duration.

**distribution:** Some local miners plausibly gained exchange opportunities; no claim that coerced labourers or other communities gained equally.

**evidence limit:** Cannot quantify net welfare, coercive burden, household income or whether every observed craft expansion was voluntary.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: inherited infrastructure; state provisioning; ecological constraints; labour obligations and imperial control; local exchange and appropriation; maintenance

Large state-directed mines existed elsewhere. A local absence of state control signatures cannot be generalized to all Inka subjects or all roads.
