# MAT-C03 — Chinese iron routes and later Song–Yuan fuel substitution

**Period:** Early first millennium BCE through third century BCE; separate comparison in Song–Yuan periods, 960–1368 CE. **Region:** East Asia; China: northwest, Central Plains and southern states; Song–Yuan iron districts. **Domain:** iron; casting; fuel; ceramics.

Different iron forms and casting complements matter; later mineral-fuel substitution should not be mistaken for a uniform national transition.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/MAT/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Meteoritic use, bloomery smelting, casting and blast furnaces have distinct and disputed earliest evidence.

**feasibility:** Casting requires handling molten high-carbon iron; annealing/fining can adjust useful properties under different conditions.

**deployment:** Early practical implements and later ironworks document deployment; elite early objects are not mass-use evidence.

**diffusion:** Regional contrasts and distribution are qualitative; no unified Chinese adoption curve is claimed.

**scaling:** Moulds, thermal treatment and specialised production support repeat output; famous Song tonnage extrapolation is unreliable.

**non-adoption/niche:** Bloomery and casting routes had different requirements; reasons for limited alternative use are contested.

**displacement/reversal:** Mineral fuel could substitute selectively for charcoal, with continued use of other fuels and processes.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Subepisode A: early first-millennium BCE bloomery objects and cast-iron implements with heat treatment; subepisode B: Song–Yuan manufacture using mineral fuel in some operations

**task:** A: prestige objects and practical implements; B: iron manufacture for various products whose route/fuel allocation remains uncertain

**population:** A: excavated objects/workshops in differentiated Chinese regions; B: reported ironworks, records and a few dated castings. These are not one adoption population

**interval:** Early first millennium BCE through third century BCE; separate comparison in Song–Yuan periods, 960–1368 CE

**contemporary alternatives:** A: bronze and bloomery-derived objects; uptake varies by task and location; B: charcoal and mineral fuel, including unresolved coal/coke distinctions; remelting may differ from smelting

**adoption measure:** Qualitative occurrence/distribution and installations, not a single curve. Song quota deliveries are recorded but total-output extrapolation is contested.

**observation limits:** A and B are separated by many centuries and distinct institutions; no transitive causal claim connects them. Grave goods and surviving works overrepresent particular uses.

## Actors

**chooser:** A: artisans and commissioners/users, exact rights uncertain. B: ironworks operators and institutional purchasers, inferred from records but not systematically mapped.

**payer:** A: unknown. B: state quota receipts document deliveries, not financing of all production.

**operator maintainer:** A: smelters, casters, mould/kiln workers and smiths. B: smelters, bellows operators and foundry workers; ownership and wages unknown.

**affected nonchooser:** Agricultural users and workers/suppliers could experience changing access and work; no representative outcomes measured.

**ability to refuse:** Unknown in these episodes; claims of state suppression in MAT-S15 are hypotheses, not evidence that every worker lacked choice.

## Labour

**tasks:** A: coordinated casting, mould production, thermal treatment and forging. B: fuel supply and furnace/blast operation may change. No quantified occupational displacement.

**entry barriers:** Equipment, skilled process coordination and raw-material access; policy barriers proposed but not directly audited.

**bargaining ownership:** State/quota institutions matter in some periods, but control and gains cannot be assigned uniformly across A/B.

**transition:** A and B are distinct long episodes; no individual/cohort histories of retraining.

**distribution:** No comparable wages, worker welfare or user access series.

**evidence limit:** A division of process steps does not prove rigid separate occupations or lower total skill.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: cross-craft complements; heat-treatment time and fuel; mould repeatability; task demand; state support/suppression hypothesis

Broad China/West comparisons hide regional variation. No proof of a single origin, general coke adoption or inevitable political monopoly.

## Evidence links

- [MAT-K08](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mat-k08): Chinese iron history requires separate chronologies for meteoritic objects, bloomery production, casting and practical cast-iron implements; later finds revised earlier accounts of a wholly southern, cast-iron beginning.
- [MAT-K09](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mat-k09): Chinese cast-iron production plausibly combined iron smelting with bronze-casting and ceramic/kiln expertise, but the claim that political suppression explains the absence of alternative iron routes remains a hypothesis.
- [MAT-K10](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mat-k10): Song–Yuan mineral fuel in iron manufacture is supported by texts and anomalously old carbon dates in castings, but a universal shift to coke, or one exact production route, is not established.
- [MAT-K11](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mat-k11): The often-repeated large estimate of Song iron output is not a secure observation: Wagner explicitly rejects the reliability of the extrapolation from state quota figures.
- [MAT-K23](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mat-k23): Recycling and composite manufacture complicate inferences from surviving iron objects to initial production, replacement rates or material scarcity.
- [MAT-K24](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mat-k24): Across these purposively selected cases, repeatable processes, complementary crafts and task-specific demand are better-supported comparison variables than a universal ranking of materials.
