# MOB-C06 — Containerization: interoperable equipment, trade and contested dock work

**Period:** 1956–1990; US labour negotiations 1960–1987 explicitly separated from global trade outcomes. **Region:** United States West Coast; North Atlantic; Global trade panel. **Domain:** shipping; intermodal logistics; standards; labour.

Compatible equipment and institutional settlements expanded container systems, while published trade estimates and labour records sharply limit simple claims of universally shared efficiency gains.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/MOB/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Boxes and earlier experiments predated the postwar system; no single-inventor claim.

**feasibility:** 1956 Ideal-X commercial US voyage demonstrates one operational configuration, not global scalability.

**deployment:** Shipping lines and terminals invested in cranes, ships and facilities; US and ISO standardization followed distinct negotiations.

**diffusion:** Published study dates country-level first port/rail facilities during 1966–1983 and follows trade through 1990; first facility is not saturation.

**scaling:** Intermodal compatibility, equipment, leasing and state/military demand supported expansion; many products/locations had different utilization.

**non-adoption/niche:** Prewar attempts and early port reluctance are documented; some products remained unsuited to containers and break-bulk/bulk alternatives coexisted.

**displacement/reversal:** Many manual dock tasks were removed or relocated; new handling and off-dock work were contested rather than equivalent for all workers.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** A: international standardized container port/rail system in the trade panel. B: US West Coast labour agreements and off-dock work jurisdiction. These populations are kept distinct.

**task:** Transfer freight across modes with less repeated cargo handling; preserve or reallocate work and finance technological adjustment.

**population:** 157-country bilateral product-trade sample in published econometrics; specific PMA/ILWU/Teamster employers and workers in US court records.

**interval:** US deployment 1956; global adoption measure 1966–1983/trade through 1990; West Coast agreements and dispute 1960–1987.

**contemporary alternatives:** break-bulk shipping; palletized cargo; bulk or roll-on/roll-off systems for unsuitable products; company-specific container sizes and handling systems

**adoption measure:** First port or rail container facilities by country; product-containerizability interactions; separately, documented work rules and litigated task jurisdiction. No worker-consent or worldwide welfare measure.

**observation limits:** Containerizability changes over time; country adoption is coarse; trade-policy and selection confounding persist; court disputes do not represent all labour markets.

## Actors

**chooser:** Shipping firms, port authorities, standards bodies and state/military purchasers chose investments and specifications; union/employer negotiators chose some work-rule terms.

**payer:** Private capital, public subsidies/port investments, freight users and employer-financed compensation; assessment incidence was litigated.

**operator maintainer:** Crane operators, longshore workers, drivers, off-dock freight-station workers and equipment maintainers; no full staffing balance reconstructed.

**affected nonchooser:** Workers outside protected bargaining groups, bypassed ports and nearby communities; global producer and resident impacts are not fully observed.

**ability to refuse:** Union bargaining could condition work-rule changes, but negotiations do not imply universal consent. Teamsters and nonmember operators contested claimed jurisdiction; bypassed ports and residents had different, unmeasured voice.

## Labour

**tasks:** Less repeated break-bulk handling; mechanized lifting; stuffing/unstuffing relocated partly off-dock; changed maintenance and dispatch requirements.

**entry barriers:** Capital and compatible equipment; union jurisdiction and employer-association membership structured access to work. Individual training barriers not quantified.

**bargaining ownership:** PMA/ILWU fund and work-rule bargain; later disputes show competing longshore and Teamster claims. Investors, workers and shippers had separate interests.

**transition:** 1960 agreement, subsequent unanticipated work losses and litigation through 1987 show a prolonged institutional transition; no complete person-level cohort.

**distribution:** A $29m fund is documented and funding charges differed by shipper; it is not evidence that all job or community losses were covered.

**evidence limit:** Cannot infer net employment, complete compensation, global wage effects, land displacement or pollution costs from trade growth.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: standardization; intermodal complements; state subsidies and procurement; capital-intensive investment; labour bargaining; task relocation; trade-cost quality improvements

Large adoption-date trade estimates fail some pre-trend tests. Negotiated compensation does not prove complete compensation; technological substitution also shifts work between groups.

## Evidence links

- [MOB-K19](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mob-k19): Postwar container scaling required compatible handling equipment and intermodal facilities; the published trade study distinguishes 1956 US commercial deployment from the start of its international diffusion period in 1966.
- [MOB-K20](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mob-k20): US construction subsidies tied to approved container dimensions, international standardization and military logistics demand in the Vietnam War helped create conditions for container diffusion; these were distinct from intrinsic box efficiency.
- [MOB-K21](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mob-k21): Bernhofen and colleagues warn that very large estimates using adoption dates alone do not convincingly establish causal trade effects; their product-containerizability comparisons are smaller and provide stronger but still qualified support.
- [MOB-K22](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mob-k22): The 1960 West Coast mechanization agreement exchanged union acceptance of labour-saving changes for a $29 million fund over 1961–1966, and its financing imposed uneven charges contested by Volkswagen.
- [MOB-K23](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mob-k23): Containerization shifted much stuffing and unstuffing away from docks, creating conflict between longshore and Teamster jurisdictions after the initial West Coast mechanization bargain.
