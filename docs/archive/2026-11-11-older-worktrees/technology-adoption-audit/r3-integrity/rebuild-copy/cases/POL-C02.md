# POL-C02 — Yuan paper currency: monetary rules, fiscal pressure and alternatives

**Period:** 1260–1368 (quantitative series to 1355). **Region:** Yuan China; Dadu; Jiangnan; Heicheng. **Domain:** finance; taxation; administration.

Mongol rulers deployed successive paper-currency regimes and administrative supports; long periods of moderate reconstructed inflation coexist with later depreciation, regional diversity and fiscal strain.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/POL/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Paper currency predates the Yuan; no first-invention claim.

**deployment:** Silver-backed issues from 1260 and legal-tender/administrative use.

**scaling:** Expansion across conquered territories, while local alternatives and account units persist.

**non-adoption/niche:** Regional exceptions, private silver transactions and unequal exchange access.

**displacement/reversal:** Erosion of convertibility and severe late instability; precise late acceptance rates unavailable.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** State-issued paper notes with changing silver-redemption arrangements; distinguish 1260–76, 1277–1309 and post-1310 regimes.

**task:** Settle taxes, salaries and commerce; mobilize imperial purchasing power.

**population:** Imperial note issuers and recorded transactions/prices; Heicheng/Huizhou contracts appear only through Ichimaru abstract.

**interval:** 1260–1368; formal dynasty founded 1271; issue/price analysis 1260–1355.

**contemporary alternatives:** Uncoined silver and older metal coins; Regional cowry-shell use in Yunnan reported by scholarship; Barter in later disruption; prevalence unmeasured; Predecessor Song/Jin paper issues, not simultaneous identical configurations

**adoption measure:** Legal provisions, recorded taxes/payments, note issuance and sparse price reconstruction; no proportion of transactions conducted in notes.

**observation limits:** Money issues are not money stock or acceptance; stock uses assumed attrition. Official exchange rates may differ from market rates; about a dozen commodity observations each underpin interpolated CPI.

## Actors

**chooser:** Khan/court and monetary officials choose issues, rules and reserves; merchants and households respond within constraints.

**payer:** Treasury resources and taxpayers; note holders may bear depreciation, but incidence is unmeasured.

**operator maintainer:** Printing/authentication officials, exchange-bureau staff, tax/payroll personnel and merchants.

**affected nonchooser:** Salary recipients, creditors/debtors, taxpayers and populations affected by warfare and price changes.

**ability to refuse:** Legal restrictions and Polo’s reported sanctions constrain refusal; continued alternatives show imperfect uniformity. No consent or actual punishment-frequency measure.

## Labour

**tasks:** Note authentication, replacement, accounting and exchange administration; Polo describes official work and experts appraising goods.

**entry barriers:** Official appointment, knowledge and capital for exchange/commerce; entry conditions and exclusion not quantified.

**bargaining ownership:** State controls note rules; merchants/households retain some alternative transactions; distribution of seigniorage and losses unknown.

**transition:** Regime chronology and annual issues, not worker or household cohorts.

**distribution:** Regional redemption availability/account units differ; no occupation-specific real-wage or wealth trajectories.

**evidence limit:** Neither an index nor tax usability establishes living standards or who gained from monetization.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: tax/payment demand; redemption and administrative authentication; fiscal pressure; coercive legal requirements; regional monetary conventions

The inflation series is sparse and reconstructed; no deterministic claim that fiat money or low capacity inevitably doomed the regime.

## Evidence links

- [POL-K05](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#pol-k05): Guan, Palma and Wu distinguish full silver convertibility (1260–76), nominal convertibility (1277–1309) and a fiat regime from 1310; exchange availability and official status were distinct.
- [POL-K06](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#pol-k06): The study’s reconstructed respectability CPI estimates annual inflation of 11.0% for 1260–1290, 1.8% for 1290–1340 and 4.5% for 1340–1355, contradicting uniform century-long hyperinflation.
- [POL-K07](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#pol-k07): In the reconstructed 1260–1355 series, civil-war frequency correlates with greater nominal and real note issuance; imperial grants do not show comparable statistical associations in Table 4.
- [POL-K08](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#pol-k08): Yuan legal monetary unification coexisted with regional and task-specific practices: Ichimaru reports different uses of guanwen and dingliang, while Guan et al. report silver property transactions despite official restrictions.
- [POL-K09](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#pol-k09): Polo’s paper-money narrative combines threat of punishment for refusal with portability, official authentication and damaged-note replacement; it cannot independently establish willing universal acceptance.
