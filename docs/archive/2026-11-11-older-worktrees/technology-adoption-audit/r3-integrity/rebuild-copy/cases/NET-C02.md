# NET-C02 — Chinese woodblocks and selective movable-type use

**Period:** 868–ca. 1850; distinct dated-object, process-testimony and later publishing subepisodes. **Region:** China. **Domain:** printing; publishing; craft organisation.

Sponsored woodblock production, an eleventh-century description of clay type and later task-specific publishing show that feasibility, use and dominance are separate. Reusable blocks and divisible craft stages could remain useful; character inventory alone does not determine every method choice.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/NET/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** The 868 Diamond Sutra is a surviving dated production, not a first-invention claim. Shen describes Bi Sheng's clay type in 1041–1048 (NET-K05–K06).

**feasibility:** Shen's process account includes levelling, repeated common characters and alternating boards, but no independent timed trial or surviving Bi Sheng run.

**deployment:** The Diamond Sutra's sponsor and declared distribution purpose are recorded; later scholarship identifies movable-type deployment in genealogies and imperial projects.

**diffusion:** Woodblock predominance and movable-type niches in the comparative scholarship are qualitative; no common producer or reader denominator.

**scaling:** Retained blocks, separated stages and reprinting can alter scheduling and inventory commitments; no universal cost coefficient is assigned.

**non-adoption/niche:** Movable type is selectively employed, not categorically unavailable. Particular genres or court projects cannot stand for all publishing.

**displacement/reversal:** No automatic replacement of woodblocks by type is established; coexistence is the bounded outcome.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Three separated subepisodes: Wang Jie's 868 woodblock scroll; Shen's account of Bi Sheng's clay types; later Chinese woodblock enterprises and selected movable-type genealogical/imperial projects.

**task:** Reproduce specified texts for donation, repeat publication or institutional/private users; commercial revenue is not every sponsor's objective.

**population:** One named religious sponsor/object, one historical technical account and selected publishing practices in comparative scholarship; no common sampled population.

**interval:** 868; 1041–1048 as described in the late eleventh century; qualitative comparison chiefly 1450–1850.

**contemporary alternatives:** Hand copying; Woodblock printing; Clay or other movable type where equipment/skills were available; Reprinting stored blocks rather than holding finished-book stock

**adoption measure:** Object and process testimony plus qualitative evidence of woodblock predominance and movable-type niches. Print runs, workshop counts and population readership are unavailable.

**observation limits:** Preservation favours some texts and patrons. Shen's reported convenience is not a measured price comparison; the later workflow survey spans heterogeneous markets.

## Actors

**chooser:** Donors, court projects and publishers choose texts and methods; workshop workers execute and adapt production.

**payer:** The Diamond Sutra colophon names its sponsor; later patronage, court funding and commercial sales are distinct financing arrangements.

**operator maintainer:** Scribes, block cutters, printers, binders and type makers/compositors; maintaining reusable materials entails storage and handling.

**affected nonchooser:** Workers and prospective readers constrained by text availability, literacy, cost and patron choices; individual outcomes are not enumerated.

**ability to refuse:** Evidence identifies patron/publisher choices rather than general worker or reader consent. Availability of alternative equipment and access to capital are not measured.

## Labour

**tasks:** Copy preparation, carving, sorting/composition, printing, binding and storing blocks/types. Methods reorganise tasks rather than eliminating skilled work altogether.

**entry barriers:** Literacy/calligraphy, cutting or type skills, working materials, premises and access to patrons/distribution; no wage or training-duration panel.

**bargaining ownership:** Control may lie with sponsors, officials or publishers; separable stages allow different work arrangements but do not establish bargaining gains.

**transition:** No tracked artisans moving between manuscript, block and type work; long coexistence does not measure an individual transition time.

**distribution:** A donated religious text and a commercially sold book have different beneficiaries and success measures; access and earnings by class/gender remain underobserved.

**evidence limit:** Cannot infer national employment, universal cheapness, optimality or literacy from surviving editions or qualitative predominance.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: task-specific production economics; reusable page storage; division of craft work; patronage and demand; script costs without script determinism

Woodblocks also require cutting skill and storage; movable type has its own large-run advantages in Shen's testimony. Court subsidies and producer constraints may explain niches. Qualitative workflows do not prove that every observed choice minimised costs. A script-sensitive cost model also predicts type niches, so their existence does not disprove character-cost explanations of relative uptake (NET-S16).

## Evidence links

- [NET-K05](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#net-k05): The surviving 868 Diamond Sutra's translated colophon names Wang Jie and a distribution dedication for his parents; it documents sponsored woodblock production and declared purpose, not achieved circulation volume.
- [NET-K06](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#net-k06): Shen Gua describes Bi Sheng's clay-type process in 1041–1048, including type levelling, multiple common characters and alternating boards; he reports greater convenience for many copies than a few.
- [NET-K07](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#net-k07): McDermott and Burke identify spatially and temporally separable craft stages, reusable blocks and thin-paper use as advantages of Chinese woodblock production relative to European press-room organisation.
- [NET-K08](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#net-k08): Chinese movable-type use in particular genealogical and imperial projects coexisted with predominant woodblock printing in 1450–1850; a writing system alone is insufficient to specify which method was used for every task.
