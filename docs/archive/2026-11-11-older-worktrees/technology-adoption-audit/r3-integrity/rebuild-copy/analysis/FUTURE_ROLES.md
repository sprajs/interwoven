# Future human roles: conditional research propositions

Revised historical edition, 5 November 2026. This document contains original inference and proposed tests. It is separated from historical findings because an activity that mattered in the past is not guaranteed to remain human, scarce, accessible or well paid. It does not forecast occupations or recommend particular investments.

## The distinction that matters

A technology can increase useful output while reducing demand for a particular worker. New tasks can arise without being open to displaced people. Social value can increase without the person doing the work capturing much of it. These are separate propositions, so “human leverage” needs at least three meanings: contribution to a useful outcome; bargaining or ownership power to receive part of the value; and practical ability to enter and remain in the role.

The telephone-switching case provides a concrete constraint: adverse outcomes for linked incumbent operators coexisted with no measured reduction in overall employment for subsequent young female cohorts in the study population. Containerisation moved work across locations and jurisdictions, with negotiated compensation and disputes. Household technologies changed unpaid as well as paid tasks. A history of aggregate output or new job names cannot substitute for those transitions. [LBR-C01–C02](../contributions/LBR/report.md); [MOB-C06](../contributions/MOB/report.md); [AGR-C08](../contributions/AGR/report.md).

The following are candidate places to investigate. They are not ranked by future demand, and they do not imply that all such work should be preserved if it can be done better another way.

## Candidate A — Making a technically capable system reliable in a real setting

**Historical basis.** Penicillin supply required production, purification and delivery; electric drive required suitable installation and organisation; Meroe reconstruction exposed operating variables absent from furnace geometry. These cases support a difference between a capability and repeatable service. [MED-C05](../contributions/MED/report.md); [IND-C08](../contributions/IND/report.md); [MAT-C04](../contributions/MAT/report.md).

**Access countercase.** Telephone-switching maintenance was a complementary task, but technicians were nearly all men. The studied successive young female cohorts did not move into other telephone jobs after cutover; some linked incumbents did, a different comparison. A real technical need therefore did not establish a broadly accessible transition. [LBR-K08](../contributions/LBR/report.md).

**Proposed analogue.** People may contribute where equipment or software must be adapted to heterogeneous sites, workflows and failure modes: commissioning, process diagnosis, quality assurance and recovery. The prospective value lies in reducing measurable failure, delay or waste.

**Boundary conditions.** Local conditions must matter; reliable performance must have a payer; the worker needs relevant access and competence; gains must exceed the cost of intervention. Responsibility without authority is not sufficient.

**What breaks the analogy.** Standardisation, remote operation, better self-diagnosis or automation may remove the local difficulty. A vendor may bundle the service and capture its value. A necessary task can also become routine and low paid once training and supply expand.

**Next evidence.** Compare actual failure and recovery records across settings; identify unresolved causes, who can act, what customers pay and whether improvements persist. Measure whether the required expertise is being commoditised or remains scarce for a defensible reason.

## Candidate B — Translating capabilities into competent use

**Historical basis.** Antisepsis spread through changing operative practices as well as texts; oral-rehydration programmes required preparation, recognition and care; navigational instruments worked with learned route knowledge. [MED-C03, MED-C07](../contributions/MED/report.md); [NET-C06](../contributions/NET/report.md).

**Access countercase.** The 1498 voyage account records coercive pressure in obtaining a pilot, without establishing the pilot’s consent or pay. Hōkūleʻa teaching and voyaging also involved volunteer time and selected access to mentors. Expertise can be useful while control and compensation remain unequal or unmeasured. [NET-K24](../contributions/NET/report.md); [OCE-C01](../contributions/OCE/report.md).

The Malawi communication experiments add a direct distinction between appointing communicators and supporting the work through incentives and contacts. They do not establish a generally remunerated teaching market. ORT transmission can become informal or intergenerational, which is an adverse case for a durable paid niche. [PSY-C03](../cases/PSY-C03.md); [MED-C07](../cases/MED-C07.md).

**Proposed analogue.** Teaching, adaptation, demonstration and interpretation may matter when users need to apply a capability to unfamiliar or consequential situations. Useful work may include designing understandable workflows and evaluating what users can actually do, rather than merely delivering information.

**Boundary conditions.** There must be a genuine learning or interpretation constraint. People may instead lack money, time, authority or a working product; calling those deficits a training problem would misdiagnose adoption.

**What breaks the analogy.** Better interfaces or reliable automated assistance may make the skill unnecessary. Free instruction can also remove commercial scarcity while leaving large social value. Institutional exclusion can prevent trained people from acting.

**Next evidence.** Test competent performance and retention, not attendance or satisfaction alone. Compare instruction with changes in access, incentives and interface design. Identify who pays and who receives the improvement.

## Candidate C — Coordinating shared resources and compatible systems

**Historical basis.** Irrigation calendars, water/sewerage, printing distribution, cash-agent liquidity and container interfaces involve dependencies across actors. They require different arrangements, not one universal network effect. [AGR-C02](../contributions/AGR/report.md); [MED-C04](../contributions/MED/report.md); [NET-C04](../contributions/NET/report.md); [DIG-C05](../contributions/DIG/report.md); [MOB-C06](../contributions/MOB/report.md).

**Power countercase.** Jahaly-Pacharr management could withdraw plot access after credit arrears, while household heads could separately control crop returns. Tae Rak restoration balanced water access with neighbouring farm interests and left one complex outside expected hydraulic access. Coordination can resolve a shared problem while constraining particular participants. [AGR-K25](../contributions/AGR/report.md); [OCE-C02](../contributions/OCE/report.md).

Ghanaian roads add local initiative alongside unequal land/asset access and contested labour obligations. Owner-driver entry and hire-purchase show a route into enterprise, without measuring debt-adjusted returns or a transition for displaced carriers. [GHA-C01–C03](../contributions/GHA/report.md).

**Proposed analogue.** People or organisations may create value by establishing compatible rules, resolving disputes, aligning supply, maintaining shared records or arranging credible commitments where no participant can solve the problem alone.

**Boundary conditions.** Coordination must improve a specified outcome relative to feasible alternatives; authority, accountability and benefit sharing matter. A broker who merely controls access may capture rents without adding comparable productive value.

**What breaks the analogy.** Open standards, platforms or automated contracting may simplify coordination. Alternatively, monopoly or political power may prevent an otherwise useful arrangement. More coordination can impose exclusion or surveillance costs.

**Next evidence.** Identify the blocked transaction or shared resource, each actor's incentives, the proposed rule and the alternatives. Compare adoption and distribution before and after a credible rule change. Include those who cannot refuse.

## Candidate D — Maintaining, repairing and adapting an installed base

**Historical basis.** Ancient hydraulic upkeep, long-lived material practices, specialised electric fleets and hybrid textile systems show that useful work need not centre on the newest invention. [ANC-C03–C05](../contributions/ANC/report.md); [MAT-C02, MAT-C05](../contributions/MAT/report.md); [MOB-C05](../contributions/MOB/report.md); [IND-C07](../contributions/IND/report.md).

**Access and quality countercases.** Tanzanian garage histories connect practical learning and independent work to access to mentors, tools, customers and ownership; selected successful careers do not measure entry rates or average returns. Appliance testimony records restored service but supplies no repair survival or safety comparison. Telephone maintenance’s gendered entry supplies another direct limit to treating useful repair as an accessible transition. [SKL-C04–C05](../contributions/SKL/report.md); [LBR-K08](../contributions/LBR/report.md).

The post-1949 Guanzhong contrast is sharper: an old task persisted partly through constrained access and unpaid women’s work. That is evidence of continuing production, not a desirable independent occupation. Material usefulness and social gains also remain in the record. [CTX-C01](../cases/CTX-C01.md).

**Proposed analogue.** Repair, refurbishment, retrofitting and local adaptation may remain valuable where replacement is costly, users have varied constraints and equipment lasts longer than its initial commercial fashion.

**Boundary conditions.** Repair must be technically possible, lawful, supplied with parts/information and worthwhile over the remaining service life. The owner must have a reason to pay. Some repair work is hazardous, precarious or poorly compensated despite being useful.

**What breaks the analogy.** Cheap modular replacement, inaccessible components, software restrictions or improved reliability may reduce independent repair. A shrinking installed base may eliminate demand. Environmental value alone does not guarantee a viable business model.

**Next evidence.** Study the installed stock, failure rates, parts access, downtime value, replacement alternatives, training and margins. Do not infer demand from the number of devices alone.

## Candidate E — Defining the right task and measuring the right outcome

**Historical basis.** Material trade-offs, crop yield versus labour, household device access versus a child's use, and installation versus service show how the wrong denominator can lead to the wrong intervention. Source audits also expose misleading headlines and observational limits. [MAT-C05](../contributions/MAT/report.md); [AGR-C01](../contributions/AGR/report.md); [DIG-C04](../contributions/DIG/report.md); [MED-C04, MED-C07](../contributions/MED/report.md).

**Evidence limit.** These histories establish consequential measurement distinctions. They do not observe a labour market rewarding independent evaluators, nor show that organisations acted on better measures. The occupational inference therefore begins with an unmeasured access and value-capture margin. [SYN-K17](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#syn-k17).

**Proposed analogue.** Domain-informed problem definition, evaluation and measurement may create value where cheap technical output makes it easier to optimise the wrong target. This includes identifying which users and harms a metric excludes.

**Boundary conditions.** The evaluator needs independent access to outcomes and incentives to report inconvenient results. A better metric matters only if decision makers can and will act on it.

**What breaks the analogy.** Evaluation may itself be automated or standardised. Organisations may reward appearance rather than real performance. Measurement can also become intrusive or costly without improving decisions.

**Next evidence.** Compare decisions made under alternative outcome definitions; examine errors caught, action taken and realised value. Test whether the service changes behaviour or merely produces reports.

## What the historical evidence actually observes

“Useful task” below does not mean universal physical necessity or a demonstrated future human advantage. Unknowns are part of the result.

| Candidate | Task and people observed | Voluntary access and control | Remuneration/value capture | Actual transition evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: reliability | Operators, engineers and experimenters supply different process/service tasks | Access/authority vary; modern replicas are not ancient worker observations | No representative pay or ownership return established by the cited production cases | Telephone comparison warns that complementary work did not absorb the relevant female cohorts; no general transition route measured |
| B: competent use | Navigators, teachers, caregivers and volunteer crew | Pilot episode includes coercive pressure; voyaging requires time and mentor access; consent cannot be assumed | Paid, unpaid and unmeasured work must remain separate; no common return measured | No representative career transition in these examples |
| C: coordination | Managers, water users, health authorities, cash agents and interface organisations | Sanctions, vetoes and resource rights differ; authority may benefit while nonchoosers bear costs | Some contractual flows are documented; net participant gains and coordinator earnings not pooled | No general individual transition estimate |
| D: repair/adaptation | Mechanics, technicians, operators and household repair users | Training, tools, parts, permission and gendered entry constrain access | Selected garage exchanges and ownership histories; no average secure wage or business margin | Reported freelance moves lack a matched before/after livelihood panel; telephone cohort result is separate |
| E: evaluation | Studies and audits reveal how measures change interpretations | Evaluator independence, organisational authority and practical entry unmeasured | No inspected evidence of reward for this proposed role | No occupational transition established |

A candidate advances beyond a question only after contemporary evidence clears its gate: an independently measured unresolved problem; a human contribution compared with feasible automated or organisational alternatives; practical access and authority; an identifiable payer or explicit unpaid social purpose; and a plausible way for the contributor to retain value. None of the five candidates has passed that contemporary test in this historical edition.

## How to turn a historical analogy into a credible opportunity study

For one proposed role, record:

1. **Task and beneficiary:** the concrete outcome, current alternative and person or institution that values improvement.
2. **Binding constraint:** evidence that the problem remains after readily available technical and institutional options are considered.
3. **Human contribution:** what a person adds, and why a cheaper automated, standardised or organisational alternative is insufficient under stated conditions.
4. **Entry and transition:** skills, credentials, assets, relationships, time, lost income and exclusion risks; distinguish an entrant from an incumbent who must retrain.
5. **Value capture:** who owns the assets and customer relationship, who sets prices, and how competition or bargaining affects compensation.
6. **Durability and falsifier:** what observable change would erode the role, and the evidence that would cause the proposal to be abandoned.
7. **Social account:** harms, unpaid work and affected people without a choice, separately from private profitability.

These questions can justify a targeted empirical study. They cannot produce a reliable occupational forecast by deduction alone. A later edition should investigate actual contemporary tasks and markets once the historical programme has narrowed which analogies are worth testing.
