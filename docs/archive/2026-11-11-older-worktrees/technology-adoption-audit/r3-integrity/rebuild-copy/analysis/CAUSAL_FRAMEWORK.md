# A testable framework for adoption

Status: working model, 5 November 2026. This is an analytical proposal to organise evidence, not a historical law or an estimated predictive model. Case-specific tests will be added after the independent investigations.

## Start with actors and an actual task

A technology changes a feasible set of actions. It does not supply its own purpose, buyers, institutions or maintenance. Define a task, the people affected, the decision maker, the available alternatives and the time horizon. A household cooking a meal, a state equipping an army and a port operator moving containers face different objectives and constraints. Even within one household or firm, the person paying may not be the person using or benefiting.

The initial primitives are limited resources and time; physical performance and reliability; learned capabilities; rules and decision rights; information and beliefs; relationships with other users; and a distribution of benefits and harms. These can change. No premise requires people to prefer novelty, progress, convenience or safety in every choice. An institution may create demand for a previously unimportant task, make a technology compulsory, redistribute its gains, or change the alternatives against which it is judged.

Specify at least four actors when they differ: chooser, payer, operator/maintainer and affected non-chooser. For each, record the objective or plausible incentive and its evidential basis. An authority's procurement decision does not reveal citizens' preferences. A purchase does not establish satisfaction, sustained use, profit or social benefit.

## Distinguish a working object from a working system

Technical feasibility is conditional: a device produces an output within a range of inputs, tolerances, environmental conditions and operating skill. A successful demonstration establishes only that range. Reliable deployment also requires repeatable manufacture, supply, installation, operation and recovery from failure. Scaling can expose constraints absent at demonstration scale: scarce inputs, quality variation, bottleneck suppliers, training time, finance, waste disposal or coordination.

For a particular adopter and task, define the **effective service** as delivered useful output after downtime, defects and unusable output. Define the **whole-use burden** as acquisition, financing, infrastructure, consumables, operation, learning, coordination, maintenance, repair, disposal and expected losses. These are accounting categories, not quantities that can always be collapsed into a single monetary price. Time, dignity, autonomy, bodily comfort and risk can remain separate dimensions; their weights require evidence.

A lower purchase price can coexist with a higher whole-use burden. Greater peak performance can coexist with less effective service for a specific task. Conversely, a costly or hazardous technology can spread where its chooser captures benefits and others bear costs. The framework therefore does not predict adoption from a universal ranking of gadgets.

## A compact decision representation

Let x be an available technology-system alternative, a an actor, t a time and s a state of the world. Let F(a,t) contain the alternatives that the actor can access and is permitted and able to use. Let B and C denote actor-specific expected benefits and burdens, including non-monetary dimensions; let I encode information and beliefs. A voluntary choice can be studied as a comparison of expected consequences over F, subject to budget, time, risk and coordination constraints.

This is a bookkeeping representation, not a claim of perfect rationality. It becomes circular if B and C are inferred entirely from the observed choice. To test it, measure constraints or beliefs independently, examine choices when one changes, and compare with a rival prediction. Under coercion, model the authority's decision and the compelled user's response separately; do not stretch “utility” to erase power. Under habit or uncertainty, adoption, trial, continued use and abandonment can follow different processes.

An intervention may change access without changing continued use, or change beliefs without changing feasible options. These differences generate discriminating tests. Providing a device, lowering its price, supplying repair and changing decision rights are different interventions.

## Stages and margins

The ledger separates invention, reproducible feasibility, operational deployment, diffusion, scaling, non-adoption/niche and displacement. A stage may recur after a redesign or institutional change. These are analytical distinctions, not mandatory steps followed once in a fixed order.

Measure at least: availability; initial trial; installed base; active users; intensity of use; retention; and useful outcomes, when records permit. For state or industrial systems, capacity and delivered service may matter more than user counts. Aggregate adoption curves conceal composition, geography, inequality and coexistence. Comin and Mestieri explicitly separate estimated adoption timing from intensity of use; their historical growth results depend on a model and a selected technology sample, rather than identifying every cause of diffusion (SYN-S02, introduction and §4.1). [Author manuscript](https://dcomin.host.dartmouth.edu/Publications_files/CM_rev_71917.pdf).

## Mechanisms must distinguish outcomes

| Candidate mechanism | Independently observable implication | Rival or potential disconfirmation |
|---|---|---|
| Binding material/energy constraint | Uptake changes after a supply, quality or energy-access change, especially where the input was scarce | Demand or policy changed simultaneously; successful use with substitutes weakens a necessity claim |
| Whole-use cost or liquidity | Comparable adopters respond differently to operating-cost reduction versus financing/payment timing | The intervention also signals quality or changes distribution; no response where cost allegedly binds |
| Learning and tacit skill | Reliability or productive use changes with training, experience or local skilled support | Better-trained users were selected; equipment quality changed; classroom instruction does not transfer |
| Maintenance and supply | Retention/downtime differs with spare parts, repair capability and recurring supply | Repair follows demand rather than causes it; initial poor design explains both |
| Complementarity | On a declared outcome scale, the marginal benefit of one input is greater when another is present; compare both separate effects and the interaction | Additive effects, selection or a bundled intervention can explain joint success; substitutes and bounded necessity are separate questions |
| Coordination/network effects | Value depends on compatible adopters, reachable partners or standards; staged rollout changes who benefits | Common demand growth or infrastructure explains correlated adoption |
| Institutional power and distribution | Uptake follows procurement, mandates, enclosure, labour discipline or altered rights; benefits/harms differ by actor | Voluntary alternatives also spread; the policy follows rather than creates demand |
| Trust, beliefs and social learning | Information, trusted intermediaries or demonstrable experience changes trial or use while material access is held stable | Information also changes access; socially connected people face common constraints |
| Status, identity and habit | Behaviour varies with audience, group meaning, routine or changed norms beyond functional differences | Income, task mix or availability explain the apparent preference |
| Task and bodily fit | Adoption/retention differs across tasks, settings and physical capabilities even with similar price/performance | Accessibility or content supply changed simultaneously; preference is being inferred from uptake alone |

The list is deliberately plural but cannot be used as an after-the-fact explanation that always wins. Each case should identify which mechanism is actually supported, what remains unmeasured, and which plausible explanation would predict a different result. A case may be underdetermined.

## Complementarity needs a boundary

“Everything needs complements” is too broad to be useful. Name the complement, specify its availability and a plausible threshold, and explain the task-level interaction. Treat **necessary under stated conditions**, **helpful**, **one of several substitutes**, and **co-occurring** as different propositions. The graph records scoped enabling, dependence, competition, displacement and constraint relations; a path through the graph is not proof of historical causation.

Historical change also alters incumbent systems. Improved old technologies, hybrid arrangements, reused machinery and repair can outcompete a new system for particular populations. Edgerton's use-centred approach motivates looking at persistence, coexistence and mundane operation rather than taking invention dates as the sole chronology (SYN-S01, introduction pp. xi–xv). [Inspected excerpt](https://sidoli.w.waseda.jp/Edgerton_2008_01.pdf).

## Worked interaction test, and a non-example

Fix an actor/population, outcome, time window and measurement scale before testing complementarity. Let Yab be the expected outcome under inputs A=a and B=b, where each input is0 or1. For an outcome where higher is beneficial, the interaction contrast is:

`I = (Y11 − Y01) − (Y10 − Y00)`

Positive I means A has a larger marginal benefit when B is present. The equivalent expression is Y11−Y10−Y01+Y00. This is a causal contrast only under a design that supports those counterfactual comparisons; a four-cell association is insufficient. A log scale and a level scale need not give the same interaction. If the outcome is harmful, such as mortality, the favourable sign is reversed.

An invented additive example makes the distinction clear: Y00=0, Y10=2, Y01=3 and Y11=5 gives I=0. Joint output5 is unavailable from either input alone, yet there is no interaction. Neither an impressive bundle nor an input appearing in every successful case establishes complementarity. A bounded physical necessity is another proposition: a specified output cannot occur without an input or one of its stated substitutes.

**Historical worked example.** Alsan–Goldin’s Massachusetts model,1880–1920, includes water, sewerage and their interaction before measured mortality outcomes, with municipal/year controls. In Table2,column5, the estimated interaction on log under-five mortality is−0.307 (standard error0.106). On the beneficial scale−log mortality this corresponds to+0.307: the estimated marginal health benefit is greater with the other intervention. This result is conditional on rollout, common-trend and modelling assumptions; it neither measures a universal interaction nor explains why municipalities adopted. The paper’s unit and timeline matter. [MED-K09; original equation1,p.597 and Table2,p.605](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.harvard.edu/dist/e/935/files/2025/11/alsangoldin_jpe.pdf).

**Unidentified-component example.** The Odisha stove offer included training and village promoters; later rebuilding was not separately randomized. A package result and changing stove condition do not supply the four input contrasts needed to identify an interaction between hardware and maintenance. Task fit may affect both use and upkeep. Keep the package effect separate from those unresolved mediators and define adequate support independently of the outcome. [AGR-K18; final article pp.86–87,106–108](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/52%20Up%20in%20Smoke%20AEJ2016.pdf).

Historical process evidence can establish a narrower physical contribution without this statistical design. Its graph basis must say so. A complement in producing health, profit or output is not automatically a cause of trial, continued use or investment: that requires a separate chooser-level comparison.

## What would count as failure of this framework?

A framework is useful only if it makes evidence more discriminating. It fails operationally if every case receives the same checklist without bounded causal claims; if its labels cannot distinguish adoption from abandonment; if unobserved preferences explain every contradiction; or if it hides whose welfare is being assessed. It also fails if the graph only redescribes chronology, or if well-documented rival histories can reverse its central conclusions without changing its confidence labels.

The programme will retain negative cases, disconfirming observations and explicit unresolved disputes. Broad recurrence can justify a research question or conditional proposition. It cannot by itself establish a universal law, estimate an effect size or forecast which future occupation will prosper.
