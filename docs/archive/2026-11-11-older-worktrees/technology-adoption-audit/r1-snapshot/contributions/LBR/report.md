# Labour transitions: what adoption histories cannot tell us by themselves

This supplementary investigation was conducted by the coordinating researcher to address the gap between system adoption and the user's interest in future human roles. It provides two bounded US cases, not a general history of work. The evidence does not justify either inevitable mass unemployment or automatic opportunity for displaced people.

## LBR-C01 — Telephone switching and different worker cohorts

The adoption question and the livelihood question require different evidence. Feigenbaum and Gross attribute delayed AT&T automation to interdependent service organisation and market scale, despite much earlier switching invention. Here that explanation has abstract-level support only; its internal archival tests have not been independently audited (LBR-K01). [Adoption study](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2022.01760).

Their separate labour study compares local cutovers, later young female cohorts and linked incumbent operators. Incumbents experienced adverse decade-later employment or occupational outcomes while later cohorts found other clerical/service work. The main cohort estimates concern white American-born women aged 16–25, principally in smaller cities; the linked incumbent sample is separate and selective. A null wage result cannot establish unchanged pay: the authors acknowledge limited power. These qualifications matter more here than a headline net-employment number (LBR-K02–K04). [Labour study, §§III, VI–VII](https://www.dpgross.com/papers/answering-call-automation.pdf).

The programme's inference is that opportunity must be evaluated at the transition level: which person can enter which role, with what skills, delay, lost income and bargaining power? A firm's successful deployment and a later cohort's employment resilience answer different questions. The historical comparison does not identify how a present individual should retrain.

## LBR-C02 — New work is measurable, but its units matter

Autor and colleagues track US job-title emergence over 1940–2018 and compare innovation/demand exposures. Their often-repeated roughly 60% figure is an estimated employment share in titles added since 1940. Workers in the new micro-titles are not directly counted; the estimate uses title shares within occupations. It should not become “60% of occupations were invented” or “technology caused 60% of jobs.” Patent categories also overlap and omit some innovation (LBR-K05–K06). [Study, §II and Tables IV/V](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2026-04/New-Frontiers-QJE-v139%283%29-p1399-1465-Aug-2024.pdf).

The useful question is whether a technology changes the tasks people can productively perform and whether there is demand for their output. That differs from asking whether an occupation's name is new. Even a real new task need not be accessible to displaced workers or yield them a large share of its value.

## A limited bridge to future-role research

Task models separate displacement, productivity effects and the creation of new labour tasks. Those channels can have different signs; productivity improvement alone does not settle labour demand. This is a model distinction, not a guarantee about future adjustment (LBR-K07). [Acemoglu and Restrepo, introduction](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/Automation%20and%20New%20Tasks%20-%20How%20Technology%20Displace.pdf).

For each proposed future role, the next investigation should therefore identify the remaining task, paying demand, access requirements, ownership and ability to capture value. It should examine a credible substitution scenario as well as a complementarity scenario. Historical need for maintenance, coordination or trust is a reason to investigate those tasks; it does not prove they remain human, scarce or well paid.

## Unresolved limits

These studies share US census traditions and related task-based theory. They do not independently confirm a global law. The programme has not replicated their datasets or resolved every identification assumption. Unpaid work, excluded groups, ownership transitions and comparative labour institutions need separate research. The structured actor/labour fields preserve these unknowns instead of filling them from aggregate outcomes.
