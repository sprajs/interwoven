# Ghana: making transport capacity, entering it, and supplying its labour

This targeted contribution responds to review P2-SP04. It adds three bounded episodes to the railway account in MOB-C04: southern cocoa roads and lorry enterprise; changing northern public-road employment; and northern farmers' feeder roads. These are connected histories with different populations and evidence. The comparison tests whether performing a transport function implies a particular owner, financing method or labour relation. It does not test which mode produced the greatest Ghana-wide welfare gain.

Five sources were inspected. Austin and Heap connect the southern transport system to land, enterprise and labour; Kunkel and Pont Cháfer disagree about the interpretation and extent of northern labour transitions; Jedwab and Moradi supply the existing spatial comparison. The last paper directly cites Austin and shares Hill's historical scholarship. Reopening those works adds actor detail and tests narrative scope; it is not an independent replication of the railway estimates.

## GHA-C01 — Southern cocoa transport and unequal routes into enterprise

The choice set included head-loading, rail, road haulage and combinations of them. Austin reports that road and rail were largely complementary until the mid-1920s: lorries could collect cocoa for relatively cheap railway long hauls. Later lorries competed for coastal traffic. His historical series puts rail's share of cocoa exports above 83 percent in 1922–1923 and at 58 percent in 1930–1931, while head-loading commonly remained between farms and local markets. This is a task-specific rearrangement, not instantaneous replacement of an entire transport system. **GHA-K02.** [Austin, manuscript pp. 5–7](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/4392/1/Labour_and_land_in_Ghana_(LSERO_version).pdf).

Africans helped create the road alternative. Heap recounts the Kwahu ruler collecting funds and hiring a contractor for the scarp road, and a chief working with a district commissioner on the Begoro–Bosuso station connection. These are concrete initiatives involving African resources and decisions. Their official narrators do not establish that every contributor or landholder consented. **GHA-K01.** [Heap, pp. 29–31](https://www.academia.edu/80974990/The_Development_of_Motor_Transport_in_the_Gold_Coast_1900_39).

The organization of haulage also varied. Austin records chiefs' early fleets and a 1927–1928 administrative account attributing larger firms' retreat to African owner-drivers. Heap describes hire-purchase and long working days. Together these support actual entry through smaller enterprises, with a plausible overhead advantage; they do not identify its causal contribution against vehicle design, road improvements, subsidies or selective reporting. Hire-purchase could lower the initial payment without eliminating debt risk. Contract terms, defaults, unpaid household support and drivers' net hourly returns are missing. **GHA-K03.** [Austin, p. 7](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/4392/1/Labour_and_land_in_Ghana_(LSERO_version).pdf); [Heap, pp. 34–35](https://www.academia.edu/80974990/The_Development_of_Motor_Transport_in_the_Gold_Coast_1900_39).

Access to the crop income behind this economy was institutionally differentiated. Austin describes migrant groups organizing land purchases in Akyem Abuakwa, often with extended credit, while most Asante chiefs preferred leases. Political authority and colonial interventions shaped who could charge for land and the form of payment. A person categorized locally as a stranger could owe rent even when an Asante elsewhere. A generic autonomous farmer would conceal these differences. The contrast supports institutional mediation, but does not isolate political organization from land availability, timing or location. **GHA-K04.** [Austin, pp. 15–17](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/4392/1/Labour_and_land_in_Ghana_(LSERO_version).pdf).

Nor was the surrounding labour system uniformly free. Austin argues that enslaved people, pawns and chiefly corvée contributed to early colonial Ashanti cocoa planting; returns from the resulting farms subsequently helped finance hired labour and farm pledging. Legal abolition and changing economic alternatives were distinct processes. Hired migrants from the savanna had different land opportunities from forest landholders, and improved opportunities after slavery and pawning were strongly unequal by gender. Compulsory road work also persisted alongside expanding wage employment. These findings preclude reading successful African enterprise as proof of equal entry or voluntary work throughout its supply system. The mechanism linking crop revenue to hiring remains historical interpretation, not an isolated estimate that cocoa caused emancipation. **GHA-K05.** [Austin, pp. 17–20, especially p. 18 n. 104](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/4392/1/Labour_and_land_in_Ghana_(LSERO_version).pdf).

## GHA-C02 — Northern public roads: payment, recruitment and refusal

Pont Cháfer's study distinguishes Public Works Department roads from the much larger network managed through political authorities. At Makongo, chiefs protested wages and contract duration in 1925, and commissioners supported higher pay and shorter engagements. A June 1930 Savelugu diary describes advertising paid PWD work while rejecting the demand that the chief supply compelled workers. These observations precede Britain's 1931 ratification of the forced-labour convention. The chronology separates earlier local adjustment from later ratification; neither episode identifies the independent influence of international pressure or anticipatory policy change. **GHA-K06.** [Pont Cháfer, “The Transition from Paid Forced Labour to Paid Voluntary Labour on PWD Roads”](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0020859022000657).

The proposed mechanism combines workers' alternatives with the organization of maintenance. Increasing motor traffic and wet-season repairs put pressure on a system of short compulsory services. Higher wages could attract workers, while the Depression's reduction of southern cocoa wages subsequently made northern PWD offers more attractive. This is not a simple story of benevolent reform or rising wages everywhere. A worker could gain freedom from recruitment while facing deteriorating alternatives. Pont Cháfer also explicitly retains compulsory weeding and cleaning on PWD roads after the broader recruitment change. **GHA-K07.** [Pont Cháfer, same section, notes 50–66](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0020859022000657).

The extent of the transition on other roads remains contested. Kunkel documents institutional arrangements that preserved compulsion, including a mid-1930s subsidy calculation that assumed half of maintenance labour would be unpaid. She also records official dissent and local initiatives to pay workers. Pont Cháfer gives greater weight to earlier disagreements, district payments, larger grants and reduced maintained mileage by the later 1930s. A planned subsidy, a recorded wage payment and an individual's freedom to decline are different observations. They cannot be pooled into a single rate of forced labour. **GHA-K08.** [Kunkel, pp. 464–471](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0020859018000524); [Pont Cháfer, “Indirect Rule, Taxation, and Unpaid Forced Labour”](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0020859022000657).

This comparison supplies an alternative way to perform road maintenance, not an endorsement of any regime. The road itself did not specify whether workers would be compelled, hired at an acceptable offer, or obligated to perform a residual task. Resolving the scale of change needs road-level payrolls, recruitment instructions, sanctions and actual worker accounts—not simply a change in administrative terminology.

## GHA-C03 — Farmers' feeder roads and maintenance conditional on use

In the Krachi–Salaga–Kpandai area, Pont Cháfer reconstructs another configuration. Bajamse villagers requested help aligning a connection in 1928. Later village initiatives sought bridges, tools or grants in return for road labour; the account records grants after particular connections had been made in 1937. Farmers supplied knowledge and work, while expensive materials and skilled construction could remain outside their means. Their requests distinguish locally initiated demand from an assumption that every road originated in a colonial office. They do not show that government assistance was unnecessary. **GHA-K09.** [Pont Cháfer, “People's Roads and Unpaid Voluntary Labour,” notes 100–109](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0020859022000657).

The same account reports that the Bladjai road deteriorated after fuel shortages stopped exports in April 1945. That sequence is consistent with maintenance responding to the usefulness of lorry access. It is not a measured causal effect: labour availability, weather and funding are not held constant. Nor does it establish that every worker was volunteering, or that every village shared the same interests. The source combines contemporary administrative records with much later interviews, and the strength of those forms of evidence differs. **GHA-K10.** [Pont Cháfer, same section, notes 110–113](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0020859022000657).

Taken together, the northern cases make two classifications unsafe. Payment does not by itself show that entry was voluntary; unpaid work does not by itself show compulsion. The consequential questions concern recruitment, sanctions, whose purpose the work served, and the feasible option to stop. The cases provide observations bearing on those questions, not a universal answer for communal labour.

## Comparison with MOB-C04 and implications for the programme

Jedwab and Moradi examine spatial effects of colonial rail and later persistence, chiefly in cocoa-suitable southern cells. GHA-C01 adds actors who financed, constructed and operated another transport configuration. GHA-C02 and GHA-C03 investigate labour relations outside that main geographical sample. None is a matched replacement for the rail study's counterfactual. Rail access can matter while farmers make feeder roads, owners compete for loads and workers resist recruitment. **GHA-K11; GHA-X02.** [Jedwab and Moradi, January 2015, §4.1 printed pp. 11–12 and §5.1.3 printed p. 18, plus references](https://alexandermoradi.org/research/rail.pdf).

The historical contextual series also need care. Austin's 1930–1931 rail share and Jedwab–Moradi's broad pre-1970 rail-share summary do not align transparently; their early lorry descriptions differ too. No combined diffusion curve is constructed here. Source definitions and the underlying reports need reconciliation before using either discrepancy to challenge an estimate.

For future-role research, these cases support a demanding evidence gate: establishing that driving, maintenance or local coordination contributes to a system is insufficient. Study who can obtain the asset or contract, who supplies uncompensated time, whether workers can decline, and how revenue is divided after debts and upkeep. Nothing inspected follows a cohort of displaced carriers into better work or measures a complete welfare balance. The contribution therefore adds mechanisms and discriminating questions, not occupational predictions.
