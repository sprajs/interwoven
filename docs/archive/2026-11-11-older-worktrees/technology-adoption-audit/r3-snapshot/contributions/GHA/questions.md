# GHA: unresolved questions and discriminating next evidence

This supplement contains three purposively selected episodes, not a representative history of Ghanaian transport or labour. Five source records support eleven claims. Three complete PDFs were acquired and relevant sections inspected; two sources are conservatively labelled partial-text because the inspected article text came through web extraction and direct downloads failed. Original archival records and interview recordings remain mediated through their scholarly authors.

1. **Who could become an owner-driver?** Reconstruct hire-purchase deposits, repayment terms, repossessions, defaults, vehicle age, repair expenditure, route choices and owner/family hours. Compare failures as well as surviving operators. A lower initial payment is not proof of affordable total cost; low cash overhead can coexist with poor hourly earnings. The inspected scholarship establishes arrangements and contemporary success attributions, not a representative return or causal business-model advantage.

2. **Who supplied a locally initiated road?** For the Kwahu and Begoro–Bosuso examples, inspect collection accounts, contractor records, labour recruitment and compensation/land disputes. A chief's initiative and a governor's praise do not identify each payer's or worker's ability to refuse. Distinguish village proposals, compulsory chiefly service, hired labour and independent contractual work, including mixed configurations on the same route.

3. **Land credit is not vehicle credit.** Austin's group purchases and extended credit concern land acquisition. Do not infer particular lorry loan terms from those transactions. A useful linked history would follow households from land/crop returns to transport investment, separating chiefs, migrant buyers, renters, drivers and hired workers. Existing sources do not provide that linked cohort.

4. **Which parts of the labour transition arose locally?** The 1925 Makongo and June 1930 Savelugu accounts precede Britain's 1931 convention ratification. To identify relative causes, inspect correspondence about earlier international pressure, anticipated legislation, local protests, wages and recruitment practice. Pre-ratification timing is not proof that international influence was absent. Compare changes in paid recruitment with residual obligatory weeding and cleaning, rather than assigning the whole road one consent label.

5. **Reconcile the political-road interpretations at the same unit.** Kunkel's mid-1930s subsidy calculation assumes a substantial unpaid contribution; Pont Cháfer supplies district spending, grants and paid-gang examples. Link planned funds to actual disbursement, work days, recruitment orders, complaints and sanctions on the same roads. Payment alone does not demonstrate voluntary acceptance. The sources differ in analytical emphasis, geography and observation type, and both acknowledge heterogeneity; neither is fairly represented as asserting that every worker was coerced or every worker free.

6. **Test conditional maintenance at Bladjai.** The source links April 1945 fuel shortages, stopped exports and deteriorating road condition. Obtain traffic, maintenance, rainfall, labour and finance records for Bladjai and comparable routes. A fuel shock could alter the incentive to maintain the route, but wartime shortages might also reduce capacity to work. The qualitative sequence does not identify an elasticity or prove the consent of all previous workers.

7. **Separate contemporary records from later oral evidence.** Pont Cháfer's method describes unstructured interviews in 2014–2016, many concerning late colonial or independence-era experiences. These enrich interpretation but are not direct 1928 observations. Follow-up should retain the speaker, event date, recall conditions and archival cross-check. Claims here about 1925/1930 recruitment and 1928/1937/1945 roads are tied to the article's cited contemporary records.

8. **Reconcile historical modal summaries before graphing them.** Austin pp. 6–7 gives rail cocoa-export shares above 83 percent in 1922–1923 and 58 percent in 1930–1931, inherited from Cardinall. Jedwab–Moradi's January 2015 manuscript p. 7 says rail accounted for more than 70 percent of cocoa transport until 1970. Its footnote 6 also says there were only two lorries in 1914, whereas Austin/Heap describe imports and operation before and during the war. These statements do not align transparently; definitions, geographical coverage, dates, original tables and possible transcription errors need checking. No combined series, error attribution or inference that the spatial estimates are invalid is made here. The report retains the exact version of the rail manuscript being compared.

9. **Track people, not only functions.** Did displaced head-loaders become drivers, mechanics or road workers? At what cost, after how long, and with which gender, status, credit and land barriers? Head-loading continued in some stages. Neither a surviving old task nor an expanding new occupation establishes good transitions for an individual. No linked worker cohort or net welfare account was found in the inspected material.

## Selection and evidence boundaries

The supplement deliberately adds local infrastructure initiative and contested labour organization to a railway-centred comparison. It overrepresents publicly documented projects and disputes. Unrecorded maintenance, failed ventures, denied requests, people who avoided authorities, household work, women and poorer nonowners are underrepresented. Northern and southern cases are connected through migration and markets but differ in ecology, crops, finance, administrative regime and timing. They cannot be treated as matched controls or as evidence of Ghana-wide mechanism prevalence.

Austin's transport passage cites Heap and Wrangham, and the rail paper itself cites Austin and Hill. Repeated descriptions of the same historical episode are not independent corroboration. Kunkel and Pont Cháfer share historiography and a colonial administrative context, while Pont Cháfer adds specific district evidence and fieldwork; exact archive overlap remains incompletely reconstructed.

Wrangham's 2004 article and Akurang-Parry's 2000 article were located as leads, but their full texts were not inspected. They are not promoted to direct claim support. Their content appears only as explicitly mediated references within the inspected scholarship. No contact, request to an author, purchase, publication or remote push was performed.
