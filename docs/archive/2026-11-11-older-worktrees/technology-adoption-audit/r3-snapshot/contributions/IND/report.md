# Industrial systems: power, production and organisation, c. 1500–1950

Research contribution IND; inspected 5 November 2026. Eight purposively selected cases; no claim that their frequency represents industrial history. Case records distinguish invention, feasibility, deployment, diffusion, scaling, non-adoption and displacement. “Unresolved” means that the inspected evidence does not establish that stage. The principal finding is that adoption changed whole production arrangements: the relevant comparison was rarely an isolated machine against an unchanged human worker.

This investigation reads sixteen sources: twelve full texts with selected relevant sections inspected, three partial texts, and one abstract. The partial sources are Allen's published chapter excerpt, Ure's primary-source extract and retrieved sections of Jonell and colleagues' article; Malm's reservoir argument was accessible only as its author-repository abstract. Several full texts are working papers, identified by their actual edition. Contemporary testimony quoted within scholarship has not silently become independently inspected primary evidence. The ledgers preserve those distinctions.

## IND-C01 — Central European mine pumping, c. 1550–1700

The contemporary choice was among human and animal hauling, water-driven pumps, drainage works and foregoing extraction. Agricola's *De re metallica* describes several choices together. He differentiates pumping arrangements by water volume, describes replaceable gear teeth, and explains staged pumps driven by a diverted stream. Elsewhere he explicitly makes water access, transport expense, provisioning and political security considerations in mine location. These are documented considerations in a technical treatise, not measured elasticities of investment. They show that repairability and supporting infrastructure belonged to the practical system before steam. [IND-K01; Agricola, Books II and VI, pp. 31–33, 171–185](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/38015/38015-h/38015-h.htm).

Multhauf's comparison of technical descriptions and mining images identifies a subsequent change: reciprocating rod transmission connected pumps to distant prime movers, and balanced rods reduced the burden of moving the transmission itself. The stream did not have to reach the mine mouth. Seventeenth-century evidence therefore contradicts a static interval between Agricola and steam. His suggestion that adequate continental hydraulics discouraged steam adoption is expressly a hypothesis; his evidence cannot establish a German adoption rate or isolate geology from fuel prices. [IND-K02; Multhauf, pp. 117–120 and notes 13–22](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/31024/pg31024-images.html).

The distinction between feasibility and deployment matters here. A sufficiently detailed description makes a mechanism intelligible; it does not count working installations. Named installations and repeated later depictions strengthen the deployment inference, while their celebratory and selective character prevents treating the illustrated machine population as representative. A useful falsifier would be operating accounts showing rod systems technically workable but routinely abandoned because transmission maintenance exceeded animal pumping costs. Until then, “water lacked flexibility” needs a specified distance, terrain and maintenance regime. This case also makes abandonment of a mine a legitimate alternative to buying more power, rather than assuming that every profitable ore deposit must eventually be exploited.

## IND-C02 — British coalfield and Cornish steam pumping, 1712–1850

Newcomen's engine offered a workable drainage system within contemporary engineering capabilities, but consumed substantial fuel. Nuvolari contrasts coalfield use with Cornwall, where imported coal made fuel economy valuable. Watt's separate condenser changed that comparison; deployment in expensive-coal districts is therefore compatible with an initial cheap-coal niche. “Cheap energy caused steam” conflates the conditions supporting an early design with incentives to improve it. [IND-K03; Nuvolari, working-paper pp. 10–12](https://pure.tue.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/2343508/669221.pdf).

The same study describes royalties tied to fuel savings, conflict over Watt's patent and the subsequent reporting of engine performance in *Lean's Engine Reporter* from 1811. It interprets reporting, overlapping mine investments and engineers' reputations as supports for shared incremental improvement. Patent restriction and collective invention are interpretations grounded in a particular district, not universal estimates of intellectual property's effects. Patent expiry alone did not produce immediate improvement; the reported intervening slackness is counterevidence to that simple sequence. [IND-K04–K05; Nuvolari, pp. 12–20](https://pure.tue.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/2343508/669221.pdf).

The relevant investor needed more than an engine: finance for the house and associated works, engineering judgement, installation, fuel supply and sustained operation. Measured fuel performance could make an improvement comparable across otherwise competing installations. But published “duty” is not identical to annual mine profit: utilisation, repair, drainage depth and ore revenue could all change the business result. A stronger identification design would compare reporting and non-reporting districts while accounting for investment cycles, fuel prices and incoming engineers. It would also check whether the best engines, rather than typical machines, disproportionately shaped published impressions.

Nor should feasible steam pumping be equated with an immediate economy-wide transformation. Crafts's 2002 growth-accounting version estimates a very small direct steam contribution before 1830, rising with later deployment, including railways. That is a result conditional on reconstructed stocks, weights and the treatment of spillovers, not proof that steam was dispensable. [IND-K06; Crafts, pp. 13–19, table 3](https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/crafts.pdf). An enabling input may be essential at a particular mine while still having a small aggregate expenditure share.

## IND-C03 — Water, steam and unbuilt reservoir systems in British textiles, c. 1780–1850

Water and steam could compete, but they could also complement one another. Jonell and colleagues combine landscape reconstruction, historical climate, mill locations and factory returns. Their results indicate ample unused potential across much of Britain alongside constrained catchments around Greater Manchester. They also discuss engines supplementing waterpower or recirculating water. The scope is crucial: national theoretical potential does not tell a particular crowded mill district what dependable power it can obtain. Conversely, Manchester's constraints do not establish a nationwide shortage. [IND-K07; Jonell et al., “Impact on the British textile industry” and methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11249955/).

Malm's rival account emphasises reservoir schemes proposed in the 1820s–1830s and never realised. His abstract argues that inexpensive waterpower expansion required collective financing and coordination that competing manufacturers did not achieve. This supplies a serious alternative to technological inferiority, but the present investigation has not inspected his scheme-level calculations and therefore does not adopt “water was always cheaper” as a finding. [IND-K08; Malm, author-repository abstract](https://portal.research.lu.se/en/publications/fleeing-the-flowing-commons-robert-thom-water-reservoir-schemes-a/).

These explanations are not clean substitutes. A reservoir can change low-flow reliability, while rights and financing determine whether its physical potential becomes usable. Existing industrial concentration can itself create local scarcity. Choosing steam might preserve access to workers, customers and sunk buildings while paying more for fuel. That is a total-system comparison, not evidence that owners ignored cost. The graph accordingly includes both a competing-power edge and a supplementary-power edge; neither is a timeless technological prerequisite.

To discriminate among explanations, compare mills with similar products and labour access but different dependable flow, and planned reservoirs with different ownership arrangements. A hydrological argument weakens if reliably affordable unused water was available at the actual decision sites. A coordination argument weakens if schemes failed even after enforceable funding and water-sharing arrangements existed. Owner correspondence, reservoir engineering and seasonal production records would be more decisive than a comparison of national coal and water totals. This case retains non-deployment as an outcome: an unbuilt scheme cannot be counted as a demonstrated scalable alternative merely because its prospectus promised adequate power.

## IND-C04 — British spinning, machine making and factory control, c. 1760–1850

Allen's factor-price account proposes that expensive labour relative to capital and energy made British mechanisation profitable, with subsequent engineering widening its geographical applicability. The inspected source is his introductory formulation, not the underlying profitability calculations. It is a hypothesis about incentives requiring task-specific wages, machine costs and performance. [IND-K09; Allen, pp. 1–3](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/68273/excerpt/9780521868273_excerpt.pdf).

Humphries and Schneider reconstruct pay from accounts, output and piece rates. They find no general pre-invention surge in hand-spinners' remuneration; institutional labour supplies, geographical extension and employers' bargaining power complicate an aggregate high-wage story. Cotton observations are scarce, the dataset covers multiple fibres, and converting output records into daily earnings needs assumptions about work duration. Their evidence challenges a specific wage premise; it does not identify one uniquely sufficient motive for mechanisation. [IND-K10; accepted manuscript, pp. 15–16, 25–32, 37–41](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A4f321519-a0b7-4879-a521-139cbca81b25/download_file?safe_filename=Humphries_and_Schneider_Spinning_the_industrial_revolution.pdf&type_of_work=Journal+article).

Ure's 1835 advocacy explicitly connects machinery to substituting cheaper labour and reducing skilled workers' independence. It establishes a public managerial aspiration, not that factories automatically achieved complete deskilling or improved workers' welfare. His celebration of healthy workshops and effortless work requires independent worker and medical evidence. [IND-K11; contemporary extract, passages on factory principles and self-acting machinery](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/mod/1835ure.asp).

Engineering capacity supplies another explanation. Kelly and Ó Gráda connect instrument-making skills and precision measurement to increasingly complex textile machinery and subsequent machine tools. Repeated production and replacement of wearing parts created demands beyond a single successful prototype. Their account also links later tool adoption to attempts to substitute for expensive, organised mechanics. Skill creation and skill substitution therefore need not be opposing descriptions of the same episode. [IND-K12; working paper, sections 2.1–2.3, pp. 13–18](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/228197/1/1701742357.pdf).

The contemporary choices included expanding outwork, changing fibre or product, importing yarn, deploying small machinery and concentrating powered processes. It would be circular to infer that factory concentration was technologically unavoidable because factories later prevailed. A stronger test would compare output quality, supervision, finance, transport and labour retention across otherwise similar arrangements. Mechanisation could increase demand for engineering skill while removing household income, and could raise output without granting workers control over hours or gains. Invention, ownership and social benefit remain separate outcomes throughout this case.

## IND-C05 — India and Lancashire in cotton markets, c. 1700–1900

The comparison starts with competitive Indian manufacture, not an empty technological field awaiting European machinery. Broadberry and Gupta reconstruct a change in comparative costs and argue that rising Lancashire productivity eventually overcame its wage disadvantage. Raw-cotton prices and freight altered when that advantage reached different markets; spinning and weaving did not progress together. Their early working paper uses imperfect wage comparisons and inferred productivity, including assumptions about Indian stagnation. Its numerical ratios should not be treated as direct measurements of every household's efficiency. [IND-K13; 2005 discussion paper, pp. 16–25, 33](https://muslintrust.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/wrap_broadberry-cotton-textiles-and-the-great-diverge.pdf).

Clingingsmith and Williamson propose distinct phases: political and agricultural disruption raising grain and labour costs before the full factory import shock, followed by stronger pressure from international manufactured prices. Their model assumes mobile labour, subsistence-linked wages and initially non-traded grain; it abstracts from capital and land. Price movements are informative but do not independently identify the contribution of Mughal fragmentation, climate and Company rule. [IND-K14; 2005 working paper, sections 4–7, pp. 19–28](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w11730/w11730.pdf).

Inikori's different framing puts Atlantic demand and slavery-linked commodity production inside the explanation of British regional growth. He also reproduces Charles Grant's 1821 recognition of asymmetric tariff power between Britain and India. These establish mechanisms and contemporary awareness of coercive trade arrangements; the parliamentary original was not independently inspected here. His larger claim about slavery's central causal role remains an argued historical interpretation. [IND-K15–K16; Inikori, sections II–IV](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/709818).

These accounts answer different questions. A cost reconstruction can explain a merchant's sourcing decision conditional on a market; it cannot by itself explain who opened that market, who could protect it or how raw cotton and purchasing power were produced. Conversely, imperial coercion does not establish that every displaced Indian product could have competed indefinitely under a different tariff. The appropriate counterfactual must specify product, date, policy and transport.

The displacement evidence is also uneven: export loss, household spinning decline, handloom persistence and subsequent mill growth are not interchangeable indicators. This contribution does not claim universal disappearance of Indian hand production. A decisive next comparison would follow matched cloth qualities through export and local markets, with district employment and fiscal records. If industrial contraction preceded the hypothesised import pressure, an import-only account fails; if comparable protected producers also lost competitiveness, protection alone is insufficient. Neither result removes the distributional consequences of colonial rule.

## IND-C06 — Ring and mule spinning in Japan, India and Britain, 1878–1933

Saxonhouse and Wright's machinery-order study is unusually valuable because it follows specifications and choices rather than imposing a fixed technological ranking. Japanese mills combined rings with imported cotton varieties, mixing and preparation work, and a changed labour force. An American-origin machine thus became part of a relatively labour-intensive arrangement. Both rings and mules improved before 1914; the mule's later retreat does not prove that earlier British purchases were irrational. India was the largest destination for high-draft orders in their 1929–1933 records, although their labour-relations explanation for that result is tentative. [IND-K17–K19; pp. 537–542, 549–554, 558–561](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1T5GYPrNlqQ3QoJPo_vzEwlDRF7LhR7IG/view).

This comparison invalidates a fixed mapping from “high wages” to a particular machine. An adopter can alter cotton preparation, machine speed, product count, recruitment and the distribution of work. Capital equipment imports need not mean passively copying the exporting country's organisation. Equally, apparently identical machines can deliver different output when skills and maintenance differ.

The observed unit remains an order. It is not a census of every operating spindle, realised profit or worker experience. Machinery firms and customers jointly influence recorded specifications; successful purchasers are easier to observe than firms that never secured finance. The statistical association of prior national experience with new purchases is consistent with learning, supplier routines or persistent omitted conditions. It is not an exogenous learning experiment.

A useful falsifier of the adaptation account would be matched mill records showing that cotton mixing and labour reorganisation did not precede the claimed performance improvement, or that the gain disappears after accounting for yarn quality. The comparison also needs recruitment contracts, hours and health outcomes before calling lower production costs an unqualified benefit. At present the strongest conclusion concerns flexible technical and organisational recombination, not a general ranking of Japanese and Indian institutions.

## IND-C07 — Chinese cotton households and factory inputs, c. 1870–1949

Eyferth's historical background describes a hybrid: some weavers combined stronger machine-spun warp with thicker hand-spun weft. Cheap factory yarn could support the geographical spread of handloom weaving even as hand spinning contracted. He reports that handloom cloth still supplied about 70 percent of Chinese cotton textile output in 1934–1936, drawing on earlier research rather than a newly audited census. The same article documents changing ideals of women's work, including the erasure of household textile production from some reformers' accounts of productive activity. [IND-K20–K22; Eyferth, pp. 369–374](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/1B4DC63727682F1EEC958A62A4126757/S0020859012000521a.pdf/div-class-title-womenandapos-s-work-and-the-politics-of-homespun-in-socialist-china-1949-1980-div.pdf).

This is not simply failed mechanisation. A household could adopt an industrial intermediate input while retaining another operation. The correct diffusion denominator differs for factory yarn, power looms and purchased cloth. Market-share decline can coexist with persistence or growth in absolute hand production. The numerical estimate above is therefore retained with medium confidence and its original-study chain exposed.

It would nevertheless be misleading to infer that women freely preferred long hours of hand work. Household allocation, gender norms, market access, cash and alternative earnings shape what appears cheap to the household. Physical product properties also matter: “cloth” is not a homogeneous service if warmth, durability and social uses differ. The observed hybrid cannot, by itself, tell us the relative weight of each consideration.

A sharper investigation would pair household time budgets with local yarn and cloth prices, alternative female employment and fabric characteristics. If hybrid practice persisted where equivalent factory cloth was affordable and women's outside earnings were high, an opportunity-cost account would need revision. If adoption changed after employment access changed without altered beliefs, an autonomous cultural-preference account would weaken. Eyferth's principal study continues after 1949; those later state-socialist mechanisms are not projected backward as established facts in this bounded case.

## IND-C08 — Electrifying American manufacturing, c. 1880–1930

Devine distinguishes motors turning existing line shafts, motors serving groups, and motors attached to individual machines. These arrangements could coexist. Unit drive offered control over location, timing and speed, but required suitable machine design and production organisation. Utility growth and installation services helped create an adoptable package; owners of sunk generating equipment had different choices from builders of new plants. [IND-K23–K24; Devine, pp. 355–370](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/automation/1983-devine.pdf).

His figures place electric motors at about one-quarter of manufacturing mechanical-drive capacity in 1909 and over half in 1919. Those are horsepower shares, not shares of factories or workers. The timing of unit drive is reconstructed from technical literature and equipment design because comprehensive configuration statistics were not collected. “Electricity adopted” therefore conceals a substantial measurement problem. [IND-K25; Devine, pp. 368–370](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/automation/1983-devine.pdf).

This case separates generation from transmission and use. An electric motor could be supplied by onsite steam generation; replacing a factory shaft did not necessarily remove coal from its energy chain. The innovative service was controllable delivery at the machine, not electricity as an abstract substance. That distinction prevents a misleading succession of water, steam and electricity as mutually exclusive fuels.

Benefits from redesign remain plausible mechanisms, but a before-and-after productivity increase does not isolate them from investment, product changes or selection of expanding firms. Compare retrofits with new plants, and units with similar demand but different electricity service, before attributing the entire productivity acceleration to motors. Retaining group drive can be rational where machines operate together and extra motors cost more than the flexibility is worth. Delay can reflect real equipment replacement cycles; it is not automatically ignorance or resistance. For workers, more precise machine control could change pace and discretion as well as effort, a distributional question this mainly engineering-focused source cannot settle.

## Cross-case findings, rival explanations and limits

The cases support a conditional adoption framework, not a universal law. Begin with the actor and the task: a mine investor buys drainage, a spinner buys reliable yarn output, a household obtains clothing, and a factory owner buys productive capacity. Evaluate output quality and reliability alongside labour, fuel, capital, repair, coordination and switching costs. This is an analytical framework derived from the comparisons, not an estimated equation. It leaves room for a technologically feasible system that nobody can finance, an inexpensive proposal that cannot secure cooperation, and a profitable investment whose burdens fall on people excluded from the decision.

Prices matter, but the relevant price is often produced by institutions. Poor-law labour, control over tariffs, recruitment arrangements and ownership of raw materials belong inside cost comparisons. Separating an “economic explanation” from a “political explanation” too early can remove the process that formed the observed prices. Yet institutional stories require discrimination too: the existence of coercion does not identify its numerical contribution to each invention, and the presence of skilled workers does not prove that skill supply was the binding constraint.

There are at least four different delay mechanisms here. A design may need improvement; complements may be absent; an incumbent may retain a product-specific advantage; or the alternative may demand coordination that no actor can organise. Each implies a different test. Better engineering should shift performance at given inputs. Complement provision should change feasible use. Product-specific persistence should track measurable product attributes. Coordination failure should leave documentary evidence of mutually beneficial but blocked collective action. A historical sequence alone does not distinguish them.

Three causal claims are deliberately withheld. No source here supplies a single credible share of the Industrial Revolution attributable to wages, coal or colonialism. No sequence from invention to diffusion establishes an inevitable development path. No output or horsepower series establishes welfare improvement for displaced spinners, factory children, enslaved producers or unpaid household workers. Studying those outcomes requires evidence about their constraints and alternatives, not only the adopter's accounts.

The graph is consequently a set of scoped relationships. Water competes with steam in one setting and is supported by it in another. Precision engineering enables reproducible machinery, while expanding machinery use creates maintenance demand. Machine yarn can displace hand spinning while complementing hand weaving. Such edges concern the specified case, interval and task; they are neither universal dependencies nor claims of descent from a single inventor.

Coverage remains deliberately uneven. This industrial contribution gives substantial attention to British, Indian, Japanese, Chinese and American systems, but limited attention to continental European factories and none sufficient for African, Ottoman, Russian or Latin American industrial trajectories. Colonial power is represented through competing scholarship rather than a fresh archival reconstruction. Machinery orders, engineering journals and elite treatises privilege owners and technically successful systems. The next research priorities are specified in [questions.md](questions.md), and uncertainty is attached to each bounded claim rather than hidden in a general disclaimer.
