# Health, sanitation and public goods: adopting a treatment, building a service

Research edition 1, 5 November 2026. Eight purposively selected cases; 24 bounded claims; 16 inspected sources. This is a historical investigation, not clinical guidance. The source ledger distinguishes accessible full texts and inspected sections from the one abstract-only item. Historical actors' claims of safety, efficacy or ignorance are not automatically this report's conclusions.

Health technologies make the gap between technical feasibility and experienced benefit unusually visible. A preparation can work in a carefully observed patient while remaining unavailable elsewhere. A law can require vaccination without supplying vaccinators. A purchased medicine can be widely used for the wrong illness. A successful preventive programme can make its continued expense look unnecessary. These are different outcomes and require different explanations.

The comparison therefore follows several actors separately: patients and caregivers, practitioners, suppliers, public authorities, and people exposed to the consequences of others' decisions. It asks who controls the choice, who finances its complements, and whether reported adoption means possession, competent performance, repeated use, appropriate use, or sustained population protection. The cases do not estimate how commonly any mechanism operated across world history. Nor do they turn a historically identified complement into a universal prerequisite.

## MED-C01 — Variolation across Ottoman, African and British networks, c. 1700–1723

The relevant choice was exposure to naturally acquired smallpox versus a deliberately induced infection under an experienced practitioner's care. This was variolation, not Jennerian vaccination. Boylston's investigation reproduces reports by Timonius and Pylarinius, testimony about African practice transmitted through Cotton Mather and Onesimus, and the Montagu family's encounters with Ottoman practitioners. The record makes women's practical knowledge and an enslaved African's testimony visible alongside physicians and elite intermediaries. It does not justify treating London as the origin of the technique. [MED-S01, “Inoculation in parts of the Ottoman Empire and Europe.”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1258/jrsm.2012.12k044) **MED-K01**.

That evidence supports transmission and use, not a reliable prevalence estimate for entire societies. Boylston also shows why precise origin stories remain insecure: late testimony, secrecy, conflicting accounts and differences between techniques complicate retrospective genealogies. This report adopts that uncertainty, not his conjecture about a single ancestral route, or his precise geographical attribution of Onesimus. [MED-S01, “Inoculation in China and India”; “Conclusions.”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1258/jrsm.2012.12k044) **MED-K02**.

The adoption mechanism proposed here is brokerage of credibility as well as information. A recipe reaching an institution and an institution trusting someone who performs it are distinct events. That interpretation would be weakened if local treatment records showed substantial uptake before the purported brokers, or if changes in epidemic danger better explained the timing. The surviving elite correspondence is particularly poor at distinguishing those alternatives. Its prominence can hide practitioners who left no comparable archive.

Nor should observed resistance be classified automatically as irrational. Deliberate infection involved an immediate, visible hazard, whereas avoiding later natural infection was a counterfactual benefit. Quantifying the comparison would require comparable recipient populations and follow-up, rather than importing a single mortality ratio from partisan testimony. The record supplies evidence of non-European expertise without permitting either a European discovery myth or an equally unsupported ancient-origin myth.

## MED-C02 — Ottoman smallpox vaccination, 1840–1915

Evered and Evered's study of Ottoman publications and regulations documents free-vaccination commitments, multilingual instruction, religious authorization, professional training proposals, and increasingly extensive mandates. The 1885 rules linked vaccination to school entry and state service; later regulations expanded obligations and recording requirements. These were active Ottoman institutional projects, not merely delayed reception of European ideas. The authors also distinguish these ambitions from severe constraints on personnel, vaccine supply and rural delivery. [MED-S02, §§2–4.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7689169/) **MED-K03–K05**.

The useful comparison is between institutional populations that authorities could reach and dispersed households whose existence, location and vaccination history were harder to establish. A compulsory-service rule does not supply evidence that every child was immunized. The report therefore codes legal deployment and incomplete implementation separately. It also separates a state's explanation of popular distrust from independently established household motives: official texts criticizing unqualified healers tell us how authorities sought legitimacy, not exactly what every rural family believed.

This case suggests three interacting costs. The recipient's monetary price could be reduced while transport, lost time and confidence in the vaccinator remained unresolved. Providers needed recurring inputs and supervision. Administrators needed records connecting births, visits, inspection and certification. Calling all three “hesitancy” would collapse failures of provision into an alleged preference of the unserved.

Compulsion also changes the meaning of uptake. Exclusion from school or penalties can induce compliance even when the household remains unconvinced; conversely, evasion can express objection to state intrusion as well as fear of a procedure. The legal record demonstrates those instruments, but not their enforcement rate or aggregate welfare balance. A useful falsifier for the capacity interpretation would be locality-level evidence of adequate, reliable provision with persistent low uptake, compared with similar localities facing shortages. A useful falsifier for a trust interpretation would be rapid uptake after supply expanded without any accompanying change in messengers. Both require evidence beyond promulgated regulations.

## MED-C03 — Antiseptic surgery in Britain, 1867–1890

Lister's 1867 address presents antisepsis through wound management, dressings, case narratives and continuing modifications. It includes observations and strong claims about his wards; it is not a controlled estimate of the effect of a fixed modern protocol. [MED-S03, 2010 reprint of the 1867 address, opening and concluding clinical discussions.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2895849/) **MED-K06**.

Worboys reconstructs the communication of operative skills through demonstrations, publications, assistants and professional example. Surgeons adapted combinations of practices, while the regimen itself changed. Tröhler examines the disputed statistical comparisons: small groups, different selection periods, hospital epidemics and case comparability made sweeping conclusions contestable. These are reasons to study disagreement, rather than assuming that recognition of an obvious truth was blocked only by pride. [MED-S04, “Antiseptic performance” and “Professional performance”;](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3744349/) [MED-S05, sections on statistics and the longer-term perspective.](https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/articles/statistics-and-the-british-controversy-about-the-effects-of-joseph-listers-system-of-antisepsis-for-surgery-1867-1890/) **MED-K07–K08**.

The contemporary choice set included established cleanliness practices and selective borrowing, not simply complete acceptance or complete refusal. Accepting a principle, owning carbolic acid and reliably carrying out an operation are therefore different adoption variables. If a complex routine failed elsewhere, its advocate could attribute failure to imperfect execution. That may sometimes be correct, but it also makes the claim hard to refute. A convincing evaluation must specify the routine, the operators, the cases and the standard of comparison before looking at outcomes.

Professional incentives have two plausible directions here. Reputation may discourage a surgeon from abandoning familiar methods, but it may also reward conspicuous improvement, visiting an influential teacher or demonstrating mastery of new techniques. The inspected evidence supports transmission through professional practice; it does not identify the net effect of status competition. Nor does it establish that every skeptical surgeon had sound reasons. The bounded conclusion is that tacit skill, changing protocols and contested outcome evidence are indispensable to explaining uneven adoption. A morality tale centred on a lone neglected genius cannot account for these mechanisms.

## MED-C04 — Municipal water and sewerage in Massachusetts and US cities, 1880–1940

Alsan and Goldin exploit staggered infrastructure completion around Boston, where regional sewerage participation was compulsory and engineering conditions influenced rollout. Their preferred estimates assign approximately one-third of the 1880–1920 decline in log under-five mortality in the relevant treated municipalities to combined safe water and effective sewerage; isolated interventions had smaller effects. The interpretation depends on their identification assumptions and concerns a specific regional system. It is not a universal coefficient for pipes. [MED-S06, pp. 589–590, §II.B and §IV.](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.harvard.edu/dist/e/935/files/2025/11/alsangoldin_jpe.pdf) **MED-K09–K10**.

The mechanism is both physical and political. Removing contamination and limiting exposure can be complementary; the service also crosses municipal boundaries. An upstream jurisdiction does not bear every downstream cost. Regional obligations can change that choice, although the authority to compel payment is itself a distributional issue. Household willingness to buy cleaner water cannot alone settle the design and finance of a shared watershed. An evaluation of health gains also does not by itself assess the environmental consequences of where the collected waste was discharged.

The magnitude of the broader US mortality contribution is contested. Anderson, Charles and Rees's inspected July 2019 manuscript uses 25 cities, 1900–1940, and reports smaller aggregate effects than influential earlier estimates. Cutler and Miller's inspected 2022 comment abstract defends a substantial total-mortality contribution while acknowledging smaller infant effects and differences in coding partial intervention years and population denominators. [MED-S07, §§3–6;](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2020/preliminary/paper/s855Kd6r) [MED-S08, abstract only.](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.20190711) **MED-K11**.

This disagreement is not a reason to erase waterborne transmission; it limits how much of a historical transition can be assigned to particular investments. It also prevents quietly combining a local complementarity estimate, a national narrative and an old cost-benefit ratio into one seemingly established result. The competing explanations include nutrition, housing, other sanitary measures, mortality measurement and changing populations. Falsification requires outcomes timed to actual service delivery, credible comparison populations and checks for mortality changes preceding the intervention. Negative-control outcomes can help, but cannot rescue incorrect installation dates or denominators.

For adoption analysis, distinguish a municipal vote, completion of a plant, network coverage, household connection and effective operation. They may occur years apart. Treating any one date as the whole system's adoption can distort both the history and its estimated consequences.

## MED-C05 — Penicillin industrial production, Britain and the United States, 1941–1945

The inspected USDA and American Chemical Society accounts describe the move from promising material to large-scale supply through improved culture media and strains, fermentation experience, plant conversion, extraction, purification and sterile finishing. Pfizer's previous fermentation work is a concrete cross-industry complement; British and US governmental initiatives helped connect researchers and manufacturers. [MED-S09, “Development of Deep-tank Fermentation” and “Penicillin Production by Deep-tank Fermentation”;](https://www.acs.org/education/whatischemistry/landmarks/penicillin.html) [MED-S10, “The Rescue of Penicillin,” paragraphs on the 1941 collaboration.](https://www.ars.usda.gov/oc/timeline/penicillin) **MED-K12–K14**.

These are institutional commemorations with incentives to celebrate their own contributions. Accordingly, this case uses them to establish specified activities and process connections. It does not derive a global production denominator, settle discovery priority, or claim that one institution caused mass availability by itself. Plant-level records and procurement archives remain important gaps.

The adoption barrier faced by a clinician was not necessarily disbelief. It could be an inadequate quantity of usable material. For a manufacturer, however, the choice included capacity committed to other products, uncertain yields and contamination losses. The same intervention could therefore be medically attractive and commercially difficult at the same moment. A laboratory concentration, an industrial batch and a delivered treatment are not interchangeable units.

The cross-case inference is that a change in the feasible production frontier can unlock use without requiring a change in recipients' preferences. But this does not establish that wartime coordination was uniquely necessary: it may have accelerated developments that other financing arrangements would eventually support. A discriminating comparison would examine firms with different prior fermentation capabilities, access to shared strains, capital commitments and purchasing arrangements. It would also ask who received scarce output. Increased production can coexist with rationing and unequal access; the sources inspected here cannot quantify that distribution.

## MED-C06 — Malaria control, reversal and elimination in Sri Lanka, 1945–2016

Sri Lanka's experience prevents coding successful deployment as permanent adoption. A retrospective analysis reports six indigenous and eleven imported cases in 1963, followed by reduced control activity, weakened vigilance and a major later resurgence. It dates emerging DDT resistance to 1969 and replacement by malathion to 1977. This sequencing does not support explaining the initial reversal solely through an insecticide ban or resistance. [MED-S11, “Malaria in Sri Lanka During the Second Half of the Twentieth Century.”](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2017.00212/full) **MED-K15–K16**.

WHO's contemporaneous 2016 address records certification after three consecutive years of documented zero transmission and describes sustained case detection, treatment and coordinated outreach. Certification concerned interrupted local transmission, not the disappearance of all imported infections or the permanent absence of risk. [MED-S16, paragraphs beginning “On September 5, 2016” and “First, a Strong Health System.”](https://www.who.int/southeastasia/news/speeches/detail/address-by-regional-director-on-the-occasion-of-malaria-elimination-certification-by-the-government-of-sri-lanka) **MED-K17**.

Cohen and colleagues provide a wider negative-case comparison: their review catalogued 75 historical resurgence events, frequently attributed to weakened programmes. Crucially, only 16% of the proposed causes had substantial supporting analysis, and the review did not independently validate each attribution. Its counts are not causal probabilities. [MED-S12, “Reported causes of resurgence” and “Discussion.”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1475-2875-11-122) **MED-K18**.

The mechanism proposed is a maintenance trap: suppression reduces visible illness while leaving the ecology and organizational requirement for suppression partly intact. Officials then face an apparently less urgent budget claim. This is a plausible interpretation, not a reason to read every spending reduction as irrational complacency. Competing health needs, conflict, changes in settlement and vector behaviour also alter the available choices and consequences.

A strong test would align local staffing, surveillance intensity, spray coverage, population movements and resistance measurements with subsequent infection patterns. More testing can raise recorded incidence without raising true transmission; less testing can conceal deterioration. That observation process must be part of the case. Sri Lankan expertise and organization belong within the explanation, rather than appearing as a passive setting in which an imported chemical either worked or failed.

## MED-C07 — Oral rehydration in Dhaka, refugee camps and rural Bangladesh, 1968–1993

Ruxin's interview-based history distinguishes physiological research, practical therapy and wider implementation. The work involved Dhaka and Calcutta research teams; Mahalanabis's 1971 refugee-camp work demonstrated administration by family members under emergency conditions. Its contribution was operational as well as clinical. [MED-S13, pp. 389–393.](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/4B8A9C4B64657965297CCE1E21C108C7/S0025727300036905a.pdf/magic_bullet_the_history_of_oral_rehydration_therapy.pdf) **MED-K19**.

BRAC's later household teaching combined demonstration, monitoring and wider community engagement. Chowdhury and colleagues' 1993 survey revisited 90 villages from different programme phases. Its uptake denominator deserves care: Table 2 gives 51.8%, 54.5% and 53.0% across cohorts for all reported diarrhoeal episodes. The abstract says 60%; the discussion reaches over 60% after excluding dysentery. The table's ORT definition also includes other home fluids, so these numbers are not strict packaged-ORS coverage. [MED-S14, pp. 59–60, Box 1, Table 2 and pp. 64–65.](https://doi.org/10.1093/heapol/12.1.58) **MED-K20–K21**.

This discrepancy matters substantively. A history using only packets sold would miss home preparation; one using knowledge would count people who did not act; one using all household fluids might overstate fidelity to the intervention being evaluated. An apparent plateau could reflect changed denominators or different illness categories rather than a stable resistance to treatment.

The relevant human work included teaching, remembering, recognizing an episode, obtaining ingredients or packets and spending time caring for someone. Material simplicity should not be mistaken for zero implementation cost. A caregiver's judgment of whether the remedy addresses the perceived problem may differ from an investigator's judgment of its physiological purpose. The survey is useful for observing these distinctions but does not independently randomize the programme's components. Other changes in distribution, education and care could contribute to the observed pattern.

A useful falsifier for the teaching mechanism would be equal preparation competence in otherwise comparable unexposed communities, after accounting for informal transmission. A useful test of convenience would compare access and actual use while holding knowledge reasonably constant. Neither is answered by celebrating the reach of a teaching campaign. The non-European institutions here produced research, implementation methods and evaluations; they should be treated as sources of expertise in their own right.

## MED-C08 — Antibiotic prescribing in one Chinese city, October 2011–June 2012

Currie, Lin and Meng used standardized patient audits to distinguish prescribing incentives from expressed patient demand. In their first experiment, the baseline antibiotic-prescription rate was 55%; saying the prescription would be filled elsewhere reduced it to 10%. A direct antibiotic request raised prescribing to 85% when dispensing was expected at the hospital, but only 14% when the patient would buy elsewhere. These are audited encounters with young, healthy simulated patients in one city, not national prevalence estimates. [MED-S15, §§I and III, experiment 1.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4776334/) **MED-K22**.

Additional scripts addressed alternative interpretations such as offense or concerns about outside drug quality. They strengthen the financial-incentive interpretation without turning the exercise into a complete reform evaluation. The study measured prescribing, not resulting resistance or patients' long-term health. [MED-S15, §§II–III and conclusion.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4776334/) **MED-K23–K24**.

The adoption lesson is that reducing price or increasing familiarity need not monotonically improve welfare. A provider's revenue motive can support use that is inappropriate to the immediate case, while antimicrobial resistance creates a potential cost beyond that encounter. The latter is a mechanism and risk in this analysis, not an outcome quantified by this experiment. Private financial desirability for a seller must not be confused with medical benefit for the patient.

This also qualifies the recurring explanation that “the public demands it.” Demand had different effects under different dispensing arrangements. Conversely, a successful incentive change in a short audit need not survive a system-wide reform: providers might replace lost revenue elsewhere, and institutions still need a workable financing model. A durable test would examine those substitutions, actual patients and access to needed treatment. The counterexample concerns inappropriate use, not an argument for withholding beneficial antibiotics.

## What the comparisons support—and leave unresolved

The strongest common inference is about the unit of adoption. For these cases, the operational unit is often a maintained relationship between a material intervention and organized human activity. That does not mean “everything depends on everything.” The graph names particular observed complements: operative teaching for a changing surgical regimen; regional obligations for a cross-boundary sewerage project; fermentation capabilities for a specified production process; surveillance and staff for sustained suppression. These links have different evidentiary strengths and historical scopes.

Evidence, trust and incentives are also not mutually exclusive explanations. A household can reasonably want treatment while lacking access, mistrusting the offered provider or facing prohibitive opportunity costs. A practitioner can know a technique and lack the circumstances to reproduce it. An organization can possess capacity while being rewarded for a different action. The cases suggest asking which constraint is binding for which actor before assigning a general label such as cultural resistance.

Public goods are similarly heterogeneous. Coordinating a watershed, requiring a vaccination certificate and financing repeated surveillance are different uses of authority. Increased uptake under compulsion records a response to altered consequences, not necessarily consent or improved welfare. Institutional power can make provision possible and impose burdens at the same time. The current evidence is stronger on the institutions' instruments than on the experiences of people subject to them.

Finally, failure and reversal belong in the evidence base. A production bottleneck, an unenforced mandate, a disputed clinical comparison, an interrupted control service and an unnecessary prescription do not share a single cause. Their comparison rules out simple sufficiency claims: efficacy alone, low material cost alone, information alone and law alone did not ensure appropriate sustained use in these episodes. Establishing which combinations generalize requires additional matched cases and records, especially outside the regions represented here. The companion questions file sets out those tests rather than projecting future winners.
