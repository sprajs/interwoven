# Precision, learning and repair: six bounded histories

Research contribution SKL, 5 November 2026. These six cases distinguish understanding a principle, reproducing an artifact, delivering a service and retaining access after breakdown. They are deliberately selected contrasts, not a representative sample of technologies or workers. Eighteen claims, twelve sources and nineteen scoped graph edges accompany the report. Full text was available for eleven sources, with the inspected sections stated in the ledger; the remaining source is a publisher's partial display of a paper's footnotes. Having acquired a book does not mean every chapter was read.

The organising question is concrete: which operation could a particular actor perform, with which tools, parts, instructions and rights? “Tacit knowledge” is useful only after identifying what information or action was missing and considering rival explanations. A failed replica may reflect hidden dimensions, misunderstood tolerances, unreliable components, poor documentation, selective disclosure or insufficient practice. Calling all these failures “lack of skill” would prevent investigation.

## SKL-C01 — A copied sea watch was not yet an autonomous navigation service

The Board of Longitude commissioned Larcum Kendall to copy Harrison's H4 in 1766. K1 was completed in 1769 and used on Cook's second voyage. The surviving object and its institutional history establish a named execution by another maker. That differs from a successful demonstration by the original inventor, but still does not establish inexpensive serial production. A contemporary obituary describes the commission as testing another craftsman's execution of Harrison's principles. Its approximate delivery chronology differs from the museum's completion date; the ledger preserves the disagreement instead of manufacturing an exact reconciliation. [SKL-K01; RMG, object ZAA0038](https://www.rmg.co.uk/sites/default/files/import/media/pdf/K1.pdf); [Kendall obituary, opening paragraph](https://www.captaincooksociety.com/cooks-life/people/cooks-officers-and-crew-and-contemporaries/larcum-kendall-obituary).

The same obituary records cleaning in 1775 and later third-voyage deterioration, stoppage and corrosion requiring replacement parts. This is evidence of service work on one celebrated instrument, not a measured failure rate for all early chronometers. Its praise of Kendall makes it especially important not to adopt the author's judgement of workmanship as an independent performance assessment. Nor does corrosion identify its own cause: design exposure, handling, contamination and care could differ between voyages. The discriminating evidence would be rate and service logs from comparable watches with known operating conditions. Until then, the supported conclusion is that successful construction left recurring operational and repair tasks. [SKL-K02; obituary, 1775 cleaning and third-voyage passages](https://www.captaincooksociety.com/cooks-life/people/cooks-officers-and-crew-and-contemporaries/larcum-kendall-obituary).

Higgitt's research synthesis also rejects a simple substitution of clocks for astronomy. Voyage timekeepers supplied reference time; users still observed local time and checked or restored confidence in their references. Cook's expeditions tested timekeepers alongside astronomical tables and observing instruments. Dead reckoning remained another practical route, particularly where its accuracy was adequate. The relevant system therefore included skilled observation, interpretation and maintenance, with different combinations suited to different journeys. [SKL-K03; Higgitt, “Investing in longitude” and “Navigation systems”](https://ethos.lps.library.cmu.edu/article/id/451/).

Commissioners chose and funded tests; makers constructed and serviced instruments; officers and astronomers turned readings into navigational information. Those are different positions in the adoption process. The evidence does not reveal the full labour of crews, whether they could refuse a particular arrangement, or the distribution of navigation's benefits and imperial harms. It would be inappropriate to turn the maker's recognition into a claim of universally improved opportunity. This case's unit remains a copied watch and its service episodes, not “the solution of longitude.”

## SKL-C02 — Japanese clocks could preserve a different task definition

Seasonal-hour clocks divided day and night into changing units. Japanese makers could accommodate that convention by changing the mechanism's rate or the position of hour markers. Hashimoto describes alternating foliots and movable markers; users or clockmakers periodically adjusted many examples. The coexistence of these arrangements matters: a mechanical clock did not compel adoption of equal hours. A user could obtain mechanical timekeeping while retaining the prevailing definition of the task. This does not prove that every household possessed a clock or that actual temporal practice was uniform. [SKL-K04; Hashimoto, pp. 36–37](https://utcp.c.u-tokyo.ac.jp/publications/pdf/CollectionUTCP6_Hashimoto_02.pdf); [Seiko Museum, opening explanation](https://museum.seiko.co.jp/en/knowledge/relation_15/).

Tanaka Hisashige's 1851 Myriad Year Clock supplied an especially elaborate automatic adjustment. Hashimoto's account draws on disassembly and measurement, while carefully distinguishing some conjectures about abandoned design possibilities. The reported commercial outcome is narrower: only one clock was completed despite an intention to sell more, and no buyer was secured. The explanation that prospective purchasers found it excessively expensive contains an anecdotal element. It cannot substitute for an order book or a calculation of production cost. Career redirection and limited selling opportunities are live rivals to a price-only account. The reliable lesson is the gap between an exceptional artifact and demonstrated repeated production. [SKL-K05; Hashimoto, pp. 37–41](https://utcp.c.u-tokyo.ac.jp/publications/pdf/CollectionUTCP6_Hashimoto_02.pdf).

The museum describes a gradual decline of these clocks after the fixed-hour reform of 1873. The reform changed the convention the designs served; that is different from finding that the old mechanisms had never worked. It also does not establish immediate household compliance or freely chosen preferences. Continued manual adjustment before reform supplies a useful older-practice comparator, while the automatic masterpiece supplies a non-scaling episode within the same technical tradition. [Seiko Museum, “Production of mechanical clocks in Japan”](https://museum.seiko.co.jp/en/knowledge/relation_15/).

Tanaka's career crossed commercial craft, specialist calendrical instruction from the Tsuchimikado family in 1847 and later Saga patronage. Hashimoto nevertheless says the tools used for the finest clock components remain uncertain. A documented learning route should not be expanded into a claim that every comparable maker needed the same instruction, or that the work was indescribably intuitive. [SKL-K06; Hashimoto, pp. 33–34, 40](https://utcp.c.u-tokyo.ac.jp/publications/pdf/CollectionUTCP6_Hashimoto_02.pdf). Ordinary makers' wages, household adjustment work and occupational transitions after reform are absent. A biography of a celebrated engineer cannot supply those distributions.

## SKL-C03 — Gauges had to be checked, and success had a product boundary

Hall's rifle patent dated from 1811; his government production contract followed in 1819, with further years of workshop tooling. His 1822 letter and Talcott's later account describe manufacture less dependent on hand operations. They are evidence of contemporary claims and institutional commitment, not independently audited savings or a census showing the extinction of skilled work. The main Harpers Ferry factory already used piece rates and a detailed division of tasks before Hall arrived. Production organisation therefore cannot be attributed wholly to his machines. [SKL-K07; NPS, “John H Hall”](https://home.nps.gov/hafe/learn/historyculture/john-h-hall.htm); [Hassler and Ebersole, p. 55](https://npshistory.com/series/archeology/ncr/21/report.pdf).

The NPS historical report describes working gauges checked against a separate reference set. This makes measurement maintenance a specific operation, rather than an unexplained attribute called precision. Machine motion, tool condition, component inspection and the references used to judge it belong to related but distinct tasks. The surviving Model 1841 inspection set, HAFE 7427, supplies physical evidence of such equipment. It is dated around 1846 and must not be passed off as Hall's original apparatus. It is also the same set pictured in the archaeological report, so the two links are not independent discoveries. [SKL-K08; report, p. 59, notes 244–245](https://npshistory.com/series/archeology/ncr/21/report.pdf); [NPS object record, HAFE 7427](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/view-4/KAFQGvXgiHqy_Q?childAssetId=XgHi054cXIxrog).

A negative observation prevents the word “interchangeable” from swallowing the whole institution. The report's account of an 1829 examination of Model 1816 muskets, made in 1819–1820, describes generally good quality but remaining defects and incomplete interchangeability. That account is mediated through Peter Schmidt; the original test report was not inspected. Even with that limitation, it warns against extending claims about Hall's separate rifle works to every arm and production year. [SKL-K09; report, p. 56, note 232](https://npshistory.com/series/archeology/ncr/21/report.pdf).

The stronger counterfactual asks whether maintained gauges changed rejection, fitting or repair costs within a constant model and workshop. Different products, waterpower conditions, supervision and budgets make inter-factory comparisons difficult. Government procurement could support prolonged tooling work that a private purchaser would not fund, but this evidence does not calculate that effect. Workers might lose discretion in one operation while other workers acquired responsibility for setup and inspection. Who captured the savings requires wages, contracts and employment histories, rather than inference from the rhetoric of ordinary labour. The documentary sources repeatedly inherit Smith's research; repeated citation is not repeated causal identification.

## SKL-C04 — Repair access involved learning rights and ownership of tools

Grace's Tanzanian research combines oral histories, photographs and participation in a garage during 2011–2012. He explicitly distinguishes mechanics' accounts of inventive achievement from routine work revealed by apprenticeship. Novices cleaned, assisted with heavy parts and learned through supervised repairs; some also cooked or performed household errands. Instruction, small profit shares and occasional job earnings formed a labour exchange that a simple paid/unpaid label would obscure. Earlier careers are reconstructed retrospectively, not directly witnessed. [SKL-K10; Grace, chapter 2, pp. 94–95, 118–121](https://www.bibliopen.org/p/bopen/9781478094456).

Accumulating personal tools widened opportunities for work outside a head mechanic's garage. Grace records salvaging damaged tools and a named mechanic's movement from a small under-tree operation to a larger equipped workshop. This supplies a concrete ownership mechanism, but reputation, skill and customers changed alongside the tools. A comparison of apprentices with similar experience but different access to usable equipment would be needed to isolate the effect. [SKL-K11; Grace, p. 120](https://www.bibliopen.org/p/bopen/9781478094456).

Repair methods also varied with spare parts and machine access. Mechanics disagreed about the reliability of hand methods and extensive modification; some preferred stock modules and specialised by make or task, while manuals and practical experience coexisted. That evidence preserves both continued older practice and a limit to romantic accounts of improvisation. [SKL-K12; Grace, pp. 136–142 and table 2.1](https://www.bibliopen.org/p/bopen/9781478094456).

The economically relevant comparison is consequently not “African repair versus the technically superior new car.” It is a set of actually available choices: repair with locally available resources, obtain a replacement component, wait, retire the vehicle or buy another vehicle if finance permits. The affordability of one route does not certify its safety or durability. An owner and mechanic may agree to work whose risks are borne partly by passengers and road users. Nor does an apprenticeship's accessibility mean that a young person had abundant alternatives or could negotiate the allocation of unpleasant tasks.

The source is unusually valuable for distinguishing possession of a manual from access to a learning opportunity, and technical ability from ownership of tools. Its limitations remain substantial: selected successful mechanics, retrospective recollection, a short researcher apprenticeship and no matched road-performance tests. A rigorous follow-up would retain failed trainees, failed businesses and failed repairs in the denominator. It would also examine women's participation and the distribution of household work instead of accepting the workshop's masculine account as a complete occupational history. No claim of net job creation or average upward mobility follows from these examples.

## SKL-C05 — Appliance purchase did not guarantee continued service

Mchome's Dar es Salaam study uses interviews conducted in 2018 and 2020, supplemented by newspapers, archives and earlier scholarship. Most technicians he interviewed reported vocational schooling as well as learning through subsequent informal practice. The author acknowledges the shortage of educational statistics. This supports coexistence of learning routes within the sample; it does not establish their prevalence across Tanzania or the marginal value of either route. [SKL-K13; Mchome, pp. 78, 83–85](https://reference-global.com/pdf/10.2478/host-2022-0005).

Users reported restored appliance function, including one television still used four years after repair. Repair could therefore preserve access to an existing service where replacement was costly. The article also describes power-supply modifications. These remain reported interventions: neither their safety nor the technicians' broad explanation of voltage incompatibility was independently tested. A household testimony is evidence of that account, not a survival curve for repaired devices. [SKL-K14; pp. 85–87](https://reference-global.com/pdf/10.2478/host-2022-0005).

The source proposes a further labour pathway: utility restructuring and retrenchment helped shift some trained workers into freelance electrical services. Computerisation, prepaid metering and structural-adjustment cuts occurred together in the account. It does not identify their separate effects or track whether workers recovered earlier pay and security. This remains a candidate mechanism, not a verified technological-displacement estimate. [SKL-K15; pp. 91–93](https://reference-global.com/pdf/10.2478/host-2022-0005).

Appliance repairs and unofficial work on the utility network must also be distinguished. Their technical tasks, authority and legal status differ, even where people or learning networks overlap. The case centres household appliance use; it does not treat every informal technician as performing the same intervention. Household members also differ in ability to finance replacement, protection or backup equipment, making ownership an incomplete indicator of experienced access.

A rival to an outage-centred explanation is growth in the installed stock of appliances: more devices create more repair work even if individual reliability improves. Another is selection of successful repair stories. Distinguishing these requires device counts, failures, all attempted repairs and subsequent service histories. A rising repair-sector workload can reflect useful expertise, worsening infrastructure or both. Calling it resilience should not conceal the money, time and uncertainty transferred to households, or imply that a new freelance job compensates for a lost formal position.

## SKL-C06 — A bounded test of learning, including evidence against simple precision stories

Collins investigated seven identified British laboratories in summer 1971, supplementing his snowball search with other searches for isolates. Within the network he examined, written-only attempts had not succeeded; working copies followed contact with successful builders. But visits sometimes failed, early publications omitted information, and he explicitly allowed the possibility of successful written-only construction. This is strong evidence for a local communication problem and weaker evidence for an intrinsically unwriteable skill. [SKL-K16; Collins, pp. 172–173, 176–177, 182–183](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~jbain/scitechsoc/readings/74Collins.pdf).

A particularly discriminating episode concerns electrode profiles. One laboratory found demanding machining requirements insuperable, while rougher profiles operated elsewhere. That undermines a universal claim that tighter tolerances were the missing ingredient. It does not prove that all configurations tolerate the same geometry: other circuit and construction differences remained uncontrolled. [SKL-K17; Collins, p. 177](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~jbain/scitechsoc/readings/74Collins.pdf).

The accessible footnotes of Collins and Harrison's follow-up report simultaneous changes to two variables, a teaching burden and repeated power-unit failures. Those admissions matter because a successful adjustment sequence cannot reveal which alteration caused success. The full paper's narrative was not acquired; this report uses only the displayed footnotes and does not reconstruct an unavailable build history. [SKL-K18; Collins and Harrison, footnotes 2, 8, 10–11](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/030631277500500404).

Contact could transfer missing explicit information, opportunities to observe, confidence about which feature mattered, or access to suitable parts. Better resourced or more experienced laboratories might also secure better contacts. A useful test would hold components and documentation quality constant while comparing instruction and contact conditions, recording unsuccessful attempts rather than only eventual builders. Later successful builds from improved documentation would count against permanent tacit necessity without erasing the historical usefulness of visits.

The early network also included selective disclosure, commercial licences and purchase as an alternative to construction. These are organisational choices around a shared physical principle. Researchers pursuing a beam for another experiment could rationally value rapid operation differently from researchers developing the laser itself. Teaching, machining and troubleshooting absorbed labour, but the study does not establish occupational gains or losses. The graph therefore records contact as a documented contribution within the early network, never as an unqualified prerequisite of laser technology.

## Comparisons and remaining limits

Four comparisons are recorded separately. H4/K1 and TEA lasers distinguish a working demonstration from transfer and service. Seasonal clocks and TEA electrode choices show why the relevant performance requirement must be specified before ranking complexity or precision. Harpers Ferry and Tanzanian garages locate different work around making components fit. The two Tanzanian cases distinguish access restored by repair from infrastructure reliability and secure livelihoods. Their geography is shared, and Mchome cites Grace for earlier garage history; those connections preclude counting every recurrence as independent corroboration.

The graph makes nineteen local claims. No edge asserts universal necessity, and no cluster of incoming edges asserts sufficient cause or a jointly indispensable bundle. The model does not project a lineage from clockmakers to every later precision industry. Its uncertainty is purposeful: original armory inspection records, complete workshop costs, comparative repair lifetimes and worker transition cohorts would materially improve the argument.

These cases support asking where useful work moved, who could enter it and what resources they controlled. They do not support a forecast about future occupations, a historical success rate for repair or a general rule that investment, documentation or formal schooling can be replaced by experience. The inspectable distinction is between a resource with an identified task and an attractive explanation whose decisive comparison has yet to be made.
