# Behaviour, social meaning and accessibility in technology use

Independent PSY contribution, 5 November 2026. Five case dossiers; 20 bounded claims. The bicycle dossier contains explicitly separate Bihar and Zambia episodes. This is a purposive comparison of modern evidence, principally from 2000–2021, not a history of human psychology or a representative global sample.

The evidence changes the adoption account in three ways. First, behavioural explanations need to identify a process between an offer and an outcome: noticing a relevant input, undertaking communication work, anticipating a social response, or learning to operate a device. Naming a preference after observing non-adoption explains little. Second, institutions alter those processes through payment arrangements, eligibility, professional services, information presentation and control over an asset. Third, purchase, use, task benefit, retention and welfare are different outcomes. Several cases improve one without clearly improving another.

The sources include field experiments, a clinical trial, quasi-experimental analyses, observational comparisons and original qualitative interviews. They are not interchangeable evidence. No laboratory experiment or marketing survey supplies an independent causal claim here. Fourteen source records include one correction and the final abstract of an already inspected author manuscript; this is not fourteen independent confirmations. Malawi incentive/gender papers share a larger experiment; the Bihar road analysis reuses the main household survey; Zambia manuscript and final abstract describe the same trial. Downloaded files, versions and failed acquisition attempts remain in the local provenance record.

## PSY-C01: Buying fertilizer at a different time

A technology must first be specified at the level at which it works. In Busia, Kenya, adjacent-plot experiments found positive mean calculated returns for half-teaspoon CAN top-dressing, while the fuller officially recommended fertilizer/hybrid-seed package had negative mean returns despite raising yield. Assistance with application and small experimental plots limit extrapolation. “Fertilizer works” is therefore too coarse a premise for explaining non-adoption. **PSY-K01.** [Duflo, Kremer and Robinson 2008, pp.483–487, Table 1](https://poverty-action.org/sites/default/files/publications/How%20High%20are%20Rates%20of%20Return%20to%20Fertilizer.pdf).

The subsequent SAFI experiment sampled 1,230 school parents, obtaining adoption data for 924. Its outcome was fertilizer on any household plot. Early offers combined market-price fertilizer with free delivery. Adjusted use rose by 14.3 and 18.1 percentage points in the two treatment seasons, without detectable persistence after offers ceased. Late free delivery estimated a positive 9–10-point effect, but imprecisely; the early–late difference had p=.08. A late 50% subsidy produced a comparable treatment-season gain. Timing-choice results also failed to fit one prediction of the authors’ simple present-bias model. **PSY-K02–03.** [Duflo, Kremer and Robinson 2011, Table 4, pp.2373–2377; §VI](https://www.atai-research.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/aer-Nudging-Fertilizer-2011.pdf).

The identified effect is an offer effect. The preferred psychological explanation is less secure. Harvest resources, alternative uses of cash, acquisition effort, advance notice and trusted delivery can share predictions with procrastination. The paper’s prechoice, maize-purchase and smaller payment-delay experiments constrain some simple alternatives, but do not directly measure a present-bias parameter. Nor does failure to maintain the increase prove that farmers learned nothing: recurrent resource constraints can prevent use of something whose value is understood.

For the programme’s causal account, this case should replace a single “low cost causes adoption” link with a dated acquisition arrangement. It also supplies a negative test of automatic habit formation. A sharper future test would independently vary seasonal resources and advance announcement while prospectively measuring intended and actual purchases. That is a proposed test, not a result obtained here.

The farmer chooses and pays; the NGO absorbs delivery work. Household members may cultivate separate plots, so the respondent is not the only operator or beneficiary. Neither plot returns nor seasonal uptake establish who gains bargaining power, whether hired workers benefit, or whether a permanent occupation emerges.

## PSY-C02: Learning which seaweed input matters

The Indonesian seaweed experiment improves on reasoning backwards from low productivity to inattention. At baseline, 86% of 489 growers could not state their current pod size and 87% could not state an optimum, whereas most could state spacing. Enumerators also observed actual practices. Of these growers, 117 were assigned on-plot trials, followed later by personalised summaries. **PSY-K04.** [Hanna, Mullainathan and Schwartzstein 2014, §III and Table II](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/Learning%20Through%20Noticing%20Field%202014_c1214d62-9429-4357-b47a-4195dae00881.pdf).

Change was more evident after summaries than after trials alone. Objective pod-size effects were approximately seven grams and imprecise, while the change in those treatment coefficients across phases was clearer. Self-reported practice-change phase differences were not significant; spacing remained largely unchanged. The sequence therefore supports a trial-plus-explanation pathway, with weaker evidence for any single cognitive mechanism. **PSY-K05–06.** [Same study, Tables IV–V, pp.1337–1342](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/Learning%20Through%20Noticing%20Field%202014_c1214d62-9429-4357-b47a-4195dae00881.pdf).

My interpretation is that information is not a homogeneous input. Recording a number, recognising its relevance, calculating a comparison and trusting its implication are distinct tasks. Giving a summary can alter several simultaneously. Attention was not independently manipulated while holding arithmetic, recommendation and timing fixed. Farmers also knew a summary would arrive, which could change their incentive to calculate independently. An explanation that sounds psychological can therefore describe a rational division of cognitive work between grower and extension service.

This does not erase the evidence for attention: ignorance was input-specific and recorded before later behaviour. It does prevent treating the paper as proof of a stable, innate farmer trait. A useful discriminating experiment would separately vary salience and computational assistance at a common date, with measured recall and subsequent input choices. The existing experiment cannot answer that question.

The labour boundary matters too. Carefully cutting, sorting and weighing pods are activities, not frictionless decisions. Researchers supplied inputs and a return floor for trials; routine cultivation remained with farmers. The evidence does not identify permanent income gains, a return to paid advisory labour, or the burden on unpaid household helpers. Continued customary spacing is an informative comparison, rather than an instance of universal resistance to change.

## PSY-C03: Social learning requires social work

Malawi supplies three related tests, not three independent replications. In a 120-village extension experiment, incentives to ordinary peer communicators increased experimentation, communication and downstream adoption. Without rewards, appointing intermediaries alone produced weak diffusion. The design measured knowledge and observable farming practices as well as reported contact. However, five peer farmers were compared with one lead farmer, so an “ordinary identity” effect cannot be cleanly separated from reach and numbers. **PSY-K07.** [BenYishay and Mobarak, §§2,5–7](https://spinup-000d1a-wp-offload-media.s3.amazonaws.com/faculty/wp-content/uploads/sites/45/2021/11/Benyishay_Mobarak_Malawi.pdf).

A separate 200-village experiment held the number of trained seeds at two and changed their selection. Central-network targeting improved pit-planting diffusion relative to extension-worker selection. The roughly three-point adoption-rate advantage was significant in year two, not year three. After three years, 45% of benchmark villages had no non-seed adopter. This supports targeting as a contribution, not necessity: other benchmark villages did diffuse, and most farmers still did not adopt. **PSY-K08.** [Beaman et al., January 2021 manuscript, pp.5–15 and §4](https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/~lab823/BBMM_Jan2021.pdf).

The network study’s two-contact results and knowledge measures fit reinforcement in learning. They also fit greater exposure, repeated demonstration or differing communicator effort. “Complex contagion” remains a supported interpretation rather than a uniquely observed mental process. Expensive network collection was itself part of the configuration. A cheap universally scalable algorithm was not demonstrated. **PSY-K10.** [Same manuscript, §5](https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/~lab823/BBMM_Jan2021.pdf).

The gender-reservation analysis uses 143 villages from the larger 2009–11 experiment underlying the incentive paper. Female-reserved roles received lower recipient ratings and lower training attendance despite comparable measured communicator knowledge and own use. Yet a precise pooled penalty in downstream adoption or yields did not follow. Some female-recipient subgroup differences were negative at weaker significance levels. Unreserved roles were not identical to an exclusively male treatment. **PSY-K09.** [BenYishay, Jones, Kondylis and Mobarak, §2.7 and Tables 3–7](https://spinup-000d1a-wp-offload-media.s3.amazonaws.com/faculty/wp-content/uploads/sites/45/2019/10/GenderMalawi.pdf).

These observations challenge two simple stories. Farmers do not automatically imitate any available local model: someone must experiment, explain, answer questions and arrange contact. But negative social perceptions do not mechanically determine final uptake either. A chain can lose strength at one stage and recover elsewhere through informal contact, other sources or compensating effort. The last possibilities are hypotheses, not mechanisms established by the gender experiment.

The relevant institution is an extension service with limited staff, choosing intermediaries and allocating rewards. Lead-farmer criteria privileged literacy, status and resources; peer selection broadened those criteria. Gendered reception was measured within that assignment system, not established as an immutable characteristic of Malawian society.

The labour implication is concrete but bounded: communication is productive work whose incentives matter. Pit construction, weeding and residue handling also reorganise farm work. These experiments do not establish an extension-worker replacement rate, a female earnings premium or the distribution of benefits to landless labourers. Household permission to accept a role and the cost of refusing were not adequately measured.

## PSY-C04: Hearing benefit is not the same as keeping an aid

The 2017 Indiana trial randomised service models using the same digital hearing-aid hardware. Among 154 completers, both audiology best practices and consumer-selected presets improved major hearing-benefit measures relative to placebo programming. Satisfaction and preliminary keep intention differed; logged daily use did not differ significantly across arms. The commonly quoted 81%, 55% and 36% figures are preliminary keep intentions, not final retention. Subsequent optional fitting changed the comparison. **PSY-K11.** [Humes et al., Methods and Results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5597084/).

Price was not randomised. Earlier participants paid $3,600 and later participants $600. Although many benefit measures showed no price association, 17 of 20 final returners had paid the higher price. Neither observation identifies price’s causal effect. **PSY-K12.** [Same study, Trial Design and final returns](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5597084/). The speech-test calibration was subsequently corrected from +3 to +1 dB signal-to-noise ratio; the correction is preserved rather than silently ignored. [Published erratum](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6808309/).

A separate analysis of 2004–08 Indiana volunteers compared 105 purchasers with 34 nonpurchasers. Twenty-one purchasers returned aids; only fifteen returners completed outcome measures. Perceived difficulty and expectations distinguished purchase groups, while returners experienced poorer aided outcomes. These are selected observational associations, not population abandonment rates or identified effects of expectations. **PSY-K14.** [Humes 2021, Participants and Results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8182629/).

Wallhagen’s independent qualitative evidence adds what those trials cannot. Ninety-one Bay Area dyads were interviewed separately at baseline, then followed at three and twelve months. Participants connected visible aids with ageing, disability and perceived competence; some discounted stigma or found supportive relationships made it less consequential. These meanings were elicited, not invented from refusal. Nevertheless, retrospective accounts and selected help-seekers cannot identify how much de-stigmatisation would change uptake. **PSY-K13.** [Wallhagen 2010, Methods and Results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2904535/).

The synthesis should therefore keep three questions apart: does amplification help the person’s task, does the delivery arrangement support operation, and is the resulting experience worth retaining in their actual social setting? Acoustic benefit, maintenance effort, identity, money and relationships can coexist. No single one needs to be relabelled the “real” reason.

Applicability is narrow. The trial was predominantly White, educated and relatively affluent, with language and cognitive eligibility rules. It does not represent all disabled people, severe hearing loss, Deaf cultural identity or people facing major dexterity barriers. Partners also do unpaid communication work, while wearers learn insertion, cleaning and battery management. Self-service changes task allocation, but the studies do not measure professional job losses or user employment gains.

This case particularly weakens the assumption that non-adoption reveals an irrational refusal of a universally beneficial device. Returning a poorly fitting or insufficiently useful device can be an informed decision. Conversely, subjective concerns are not automatically irrelevant just because a laboratory or clinic measure improves.

## PSY-C05: Bicycles within school-access institutions

The Bihar and Zambia episodes are separate populations and interventions. Their percentages are not pooled.

Bihar’s 2006 programme supplied Rs2,000 through schools for eligible girls to buy bicycles, with receipts and certification. Comparing age-14–15 girls with older cohorts, boys and neighbouring Jharkhand, the adjusted estimate was a 5.2-point increase in age-appropriate grade-9 enrolment/completion. This measures exposure and schooling, not directly observed riding. The estimate falls materially with household controls. Distance heterogeneity and a near-zero grade-8 placebo support an access channel but cannot separate travel time, safety, peer travel and social norms. **PSY-K15–16.** [Muralidharan and Prakash 2017, pp.326–345, Tables 2 and 8](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~kamurali/papers/Published%20Articles/Publisher%20Formatted%20Versions/Cycling%20to%20School%20%28AEJ%20Applied%202017%29.pdf).

A later reanalysis associates stronger effects with all-weather road connectivity. However, its road variable concerns access to health facilities, serving as a proxy for school routes. It is not randomised; an absent indicator still allows tracks or seasonal roads. An imprecise estimate in unconnected villages cannot prove road necessity. This is a reanalysis of the same policy and main survey, not independent corroboration. **PSY-K17.** [Seebacher 2022, pp.4–10 and results tables](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/wp-2022-382-seebacher-gender-education-gap-india.pdf).

Zambia randomised 100 schools: 25 received a package with a nominal maintenance payment, 20 a free version, and 55 served as controls. Eligible girls were already enrolled in grades 5–7 and lived more than three kilometres away. Both versions included trained mechanics, supervisory committees, primary school-use conditions and a four-year resale prohibition. At one year, tables report a 35.08-minute shorter one-way commute against 103.77 control minutes, and 0.29 fewer absent days in the previous week against 1.01 control days. **PSY-K18.** [Fiala et al., May 2025 manuscript, design and Tables 1/4](https://www.nishithprakash.com/s/Wheels_of_Change.pdf). The final journal abstract rounds these to 34% and 29%; its full text was not acquired. [Final article abstract](https://doi.org/10.1093/ej/ueaf122).

The May 2025 Zambia manuscript reports selected gains in self-reported agency, but general mobility and pooled aspirations are imprecise. The pooled aspirations coefficient is 0.07 (SE 0.05) in Table 3 and 0.06 with corrected p-values .44/.50 in Table A.11. Fee/free contrasts do not establish a payment-induced ownership effect: aggregate empowerment’s between-arm p-value is .13. Multiple-testing corrections and specifications weaken an undifferentiated empowerment conclusion. **PSY-K19–20.** [May 2025 manuscript, Tables 3 and A.11](https://www.nishithprakash.com/s/Wheels_of_Change.pdf).

The [final Oxford abstract](https://academic.oup.com/ej/article-abstract/136/679/2531/8317231) says educational aspirations improved. This assertion differs from the inspected manuscript’s pooled tables; it is not resolved by the commute/absence rounding comparison. The final tables, outcome definitions and pooled versus arm-specific interpretation were not inspected. No changed published estimate or positive pooled aspirations effect is inferred. Both versions describe the same trial.

Randomisation supports effects on the recorded questionnaire indices, but the mechanism remains underdetermined. Pre/post questions and multiplicity corrections cannot separate interview demand or asset salience from durable agency or norm change; the behavioural games were discontinued and supply no confirmation. A functioning mobility package can alter daily constraints, experiences and reported control, but which component does what is unresolved. Maintenance, public eligibility and custody conditions are part of the intervention. A bicycle-only intervention, unrestricted cash and a safer walking route were not separately randomised. [May 2025 manuscript, §§4.4.3 and 6.3](https://www.nishithprakash.com/s/Wheels_of_Change.pdf).

The person who rides is also not always the person controlling the asset. Household sharing, school oversight and resale restrictions coexist with benefits. Reported agency is therefore not unrestricted ownership. Zambia’s enrolled-grade and distance rules omit out-of-school girls; the school sample also excludes urban and some very remote schools. The inspected sampling passages do not establish a disability or cycling-ability exclusion rule. Representation and outcomes for girls unable to use a standard bicycle, and route accessibility, remain unestablished. Distance heterogeneity warns that reducing a barrier need not remove it. [May 2025 manuscript, §3.3 and Appendix B, pp.9 and 74–75](https://www.nishithprakash.com/s/Wheels_of_Change.pdf).

School participation is a possible input to later opportunities, not evidence of an occupational transition. Zambia measured some time allocation, with fewer income-generating activities but no clear changes in most other categories. Adult earnings, credentials, hiring discrimination and durable control of household gains remain unobserved. That limit should survive any later synthesis about human work.

## What the parent causal account should retain

The strongest common conclusion is institutional and process-specific. Behavioural mechanisms sometimes improve explanatory power because evidence measures intermediate beliefs, knowledge, contact or task experience. They do not displace economic constraints. Timing alters the practical opportunity to act; summaries alter interpretive work; rewards alter communication labour; fitting alters usability; school institutions alter mobility and control.

Several attractive universal claims fail these tests: one trial use need not create persistent adoption; respected or familiar peers need not transmit knowledge automatically; measured negative identity judgements need not yield a precise final adoption penalty; paying need not produce demonstrably greater attachment; higher use need not mean greater task benefit. Conversely, a failure to reject an effect is not evidence of equivalence or no mechanism.

The graph consequently contains scoped contributions and hypotheses, with no `depends-on` edges. Its separate Bihar and Zambia nodes preserve different institutional packages; its stigma and road links remain explicitly uncertain. It should not acquire transitive necessity through integration. The comparative record supports asking who can access, operate, decline, repair and control a technology, while leaving broad labour-market consequences and psychological universals unclaimed.
