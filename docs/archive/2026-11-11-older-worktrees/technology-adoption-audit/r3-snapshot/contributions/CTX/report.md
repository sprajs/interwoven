# CTX — Household textiles, restricted alternatives and unpaid work

This targeted investigation responds to review finding P2-SP03. It adds **CTX-C01**, a post-1949 Guanzhong episode, and **CTX-X01**, a comparison with the earlier Chinese textile case IND-C07. Its contribution is to separate three explanations of persistence: an older process performs a valued task; the proposed substitute is difficult to obtain; or decision-makers can pass the costs of continued work to somebody else. These routes can operate together. The comparison does not turn either period into a test of every Chinese household.

## The episode and the evidence

Eyferth's original field investigation covered Gedatou village in Zhouzhi county and Danbei and Zhangli villages in Xingping county, in central Shaanxi's Guanzhong region. Across fourteen weeks in five visits during 2005–2011, the team conducted about 200 interviews with more than eighty villagers, mainly men and women born in 1920–1945. This is a retrospective local study supported by archival material, not a household adoption survey. **CTX-K01** records persistence in this population; it does not convert the author's wider geographical hypothesis into a national estimate. [Eyferth 2012, pp. 379–381](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/1B4DC63727682F1EEC958A62A4126757/S0020859012000521a.pdf/womens-work-and-the-politics-of-homespun-in-socialist-china-1949-1980.pdf).

The case interval is 1949–1980, with its strongest mechanism evidence concerning the procurement and collective-labour arrangements established in the mid-1950s and subsequent collective years. Later photographs are a coda: the captions distinguish a spinning demonstration from continued, specialised weaving. Neither proves that an unchanged production system survived generally after 1980. The original article was read in full, with close inspection of pp. 373–391; source access and exclusions are recorded in [READING_NOTES.md](evidence/READING_NOTES.md).

## A coupon was not a completed substitution

The procurement and rationing system introduced in 1954 allocated access to industrial cloth, while households still needed money to buy it. In Zhouzhi, interviewees described coupon resale and home production as a response to cash scarcity; cadres and newly married couples were reported exceptions. **CTX-K02** treats this as evidence for an access mechanism, not a preference revealed by market choice. [Eyferth 2012, pp. 377, 386–387](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/1B4DC63727682F1EEC958A62A4126757/S0020859012000521a.pdf/womens-work-and-the-politics-of-homespun-in-socialist-china-1949-1980.pdf).

This distinction changes the adoption question. An industrial substitute can exist and be administratively allocated without being an affordable option for its intended user. Comparing a loom with a factory's unit cost would miss the household's actual transaction: buying cloth could require sacrificing other necessities, while home production drew on a differently controlled resource, women's time. The relevant test is therefore access to equivalent clothing services at feasible household costs, including the operator's time and authority to refuse. It is not national factory output alone.

The interviews favour this explanation over an account based only on attachment to tradition. They do not identify the fraction of continued production caused by pricing, inadequate rations, garment durability or customary obligations. Retrospective testimony, different household needs and the lack of matched cash-rich and cash-poor households leave those contributions underdetermined.

## Whose cost made persistence feasible?

**CTX-K03** connects household clothing duties with collective agricultural obligations and unpaid textile work performed late in the day. The reported burden cannot be represented by a single household decision-maker. **CTX-K05** preserves room for action: retained cotton, concealed allocations and some commercial weaving helped sustain production, but interviewees also reported risk and limits on withdrawing labour from collective work. [Eyferth 2012, pp. 387–389](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/1B4DC63727682F1EEC958A62A4126757/S0020859012000521a.pdf/womens-work-and-the-politics-of-homespun-in-socialist-china-1949-1980.pdf).

The inference is narrower than saying that the state ordered women to weave. Official policy opposed household textile production. The proposed pathway is that public procurement, restricted spending capacity and claims on agricultural labour interacted with household demands for clothing. Operators managed the resulting conflict. An outcome can therefore be contrary to a stated policy goal while still being sustained by the institutions implementing that policy.

Nor does the evidence establish a uniform condition of helplessness. Negotiation, concealment, exchange and occasional sale are relevant actions, but observing them does not establish unrestricted entry to a profitable occupation. A source can document income in a niche while leaving unanswered who controlled the proceeds, who bore discovery risk and who could spare the necessary time.

## What the adverse case does not remove

The strongest rival deserves its own place. **CTX-K04** retains reported durability and social uses of cloth; these make equivalent-service comparison important. They do not establish that ritual gifts had to be handmade or that every woman preferred the work. Material usefulness and constrained alternatives can coexist. The decisive missing evidence is a comparison of equivalent products when users can afford the substitute and operators can refuse the work without losing essential support.

The wider policy and social history also prevents a uniform account of loss. Hershatter describes rural women's broadened social worlds and labour-model visibility, as well as continuing unequal burdens. Her account distinguishes early support for some textile cooperatives and the Great Leap's attempted reorganisation of domestic work from later household responsibility. **CTX-K06** and **CTX-K08** retain this counterevidence without claiming that those gains resolved the Guanzhong clothing constraint. [Hershatter 2019, pp. 12–18](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/D3FF69916AB8B12A27073722AF080C6F/S1557466019015365a.pdf/women-and-chinas-socialist-construction-1949-78.pdf).

This is a connected body of scholarship. Hershatter's cloth-access discussion explicitly cites Eyferth 2012 and 2015; her wider rural-labour account draws on her own earlier research, Gao and other scholars. Agreement in the cloth paragraph is consequently not a second independent observation of the access mechanism.

## Comparison with IND-C07 and the synthesis change

IND-C07 concerns c. 1870–1949 and a configuration in which industrial yarn could replace a spinning operation while household weaving continued. CTX-C01 concerns later procurement, household access and unpaid work. The first case's hybrid remains supported; the second adds an observed route through constrained substitution. Both rely on different sections of **the same Eyferth article**. They cannot be counted as independent corroboration, pooled into one output series, or used to project socialist-era institutions backward.

**CTX-K07** is the resulting bounded inference: persistence establishes that a task continued to be performed; it does not identify the reason, the beneficiary or the quality of the operator's livelihood. The revised synthesis should put the access and labour-allocation pathway beside the hybrid-input pathway, rather than compress both into a claim that older technology persisted because it remained a good fit.

For future-role Candidate D, this changes the evidential gate. Continued older practice can motivate an investigation into repair or adaptation demand. It cannot establish an accessible, voluntary or paid role. A stronger opportunity claim needs evidence of a payer, control over work and proceeds, access to materials and tools, and a feasible option to stop. This case supplies a counterexample to the inference from continued task performance to desirable independent work; it supplies no forecast about current repair employment.

No causal share, national adoption rate, net welfare balance or occupational-transition rate is estimated here. The remaining comparisons and source gaps are specified in [questions.md](questions.md).
