# CTX — unresolved questions and discriminating research

1. **Affordable substitution.** Did otherwise comparable Guanzhong households redeem more cloth coupons when their disposable cash rose, holding ration entitlement, family needs and cloth quality as constant as the records allow? Household accounts or repeated local interviews would distinguish access from a preference-only explanation. The present retrospective accounts support a pathway, not its marginal effect.
2. **Equivalent clothing services.** How did wear, warmth, repairability and care requirements differ between particular homespun and factory fabrics? Eyferth's local minimum-cloth calculation is interview based, not a textile test. Ritual exchange should not be translated into a requirement for handmade cloth unless that requirement is independently documented.
3. **Gender and authority.** Who decided to sell coupons, who controlled sale receipts and who could decline a night of textile work? Diaries, separate interviews with household members and production-team records could test the allocation mechanism. The current record establishes competing duties and uneven burdens without enumerating every household bargain.
4. **Commercial niches and entry.** Were households with successful cloth sales selected by cotton access, trade contacts, team position, age or available labour? What fraction could sustain sales, and whose income was it? The reported profitable niche is not a general employment transition; gross household receipts cannot stand in for individual compensation.
5. **Policy variation.** Compare early textile cooperatives, household production, relief weaving and attempted collective sewing services in dated locations. Early support and later suppression are not interchangeable observations. A newly discovered supportive institution would not by itself establish effective access to industrial substitutes.
6. **Distribution and recall.** Eyferth interviewed more than eighty people in three villages, not a random national population. Accounts recorded in 2005–2011 may reflect later moral and political interpretations. Labour-model visibility and women's attachment to changes in marriage/status must remain visible alongside exhaustion and scarcity. No simple welfare score should combine them.
7. **After the late 1970s.** The article's photographs distinguish a later spinning demonstration and niche weaving, but do not identify the timing or causes of household exit. Follow the same households through changes in cloth availability, prices, cash earnings and agricultural organisation before treating the end of spinning as a clean causal experiment.
8. **National quantities.** Reopen the original Xu Jianqing papers and the Supply and Marketing Cooperative records before using the article's 1960s output calculations. Printed pp. 378–379 move between 566 million metres, its 366-million commercial component and a later description of 366 million as produced cloth. Definitions, year and denominator need reconciliation. None of these totals, derived labour-day figures or national percentages is promoted to a CTX claim.
9. **Evidence lineage.** CTX-S01 and IND-S15 are the same 2012 article. Hershatter 2019 note 49 cites Eyferth 2012/2015 for cloth access, and note 40 shares Eyferth 2015 for early textile activity. Its broader rural-labour sections also draw on Hershatter 2011 and Gao 2006, which Eyferth cites. Identify original interviews/records before claiming independent confirmation; exact overlap is not reconstructed here.

## Access gaps

Original Chinese archival files and interview recordings were not acquired. The original Xu Jianqing studies were not independently inspected. A search located Gao's published “Silver Flower Contest” article, but the publisher route did not supply readable content; no detailed claim relies on its search metadata. Hershatter's 2006 Yale working-paper route redirected to an HTML programme page; that response is preserved as a failed acquisition, not a PDF or inspected paper. The accessible 2019 published chapter excerpt is used instead, with its own limits and lineage.

Direct downloads of the two used publisher PDFs returned HTTP 403. The complete Eyferth text was available and read through the web tool; relevant sections of Hershatter's published excerpt were similarly read. The access log distinguishes those successful readings from failed local file acquisition. No local original PDF hash is claimed.
