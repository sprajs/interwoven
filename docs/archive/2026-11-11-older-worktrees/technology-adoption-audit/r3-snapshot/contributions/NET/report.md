# Knowledge, copying and navigation as working systems

NET contribution, 5 November 2026. Six purposively selected cases; 15 inspected sources; 24 bounded claims. The comparison concerns particular producers, users and tasks, not civilisations ranked by inventiveness. Source IDs, access qualifications and claim-specific locators are in the accompanying ledgers.

The central result is that reproducibility and adoption belong to different units of analysis. A surviving printed object establishes an episode of production. A description of typesetting establishes that someone described a process. Neither establishes a profitable industry, a mass readership or displacement of copying. Conversely, the continued use of manuscripts can represent a working publishing system rather than technological incapacity. The cases below compare contemporary alternatives, identify complementary work, and separate demonstrated use from plausible mechanisms. They deliberately do not estimate a global frequency of successful adoption.

## NET-C01 — Making and buying paper around the Islamic Mediterranean, ca. 750–1500

Paper crossed Eurasia through multiple communities and was adapted to different materials and writing practices. Bloom places manufacturing in Baghdad by the late eighth century and the transfer of papermaking to Mediterranean Christians by the twelfth. This establishes a broad pathway, not an exact itinerary of named inventors or a single decisive battle. It also makes paper-supported manuscript cultures an outcome in their own right, centuries before European typography. **NET-K01**, [Bloom, pp. 51–54](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-44654-7_3).

For producers, knowing that fibres form sheets leaves practical problems: acquiring suitable material, cleaning it, controlling pulp, forming sheets, drying and finishing them. Barrett's reconstruction of European practice connects clean water, worn textile fibres and gelatin sizing to a sheet's usable properties. He explicitly warns that his account generalises uneven evidence, much of it recorded late. Consequently, this contribution treats craft skill and input quality as supported mechanisms, while declining to assign a continent-wide productivity coefficient to watermills or gelatin. **NET-K02**, [Barrett, “Introduction,” “Water,” and “Sizing”](https://paper.lib.uiowa.edu/european.php).

Use must also be separated from local manufacture. Bloom cites Italian watermarked paper in a Tunisian royal letter of 1350 and Ibn Marzuq's 1409 ruling permitting writing on Christian-made paper whose watermark imagery troubled some users. Imported supplies could sustain local writing even where local production contracted. Religious authority here helped resolve a concrete acceptability problem; describing religion as uniformly hostile to imported technology would miss the observed action. **NET-K03**, [Bloom, pp. 63–64](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-44654-7_3).

The rival explanations are input availability, manufacturing costs, trade access, and perceived suitability for particular texts. They could be distinguished by mill accounts, purchase prices, manuscript-paper provenance and task-specific usage. A religious objection alone predicts rejection regardless of price or supply; negotiated acceptance weakens that strong version. An imported-paper user, however, has not necessarily acquired production skills. The distribution network may substitute for local manufacturing capacity.

A modern materials study prevents overconfident reconstruction. Mahgoub and colleagues examined 228 papers; 133 lacked dates, and much of the dated material was eighteenth- or nineteenth-century. They explain why the language of a text does not establish where its paper was made and why finishing techniques need not identify a unique tradition. Its measurements cannot be projected backwards as a medieval adoption series. **NET-K04**, [Mahgoub et al., pp. 2–3, “Reference collection”](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1527462/1/2016%20Mahgoub%20HS.pdf). This case therefore supports an interconnected material economy, with weak evidence for the relative contribution of individual cost advantages.

## NET-C02 — Chinese woodblock and movable-type choices, 868–ca. 1850

The 868 *Diamond Sutra* records a named sponsor, Wang Jie, and a purpose of distribution benefiting his parents. Its survival establishes accomplished woodblock production and a religiously motivated commission, not the number of copies actually distributed. The sponsor's objective was not necessarily recovery of a printer's investment through sales. A privately financed religious edition and a commercial edition must therefore have different success measures. **NET-K05**, [International Dunhuang Programme, “Colophon from the Diamond Sutra”](https://idp.bl.uk/discover/learning/buddhism-on-the-silk-roads/articles/buddhism-on-the-ground/buddhist-texts-the-diamond-sutra/).

Shen Gua's account describes Bi Sheng's clay type in the Qingli period, 1041–1048. It specifies preparing and levelling type, maintaining multiple examples of common characters, and alternating two boards. Shen distinguishes small from large print runs when evaluating the method. This is unusually informative historical testimony about operational organisation. It is not an independently timed experiment, an authenticated surviving Bi Sheng print run, or evidence that every later printer faced the same economics. **NET-K06**, [Shen Gua, translated by Ya Zuo, appendix 3, pp. 22–24](https://asiacenter.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/2023-06/shengua_app3.pdf).

McDermott and Burke explain why woodblocks remained viable: scribes, cutters, printers and binders could work separately; prepared blocks could be used much later; and printing could use thin paper and comparatively modest premises. They also identify movable-type niches, including genealogies and imperial projects, rather than an unbroken movement toward typography. These are comparative historical interpretations, not matched price experiments. **NET-K07–K08**, [McDermott and Burke, introduction, pp. 11–15](https://hkupress.hku.hk/image/catalog/pdf-preview/9789888208081.pdf).

The resulting hypothesis is conditional. A publisher facing uncertain demand over several years may value a reusable page that can be printed when orders appear. Another producer issuing many changing pages may value reusable characters despite composition and sorting work. Inventory can reside in blocks, type or finished books; these are different commitments of storage, labour and money. That interpretation follows from the documented workflows, but relative profitability needs accounts for particular titles and workshops.

Character inventory could affect typesetting costs without determining the entire outcome. A decisive script-only explanation predicts little variation within a writing system; task-specific movable-type use is evidence against that sufficient-cause version. Equally, woodblock persistence does not prove universal superiority: wages, paper, text length, expected reprinting, quality requirements and access to trained workers could change the ranking. The case is thus diffusion of printing with selective uptake of alternative production methods, not absence of innovation. It also warns against drawing a genealogical arrow from East Asian type directly to Gutenberg merely because one was earlier; the inspected comparative scholarship regards the transmission evidence as inconclusive (pp. 18–24).

## NET-C03 — Korean metal printing and manuscript circulation, 1377–early nineteenth century

The BnF's surviving second volume of *Jikji* was printed with cast metal type at Heungdeok-sa in 1377; a woodblock version of the complete text dates to 1378. These adjacent episodes undermine a simple replacement sequence. They do not establish how many readers used either version, how widely the workshops' skills spread, or whether either edition earned money. **NET-K09**, [BnF, “What is Jikji?” and “A treasure of humanity”](https://www.bnf.fr/en/jikji-treasure-world-printing).

The later Chosŏn comparison changes the actor population from a Buddhist temple workshop to readers, copyists, families and publishing businesses. Park describes vernacular novels circulating in manuscript among elite women and the palace in the late seventeenth century, followed by commercial rental manuscripts from the mid-eighteenth century and overlapping woodblock editions. Manuscript circulation could expand through a business without being replaced immediately by print. **NET-K10**, [Park, pp. 22–24](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781000539578_A42450661/preview-9781000539578_A42450661.pdf).

Park also finds copying integral to obtaining newly imported Chinese books and to literary collections. Copies might contain selections and changes, making the desired object different from a mechanically identical edition. Her discussion of private uses of official printing resources and careers linking official editorial work with private printing complicates a complete state-versus-market separation. **NET-K11–K12**, [Park, pp. 25–28, especially p. 27 n. 16](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781000539578_A42450661/preview-9781000539578_A42450661.pdf).

These findings do not make access equal. A lender, palace reader, literatus seeking a newly arrived text, and lineage seeking commemoration faced different budgets and social permissions. Literary Sinitic and vernacular Korean also belonged to overlapping but unequal textual settings. The existence of an alphabet is therefore not a sufficient demand-side explanation: the relevant question is which people wanted which texts, in which language and material form, with what access to education and distribution. No literacy-rate series is inferred here from book survival.

A rival view attributes manuscript prevalence entirely to expensive printed books and suppressed commerce. It predicts conversion to print wherever purchase price falls or restrictions loosen. The observed uses of copying for selection, circulation and textual variation require an additional explanation. A useful test would compare the same work's manuscript and printed variants, rentals, prices and reader annotations. Preference cannot be read directly from survival: prestigious state editions were preferentially collected and catalogued, while much ordinary copying disappeared. The analytical outcome is durable coexistence and differentiated adoption, not a failed version of Europe's history.

## NET-C04 — European urban printing enterprises, 1450–1600

Dittmar's historical compilation identifies 205 cities adopting printing in 1450–1500. Table I compares these with 1,811 historical cities in the listed polities, defined through a database of places that reached 5,000 inhabitants at some time between 1000 and 1800. This is neither the fraction of Europeans who read nor a contemporaneous census of all settlements. The author's growth samples have different denominators. **NET-K13**, [Dittmar, pp. 1143–44, Table I and nn. 20–23](https://people.bu.edu/chamley/764-23/Dittmar.pdf).

The production system involved mobile trained printers, financing and a sufficiently stable customer base. A press could be technically operational while its proprietor waited to recover money tied up in paper and editions. Universities and political centres could offer customers, but proximity to technical expertise also mattered; the article reports that forty of the hundred largest European cities lacked presses in 1500. Its account also acknowledges uncertainty about when early casting methods reached their mature form. **NET-K14**, [Dittmar, pp. 1153–56 and n. 38](https://people.bu.edu/chamley/764-23/Dittmar.pdf).

Dittmar and Seabold investigate variation among already printing cities. Their working paper combines edition records with book prices, printer biographies and content classifications. They find local competition related to lower prices and different output, with transport costs keeping local conditions consequential. Their unit is generally an edition, not a copy sold or a reader taught. Their introductory and data-section edition totals differ, so this report does not treat either as a precise population count. **NET-K15**, [Dittmar and Seabold, pp. 3, 6–7, 12 and §5](https://cep.lse.ac.uk/pubs/download/dp1600.pdf).

The economic claims are stronger than a chronological story but remain conditional. Dittmar instruments early adoption with distance from Mainz; Dittmar and Seabold use printer deaths to study changes in competition and particular content. These strategies address selective location and reverse causation, but require assumptions about other channels through which geography or a printer's death could affect outcomes. Death can change expertise and contacts as well as competition. City population growth also includes migration and cannot be equated with national productivity or welfare. **NET-K16**, [Dittmar, §V.E](https://people.bu.edu/chamley/764-23/Dittmar.pdf), [Dittmar and Seabold, §§3, 5–7](https://cep.lse.ac.uk/pubs/download/dp1600.pdf).

The surviving interpretation is that organisation and content matter after a technical threshold has been crossed. A machine-level explanation predicts similar effects wherever a press operates. A complementary-system explanation predicts differences with finance, book demand, distribution and market structure. Europe supplies evidence for the latter, not a universal template against which other societies' timings should be graded. Broad claims of initially light regulation should likewise not be extended to every jurisdiction or the later history of censorship.

## NET-C05 — Ottoman permissions and Müteferrika's customers, 1588–1748

Schwartz's audit finds no inspected original for the alleged blanket bans attributed to Bayezid II and Selim I. By contrast, a surviving 1588 firman protects merchants selling printed Arabic books. This warrants rejecting the ban as an established explanation; it does not prove that every printer everywhere was unrestricted. It also prevents an Arabic-script printing history from erasing earlier Jewish, Armenian and other Ottoman printing. **NET-K17**, [Schwartz, pp. 1–2, 6–7](https://religionculturesociety.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/s1__schwartz_did-ottoman-sultans-ban-print.pdf).

The 1727 permission for Müteferrika is specific. It limits the permitted subject matter and accompanies a religious legal opinion requiring learned correctors. Permission, validation and production were connected: reproducing more copies could multiply errors as well as useful texts. The documents do not themselves establish the motives for every restriction or a previous general prohibition. **NET-K18**, [Schwartz, pp. 9–11](https://religionculturesociety.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/s1__schwartz_did-ottoman-sultans-ban-print.pdf).

Sabev reconstructs initial output from thirteen editions with reported print runs, totalling 8,200 copies, and three additional editions whose runs he estimates. His inspected probate inventory account records 2,981 copies remaining at Müteferrika's death. Subtracting stock from reconstructed output yields neither verified sales revenue nor readers: some books were gifts, initial runs are partly assumed, titles had different years on sale, and remaining copies differed in finishing. The evidence supports substantial circulation alongside unsold stock, not the familiar verdict of total commercial failure or an exact sales percentage. **NET-K19**, [Sabev, pp. 299–303](https://nec.ro/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/ORLIN_SABEV.pdf).

His reader evidence is also explicitly selected: 335 Istanbul probate inventories of people with askeri status and more than three books, dated 1724–1748; Müteferrika prints appear in sixteen. That is not sixteen out of all Istanbul readers, or a literacy estimate. Some owners were religious functionaries, directly complicating a claim that religious office entailed opposition. **NET-K20**, [Sabev, pp. 305–307](https://nec.ro/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/ORLIN_SABEV.pdf).

The relevant alternatives were manuscripts, imported printed books and this local press's particular titles. Demand, price, subject selection, proofreading authority and distribution are therefore better specified candidate mechanisms than a timeless Islamic attitude. A strong price explanation needs matched works and transaction prices; an inventory's valuation is not automatically its original sale price. A strong institutional explanation needs particular privileges, prosecutions or denied applications. Neither small output nor a licencing document supplies that missing comparison by itself. This case also shows why social uptake and commercial accounts must be investigated separately.

## NET-C06 — Indian Ocean pilot knowledge and instruments, ca. 1400–1560

The navigational system described in Arabic and Turkish sources combined seasonal routes, stellar observations and local environmental knowledge. Staples emphasises regional and transitional winds within the broad monsoon cycle: a two-arrow seasonal map is an inadequate operating manual. Route-specific knowledge therefore complements the ship and instruments rather than constituting an incidental cultural background. **NET-K21**, [Staples, §§2–3, pp. 2–5](https://www.academia.edu/111549511/Indian_Ocean_navigation_in_Islamic_sources_850_1560_CE).

Wooden star-altitude instruments could standardise measurements otherwise made with fingers. Magnetic compasses were integrated with existing stellar direction names, according to Staples. Neither observation establishes universal compass carriage. Tolmacheva characterises magnetic use as rare, without a fleet denominator. The disagreement leaves prevalence unresolved and argues against turning an available device into a universal prerequisite. **NET-K22**, [Staples, p. 4](https://www.academia.edu/111549511/Indian_Ocean_navigation_in_Islamic_sources_850_1560_CE), [Tolmacheva, paragraphs beginning “Where the Chinese compass…”](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/713561).

Nor should charts be presumed essential because they dominate later navigation. Tolmacheva contrasts the lack of maps associated with Ibn Majid's surviving instructions with Portuguese reports of chart use; verse could aid memorisation of routes. This is evidence of several representations of expertise, bounded by unequal survival. It is not proof that Indian Ocean sailors never used maps. **NET-K23**, [Tolmacheva, paragraphs beginning “It appears that early Portuguese sailors…” and “Curiously…”](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/713561).

The 1498 voyage journal provides an operational check on purely instrumental explanations. It reports the delivery of a pilot by Malindi's ruler and the fleet's departure with him on 24 April. It also reports da Gama's seizure of a court servant while demanding the promised pilots. Acquisition of useful knowledge occurred through diplomacy and coercion, not simply the purchase or invention of a superior instrument. The account's identification of the pilot as Christian should not be accepted uncritically; this contribution makes no claim that Ibn Majid piloted the fleet. **NET-K24**, [anonymous journal, Ravenstein translation, pp. 45–47](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/46440/old/46440-h/46440-h.htm).

A ship-arrival record tests operational capability but not safety across all voyages. A manual tests the existence of codified expertise but not every pilot's practice. To compare accuracy or adoption, one would need contemporary instrument descriptions, navigators' training and routes, plus failures and near misses. The present evidence supports hybridisation and reliance on people, while leaving comparative accuracy, carriage rates and fleet-level diffusion unmeasured. The commercial benefits of a crossing cannot erase the asymmetric risks and force involved in obtaining a guide.

## Comparative implications and limits

Across these cases, the strongest common mechanism is task-specific complementarity. A medium or instrument acquires practical value within a workflow: paper must suit ink and handling; type must be composed and corrected; books must reach readers; observations must become navigational decisions. These are supported interactions, not a single necessary chain. In the graph, compass and stellar practice are complements, not obligatory ancestors of successful ocean travel. Imported paper enables use without proving local papermaking, and manuscripts can complement the circulation of printed works.

Three hypotheses survive only in restricted form. First, cheaper replication can matter, but the price paid by a reader includes distribution, finance and selection of usable content. Second, institutions matter, but religious and state bodies can finance, validate, delimit or obstruct a particular practice; their direction cannot be assigned from a civilisational label. Third, skills matter, but the scarce unit can be a mobile craft worker or a pilot rather than an abstract invention that everybody can reproduce once informed of it. These are synthesis inferences from the scoped claims, not independently identified universal causes.

The principal negative evidence is coexistence: woodblock with type, manuscripts with commercial publishing, and local pilot knowledge with imported instruments. Coexistence does not by itself prove efficient choice; power, exclusion and restricted education may prevent alternatives. It does disprove any account in which the newer method automatically eliminates the older one once invented. Falsification requires specifying the actor and task where superiority should have predicted a switch.

Source criticism remains consequential. Tolmacheva's maritime essay includes a broad claim that the Islamic world resisted printing; it is not adopted here, because Schwartz's documentary critique makes that generalisation unsafe. Bloom's survey contains some causal conjectures about preference and mill diffusion which this contribution likewise does not promote to established facts. European econometric estimates and Ottoman probate counts answer different questions and cannot be compared as equivalent adoption rates. The main unresolved empirical deficits are matched production costs, ordinary readers and non-readers, transmission through unrecorded work, and the maritime practices of East African, South Asian and Southeast Asian crews poorly represented by the surviving textual corpus.


## Review correction: what the printing niches can test

NET-K08 rejects practical impossibility and uniform all-task choice. It does not reject a script-sensitive explanation of relative uptake. Angeles’s cost model accommodates the same genealogical and imperial niches through production scale, print runs and type material. Those observations therefore leave the stronger rival unresolved; the model itself does not identify historical causal weights. [NET-S16, January 2016 manuscript pp.17–22](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/120520/13/120520.pdf).
