# GHA-C02 — Northern public roads: changing paid recruitment beside persistent and disputed compulsion

**Period:** 1925–1937, with earlier construction context and explicitly separate PWD/political-road subepisodes. **Region:** Northern Territories of the Gold Coast; Makongo; Savelugu; Salaga; West Africa. **Domain:** road maintenance; public works; labour recruitment; colonial administration.

PWD recruitment changed in some places before convention ratification, while other road obligations persisted. Administrative strategies, worker alternatives, funding and local dissent interacted; scholarship differs on transition breadth.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/GHA/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** No road-technology invention; the change concerns recruitment and maintenance organization.

**feasibility:** Both compulsory service and hired gangs performed road tasks, with reported quality and recruitment problems.

**deployment:** Makongo bargaining in 1925 and Savelugu recruitment instruction in June 1930 identify particular changes before ratification.

**diffusion:** Pont Cháfer reconstructs other local shifts; Kunkel documents continuing mechanisms of compulsion. No common labour-days denominator.

**scaling:** Increasing traffic and year-round maintenance strained short obligatory service; budgets and worker supply mattered.

**non-adoption/niche:** Residual compulsory cleaning/weeding persisted even on PWD roads; political-road regimes were heterogeneous.

**displacement/reversal:** Some recruitment shifted toward voluntary paid gangs, without a single verified end date for all compelled road tasks.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Subepisode A: paid PWD maintenance/reconstruction around Makongo, Salaga and Savelugu. Subepisode B: political/public roads maintained through chiefs and district authorities in the 1930s.

**task:** Keep motor routes usable, including rainy-season repairs; reduce recruitment costs or coercion depending on the actor.

**population:** PWD applicants/recruits, chiefs and local officers in cited disputes; separate communities and administrative units under political-road arrangements. These are not one tracked cohort.

**interval:** Main observations 1925, June 1930 and mid/late 1930s; construction before 1925 is context.

**contemporary alternatives:** compulsory paid recruitment; paid voluntary recruitment at a sufficiently attractive offer; obligatory unpaid cleaning; contracted or departmental gangs; reduced maintained mileage or deteriorating roads

**adoption measure:** Dated disputes, recruitment instructions, wage comparisons, budget assumptions and reported expenditures; no estimated share of workers or days free from coercion.

**observation limits:** Original archives mediated through scholarship; PWD roads were a minority of northern mileage and different tasks had different rules. Kunkel/Pont Cháfer disagree on interpretation and extent.

## Actors

**chooser:** PWD and colonial authorities commissioned work; chiefs and district/provincial officers negotiated or enforced recruitment; workers supplied or withheld labour within uneven constraints.

**payer:** PWD/public budgets, later grants and local treasuries; compelled workers and their households also bore time and subsistence costs. Subsidy assumptions do not establish actual payment.

**operator maintainer:** Recruited road workers, hired gangs and residents obliged to weed or clean; skilled supervisors and road engineers where available.

**affected nonchooser:** Workers subject to recruitment or residual duties, households losing farm time, communities taxed or passed by, and road users facing deterioration.

**ability to refuse:** A June 1930 Savelugu instruction rejects forced PWD supply and advertises paid work; earlier desertion could be punished. Residual tasks and political-road practices retained coercive powers. No universal consent inference from wages.

## Labour

**tasks:** Construction/maintenance, drainage, repair, cleaning and weeding; motor traffic altered timing and quality demands.

**entry barriers:** Recruitment authority, acceptable wages and food costs, agricultural calendar, location and alternative southern employment; exact skills/selection distribution unavailable.

**bargaining ownership:** Workers’ alternatives and chiefs’ protests could change terms, while public authorities retained coercive and fiscal powers. Chiefs were neither uniform intermediaries nor uniformly autonomous protectors.

**transition:** Specific changes precede 1931 ratification; further administrative and financing changes occurred during the 1930s. Different tasks did not change simultaneously.

**distribution:** Freedom from compelled supply could improve for particular recruits while the Depression weakened southern earnings; no total welfare or gender-distribution estimate.

**evidence limit:** No road-linked panel of contracts, sanctions, payments and hours; no aggregate compulsory-work percentage or universal abolition date.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: worker outside options; recruitment bargaining; maintenance quality and timing; administrative classification; fiscal constraints; legal regulation and uneven implementation

Payment is not proof of free recruitment; pre-ratification changes do not exclude earlier external pressure; planned subsidy and actual payroll are different evidence.

## Evidence links

- [GHA-K06](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#gha-k06): The 1925 Makongo wage/contract dispute and a June 1930 Savelugu instruction rejecting forced supply of PWD workers show local changes in paid recruitment before Britain’s 1931 convention ratification, without identifying the independent influence of international pressure or anticipatory policy change.
- [GHA-K07](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#gha-k07): Pont Cháfer connects changes in northern PWD recruitment to maintenance demands and workers’ alternatives; the post-1929 fall in southern wages could make northern offers attractive without a general improvement in workers’ outside options.
- [GHA-K08](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#gha-k08): Northern political-road labour changed unevenly: Kunkel identifies arrangements and mid-1930s budget assumptions preserving compulsion, while Pont Cháfer emphasizes earlier dissent, district payments and later grant-financed paid gangs; their evidence does not establish one uniform transition date or compulsory-labour rate.
