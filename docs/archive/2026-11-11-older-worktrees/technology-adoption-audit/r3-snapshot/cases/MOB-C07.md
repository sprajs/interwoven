# MOB-C07 — Singapore container terminals: state planning and scarce waterfront land

**Period:** 1972–2000, with the 2000 official account retrospectively describing earlier choices. **Region:** Singapore; Southeast Asia. **Domain:** ports; container logistics; land; state investment.

Singapore’s official terminal-opening account makes port expansion an organizational and land-allocation project as well as a handling technology; its celebratory perspective limits causal and distributional conclusions.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/MOB/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Container technology already operated elsewhere; Singapore’s case is adoption and adaptation.

**feasibility:** Container berths operated from 1972 according to the official account.

**deployment:** Tanjong Pagar berths were commissioned and conventional berths progressively converted.

**diffusion:** No adoption denominator; the account describes growing terminal use and shipping-line conversion.

**scaling:** Pasir Panjang planning and construction culminated in a 2000 opening, with workforce and information-service complements.

**non-adoption/niche:** Alternative waterfront uses competed with port development; no systematic sample of rejected port options inspected.

**displacement/reversal:** Waterfront use changed; resident relocation, losses and compensation are not measured in the speech.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Tanjong Pagar container berths from 1972 and Pasir Panjang expansion opened in 2000.

**task:** Accommodate container shipping and port services within scarce urban waterfront space.

**population:** Government/port authority as adopter and official narrator; shipping lines and workforce discussed, residents not sampled.

**interval:** 1972–2000; exact decision-date controversies before 1972 are not resolved here.

**contemporary alternatives:** continued conventional cargo handling; other uses of waterfront land; other port locations or staged expansion (not comparatively costed in the inspected source)

**adoption measure:** Commissioning and expansion chronology in a government speech; no consistent throughput denominator used.

**observation limits:** Retrospective political self-presentation; claimed productivity and future designs are not independently audited in this case.

## Actors

**chooser:** Singapore government and Port of Singapore Authority, according to the speech.

**payer:** Port/state investment is implied; exact financing and ultimate cost incidence are not stated adequately in the inspected paragraphs.

**operator maintainer:** Terminal workforce and operators of equipment/information services; maintenance financing and unpaid work are unmeasured.

**affected nonchooser:** Users of competing waterfront space, adjacent residents and workers facing task change; their accounts are absent.

**ability to refuse:** Unknown for residents and individual workers. State selection of port land does not establish popular consent or an effective option to refuse displacement.

## Labour

**tasks:** Shift from manual cargo handling toward crane operations, information management and related services described by government.

**entry barriers:** Workforce upgrading claimed; actual training access, credential requirements and exclusion unknown.

**bargaining ownership:** State/port authority control is visible; union and worker bargaining evidence absent.

**transition:** Long planning horizon documented, but no worker-cohort transition measured.

**distribution:** No disaggregated incomes, displacement compensation or health outcomes.

**evidence limit:** An operationally successful port and official pride do not establish net benefits for every land user or worker.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: state coordination; land opportunity cost; long planning horizon; workforce upgrading; information services; complementary capacity

The primary source is the government’s success narrative. It cannot establish that stated planning choices were uniquely necessary or that affected communities benefited.
