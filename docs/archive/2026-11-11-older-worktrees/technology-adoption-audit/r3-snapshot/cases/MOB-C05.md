# MOB-C05 — Early electric vehicles: a service system fails, specialized uses persist

**Period:** 1897–1925, distinguishing EVC 1897–1907 from later utility/fleet efforts. **Region:** United States; New York; Europe (limited task comparisons). **Domain:** electric vehicles; urban transport; maintenance; standards.

Early electric mobility offered a managed cab-service configuration and later fleet niches; EVC failure and the wider private-car outcome require different explanations.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/MOB/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** No first inventor claimed; propulsion and accumulator development precede this episode.

**feasibility:** Electric cabs operated in New York in 1897; repeatable service did not establish profitability.

**deployment:** Fleet ownership retained maintenance and operational control within a service enterprise.

**diffusion:** Expansion to multiple cities is documented qualitatively, but no consistent adopter denominator is supplied.

**scaling:** EVC attempted system expansion and suffered business failures; later utilities coordinated EV promotion from 1909.

**non-adoption/niche:** Task-specific fleets persisted in the comparative literature, including commercial delivery and special services; not equivalent to winning the general private-car market.

**displacement/reversal:** Regional EVC ventures failed around 1901–1902; parent default in 1907. Private gasoline cars expanded while electric applications survived selectively.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** A: New York and affiliated EVC electric-cab service with retained ownership. B: utility-supported US fleet/charging efforts 1909–1925. C: general private-car displacement with European specialty fleets used only as bounded negative comparisons.

**task:** Sell reliable local passenger trips or perform predictable fleet work; private touring is a different task.

**population:** Operating companies, utilities and selected fleet applications in historical scholarship. No common statistical population across subepisodes.

**interval:** 1897–1925; EVC operational and parent-company failures explicitly separated.

**contemporary alternatives:** horse-drawn cabs; gasoline vehicles; steam cars; electric fleet operation versus privately maintained electric cars; fixed-route urban transit (not separately evaluated)

**adoption measure:** Documented operation, expansion/failure dates and associations’ activities; no harmonized market-share series used.

**observation limits:** Kirsch article summarizes a dissertation; early cost reports self-reported and retrospectively evaluated. Mom compares tasks and regions; this contribution does not reconstruct his entire archival base.

## Actors

**chooser:** Entrepreneurs chose service versus sales models; utilities chose promotional support; fleet managers and wealthy early private buyers chose equipment.

**payer:** Company investors and fares, vehicle buyers and participating utilities; distribution of financial losses not fully reconstructed.

**operator maintainer:** Specialist fleet workers, drivers, battery attendants and private owners; maintaining lead-acid batteries could not be omitted.

**affected nonchooser:** Employees of failed firms, pedestrians and urban residents exposed to competing transport systems; health burdens not quantified.

**ability to refuse:** Buyer choice among vehicle types does not measure worker bargaining or residents’ ability to refuse street hazards. Precise contract and exit conditions are unknown.

## Labour

**tasks:** Driving, vehicle/battery maintenance, charging, dispatch and utility coordination; service ownership kept some tasks with specialists.

**entry barriers:** Capital, technical maintenance and access to charging; gender/status imagery discussed in scholarship but not used here as quantified causal evidence.

**bargaining ownership:** EVC retained vehicle assets; dispersed private buyers bore more operational responsibility. Worker bargaining contracts not inspected.

**transition:** Company survival dates and technology/task comparisons; no evidence of which workers moved into gasoline vehicle employment or how long adjustment took.

**distribution:** Some fleet tasks remained viable; no total employment or household-welfare estimate.

**evidence limit:** Cannot infer profitable universal electrification, worker gains, or spontaneous consumer preferences solely from the dominant technology.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: fleet versus individual maintenance; battery duty-cycle constraints; business organization and finance; utility coordination; charging standards; differentiated tasks

General gasoline advantages were real. Neither an electric niche nor a counterfactual earlier EV association establishes that electric cars could profitably dominate all travel.

## Evidence links

- [MOB-K15](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mob-k15): The 1897 New York electric-cab venture deliberately sold transport service while retaining vehicles and specialist control, rather than merely selling cars to inexperienced owners; operation was demonstrated before profitability was established.
- [MOB-K16](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mob-k16): Kirsch’s history attributes the EVC’s breakdown to a bundle of production, financial and organizational problems, and distinguishes early operating-company failures from the parent’s default in December 1907.
- [MOB-K17](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mob-k17): Mom’s comparative history distinguishes early battery-related failures from later electric fleets that worked under specific duty cycles and disciplined maintenance; it rejects the electric starter as a sufficient explanation for all electric-vehicle displacement.
- [MOB-K18](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mob-k18): The Electric Vehicle Association of America, established in 1909, promoted utility fleets, charging facilities, off-peak rates and a common charging plug; this was an organized attempt to build complements after early electric-car setbacks.
