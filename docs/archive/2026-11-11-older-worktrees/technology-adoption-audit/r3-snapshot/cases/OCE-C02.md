# OCE-C02 — Gunditjmara Tae Rak: hydraulic modification, disabling and negotiated restoration

**Period:** c.6600 cal BP–present archaeological context; restoration episode 1954–2010. **Region:** Gunditjmara Country, southwest Victoria, Australia. **Domain:** aquaculture; water engineering; land governance.

A dated feature demonstrates changing trap construction. The modern episode tracks pastoral drainage, a failed restoration trial, land return and a negotiated weir, without assuming uninterrupted ancient operation or restored historic output.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/OCE/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Unknown; c.6600 cal BP dates concern one channel sequence, not invention of aquaculture.

**deployment:** Material channels/walls and documented fishery; modern portable nets/traps persisted alongside changing hydraulics.

**diffusion:** Regional complexes known but no dated adopter/area denominator.

**displacement/reversal:** 1954 drainage sharply restricted functioning; opposed by Gunditjmara Elders according to dossier.

**feasibility:** Modern hydrological studies and failed trial precede 2010 completed weir.

**scaling:** No inferred aquaculture production scale from mapped stonework or restored water alone.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Ancient comparator: F10 channel and later walls at Muldoons. Main modern episode: Tae Rak drainage, regulator/weir and compatible trap elevations.

**task:** Capture/hold fish, especially kooyang; restore cultural water landscape while limiting impacts on upstream farms.

**population:** Ancient builders/operators at one feature, numerically unknown; modern Gunditjmara restoration institutions, local workers and neighboring landholders are a separate observed population.

**interval:** Channel c.6600 cal BP and later walls c.600–800 cal BP; drainage completed 1954; proposals/trials from 1970s–1980s; construction January–May 2010.

**contemporary alternatives:** Portable nets and traps documented as continuing fishing methods; Drainage and pastoral use favored by some landholders; Earlier trial weir, documented as unsuccessful; Higher lake-water restoration constrained by adjacent property effects

**adoption measure:** Stratified site evidence and completed project chronology; 52.4 m AHD design setting; no catch, fishery-user or worker denominator.

**observation limits:** Mixed epochs are kept separate; archaeological survival does not prove continuous use; hydraulic availability does not count operating traps.

## Actors

**chooser:** Ancient decision structure unknown; modern Gunditjmara advocated restoration, agencies authorized works and adjoining owners influenced feasible water level.

**payer:** Modern Indigenous Land Corporation planning funding and state construction resources; federal employment support reported; ancient labor/resource contributions unknown.

**operator maintainer:** Gunditjmara fishers; restoration designers, contractors and trained Indigenous construction workers; ongoing staffing not quantified.

**affected nonchooser:** Gunditjmara users affected by imposed drainage; upstream farmers affected by retained water; ecology and downstream uses.

**ability to refuse:** Dossier records objections to drainage that did not stop it. Restoration required consultation and negotiated constraint; no assumption of equal vetoes.

## Labour

**tasks:** Channel/wall/trap building and upkeep inferred from features; modern design, construction, administration and training documented.

**entry barriers:** Land/water access, ecological and construction knowledge; modern tasks used on-site training and supervision. Ancient apprenticeship and exclusion rules unknown.

**bargaining ownership:** Ancient tenure not established; return of lake to Gunditjmara in March 2008 changed control before construction, but neighboring property interests continued to constrain level.

**transition:** 1954 loss of operating water followed by decades of proposals; 2010 construction jobs documented, not longitudinal worker careers.

**distribution:** Specific Indigenous participation in construction reported; no pay, retention or household-benefit breakdown.

**evidence limit:** Cannot infer egalitarian ancient aquaculture, total fish output, restoration profitability or sustained employment.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: access to land and water; environmental complements; property negotiation; training and procurement; persistence through adapted methods

Sources on restoration share a project lineage. Increased retained water is distinct from observed operation of every trap and from historical livelihood restoration.

## Evidence links

- [OCE-K05](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#oce-k05): At Muldoons Trap Complex, sediment stratigraphy and AMS charcoal dates support an early excavated channel around 6,600 cal BP and a later wall-building episode around 600–800 cal BP, with an intervening hiatus.
- [OCE-K06](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#oce-k06): Tae Rak’s enlarged drainage works, completed in 1954 despite recorded opposition, sharply curtailed the water conditions for lake-margin trapping; this was institutional and hydrological disabling rather than a demonstrated voluntary choice of a more productive fishery.
- [OCE-K07](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#oce-k07): The 2010 cultural weir restored retained water under a negotiated 52.4 m AHD setting intended to limit flooding of upstream farms; the nomination says this could reactivate most trap complexes but excluded Muldoons at that level.
- [OCE-K08](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#oce-k08): Gunditj Mirring reports supported Aboriginal recruitment, training and supervision during January–May 2010 weir construction, including construction and some administrative work.
