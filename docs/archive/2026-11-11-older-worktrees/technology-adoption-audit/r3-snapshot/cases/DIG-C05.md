# DIG-C05 — Kenyan mobile money: simple phones, agent networks and welfare estimates

**Period:** 2007–2014; separate 2024 national survey. **Region:** Kenya. **Domain:** payments; mobile telecommunications; financial institutions.

M-PESA’s operational complements explain how ordinary phones carry transfers; welfare estimates depend on rollout assumptions, while 2024 adoption uses a distinct adult denominator.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/DIG/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** SMS money transfer built on prior practices; no Kenyan global-first claim.

**feasibility:** Deposit, account transfer and cash redemption architecture documented.

**deployment:** M-PESA launched 2007; agents perform local conversion.

**diffusion:** Early user-household surveys and 2024 current mobile-money use; incompatible denominators not spliced.

**scaling:** Agent-network expansion observed; quantified contribution conditional.

**non-adoption/niche:** Phone/ID barriers and withdrawal delays; existing banks/cash remain alternatives.

**displacement/reversal:** No complete displacement of banks, cash or other payment channels established.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** 2007–2010 SMS M-PESA transfer/cash-agent configuration; 2008–2014 household panel welfare study; separate 2024 all-provider current-use survey.

**task:** Send/redeem money, store value and manage financial needs; welfare outcomes include consumption and occupation.

**population:** Early Kenyan user households in authors’ panel; 2014 estimation sample 1608 households outside Nairobi/far-north omissions; 2024 access/use among adults 18+.

**interval:** Launch 2007; survey 2008/2009; early agent changes 2008–2010 and outcomes 2014; later snapshot 2024.

**contemporary alternatives:** Cash carried by people; Existing remittance/payment services; Banks and ATMs; Other mobile-money providers, not separately estimated here

**adoption measure:** Early household ever-use and reported withdrawal delays; welfare model estimates, not adoption counts; 2024 current mobile-money use 82.3% adults 18+, no fixed activity window.

**observation limits:** M-PESA provider, all-provider mobile money, household and individual measures distinct. Panel attrition and assumed rollout exogeneity; 2024 cross-sectional usage does not replicate earlier poverty effect.

## Actors

**chooser:** Users choose transfers/accounts; Safaricom and agents choose rollout/operation; regulators shape eligibility; choice constrained by practical alternatives.

**payer:** Users pay transfer/redemption charges; agents commit cash/e-float; institutional/provider investments. No complete incidence estimate.

**operator maintainer:** Agent proprietors/staff, carrier infrastructure workers and banking institutions. Informal family assistance not quantified.

**affected nonchooser:** Remittance recipients, dependants and excluded people lacking phones or documents; not all choose system design.

**ability to refuse:** Official ID and phone constraints documented; network participation can be useful without being costless to refuse. Coercion not established.

## Labour

**tasks:** Agent cash/e-float management; remittance administration; Suri/Jack examine shifts from farming into business/sales.

**entry barriers:** Agent liquidity/assets and registration; users’ phones/IDs. Training, fees and gender barriers not completely measured.

**bargaining ownership:** Provider controls system; agents operate conversion; ownership of resulting household businesses and bargaining outcomes not fully measured.

**transition:** Panel 2008–2014 examines occupational endpoints and authors estimate shifts, but individual transition durations and job quality not observed here.

**distribution:** Estimated welfare gains stronger for female-headed households; causal interpretation conditional, not a universal gender benefit.

**evidence limit:** No net employment count, wages of agents, work quality, market-power losses or full social welfare accounting; overlapping panel evidence not replication.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: Remittance coordination using SMS; Local liquidity and carrier coverage; Identification and banking institutions; Savings/risk-sharing/occupation channels examined with unresolved confounding

Cash shortage/outages impede use; adoption can coexist with other financial services; poverty estimates conditional and not universal fintech evidence.

## Evidence links

- [DIG-K14](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#dig-k14): M-PESA’s early Kenyan configuration combined SMS-capable phones with a carrier network, registered users, cash/e-money agents and bank-held funds; smartphone internet was not a prerequisite for this implementation.
- [DIG-K15](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#dig-k15): In Jack and Suri’s 2008/2009 survey rounds, 20% and 16% of user households reported withdrawal delays; agent cash shortages and network outages were among reported causes.
- [DIG-K16](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#dig-k16): Suri and Jack estimated about 194, 000 Kenyan households escaped extreme poverty through increased mobile-money access, using 2008–2010 agent expansion to explain 2014 outcomes; this is conditional quasi-experimental estimation, not a direct beneficiary count.
- [DIG-K17](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#dig-k17): The 2024 FinAccess report gives current mobile-money use of 82.3% among Kenyan adults 18+, across providers, and identifies lack of a phone or identity documentation among non-use barriers.
