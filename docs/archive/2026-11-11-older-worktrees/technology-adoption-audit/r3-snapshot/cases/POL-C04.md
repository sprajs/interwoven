# POL-C04 — French metrication: standards, mixed measures and legal deadlines

**Period:** 1795–1840 (later coexistence noted only as a coda). **Region:** France; Isère; Dauphiné. **Domain:** measurement; administration; commerce.

Metric law required material standards and everyday implementation. The 1812 compromise retained metric bases while adapting use; the 1840 deadline did not establish instant complete displacement.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/POL/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Earlier scientific work and 1799 prototypes are background, not the adoption measure.

**deployment:** 1795 requirements followed by standard manufacture/distribution and repeated implementation orders.

**diffusion:** Local archival examples and secondary synthesis; no national use denominator.

**non-adoption/niche:** Older terms and task measures persist, including alongside official metric practice.

**displacement/reversal:** 1812 mixed arrangement and its 1837 repeal effective 1840; distinguish legal reversal from actual replacement.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Metric base standards, calibrated weights/measures, conversion tables and inspection; 1812 familiar subdivisions/labels form a distinct mixed retail configuration.

**task:** Comparable commercial quantities and administrative/legal measurement.

**population:** Regulated traders/administrators nationally; observed local-practice evidence concentrated in Isère.

**interval:** 1795–1840; later Isère observations only reject universal completion in 1840.

**contemporary alternatives:** Established local weights and task-linked area/capacity measures; Conversion tables retaining local practice; 1812 metric-based mesures usuelles

**adoption measure:** Laws/decrees, reported standards supply, local administrative correspondence and qualitative persistence; no fraction of compliant shops or households.

**observation limits:** Legal date, instrument possession, spoken unit and measured quantity are distinct. Surviving correspondence and official reports may privilege problems/enforcement.

## Actors

**chooser:** Legislators/executive officials establish rules; scientists/instrument makers define and realize standards; traders/households adapt daily practice.

**payer:** Public administration and purchasers of instruments; exact division of costs and replacement losses unknown.

**operator maintainer:** Instrument makers, inspectors, teachers, shopkeepers, clerks and users performing conversions.

**affected nonchooser:** Customers, workers and households transacting under new legal rules; exact groups harmed by rounding or enforcement unmeasured.

**ability to refuse:** Compulsory legal settings restrict lawful refusal; persistence/compromise show implementation negotiation, not a measured majority preference.

## Labour

**tasks:** Calibration, instrument production, inspection, teaching and conversion work; replacement of local conventions changes tasks rather than proving job elimination.

**entry barriers:** Scientific/instrument expertise and administrative appointment for specialists; learning and replacement costs for users, magnitude unknown.

**bargaining ownership:** State determines legal validity; makers/inspectors operationalize it; customer/trader pricing power not measured.

**transition:** Several decades between mandate and exclusive legal deadline; no worker cohorts or reliable national completion date.

**distribution:** Urban/rural contrast asserted by local archive synthesis; no income/sex/occupation-specific transition estimates.

**evidence limit:** Cannot infer that uniform standards paid for switching costs for every group or that apparent resistance was irrational.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: material standards supply; legal enforcement; education and verification; task fit and switching burdens; symbolic legitimacy

The institutional sources prefer uniform metric standards and sometimes explain resistance by habit; they do not establish all users’ beliefs or the relative weight of alternatives.

## Evidence links

- [POL-K15](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#pol-k15): The 1812 imperial decree retained metric base units while ordering commercially familiar fractions/multiples and dual markings on instruments, and retained official metric teaching.
- [POL-K16](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#pol-k16): A material implementation gap accompanied early metric mandates: Treat reports that secondary standards had not been distributed when compulsory use was declared in 1795.
- [POL-K17](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#pol-k17): The law of 4 July 1837 repealed the 1812 arrangement and prohibited nonmetric measures from 1 January 1840; this supplies a legal deadline, not a date of universal practical adoption.
- [POL-K18](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#pol-k18): The Isère archival synthesis describes customary measures tied to agricultural work and continued rural usage beyond the 1840 legal deadline; practical task fit is a plausible part of persistence, not a measured universal preference.
