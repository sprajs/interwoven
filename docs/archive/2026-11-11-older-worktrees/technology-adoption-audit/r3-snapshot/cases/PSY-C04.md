# PSY-C04 — Hearing aids: service fit, identity and the difference between trying and keeping

**Period:** 2004–2008 observational cohort; one-year dyad study published 2010; clinical trial November 2012–August 2016 (published 2017). **Region:** United States; Indiana and San Francisco Bay Area. **Domain:** assistive technology; hearing; accessibility; identity.

Task benefit, usage, identity and retention are distinct. Controlled delivery evidence and independently elicited interviews support a multi-part explanation, while price-cohort and selection limitations block a universal stigma or cost account.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/PSY/report.md).

## Stages

**feasibility:** Same digital hearing-aid hardware tested with effective amplification versus acoustically transparent placebo.

**deployment:** Volunteers use audiology best-practice or consumer-selected preset fittings.

**non-adoption/niche:** Some eligible volunteers decline trial purchase; some purchasers return devices; some interviewees discount stigma.

**displacement/reversal:** Returns within one-month or six-plus-week trial windows observed; not long-term national abandonment.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** S07: same digital mini-behind-ear aids with audiology best practices, consumer choice among presets or placebo programming; S08: earlier clinical hearing-aid purchase cohort; S09: help-seeking dyads discussing assorted aids.

**task:** Everyday speech communication, not a generic normal-hearing restoration or cognition intervention.

**population:** S07: 163 enrolled/154 completers, target ages 55–79 and mild-to-moderate hearing loss, no prior aid use, English native language and cognitive eligibility; 98% White,78% college degree,80% income >$45,000. S08: 139 never-users aged 60–89 in selected analytic comparison, 105 purchasers/34 nonpurchasers. S09: 91 dyads, hearing-impaired member >=60, 90% White, 84 followed to one year.

**interval:** S08 original recruitment 2004–08; S09 one-year follow-up with field calendar dates unspecified; S07 trial recruitment/data collection 2 November 2012–31 August 2016, with six-week primary trial and optional four-week extension, published 2017.

**contemporary alternatives:** No hearing aid or delayed purchase; Audiologist-selected/verified fitting with orientation; Consumer choice among preset aids; Communication adaptation by users/partners; not randomised as alternative

**adoption measure:** S07 speech/self-report benefit, satisfaction, datalogged daily hours, preliminary keep intention, later actual returns. S08 purchase decision and one-month returns. S09 narrated decisions and meanings; no population uptake denominator.

**observation limits:** Three distinct study populations; no pooled rate. Placebo trial and interview evidence address different questions. Severe loss, cognitive/dexterity barriers and Deaf identity not represented by these samples.

## Actors

**chooser:** Adults elect trial participation and retention; service model randomised after consent. Dyad partners influence discussions but do not necessarily choose devices.

**payer:** Trial participants pay device charge; research support covers trial infrastructure. In S07 typical/reduced prices occur sequentially.

**operator maintainer:** Wearer inserts/cleans devices and changes batteries; audiologists fit and troubleshoot; partners adapt communication.

**affected nonchooser:** Communication partners bear repetition, relationship and communication costs.

**ability to refuse:** Nonparticipation and returns documented; consent and refund options available. Clinical recommendation does not establish a duty to adopt; constrained alternatives for severe impairment outside scope.

## Labour

**tasks:** Audiometry, fitting, orientation, maintenance and user self-management; unpaid communication adaptation by partners.

**entry barriers:** Clinical credentials for providers; for users hearing configuration, cognition/language eligibility and payment. Severe dexterity barriers mostly excluded/unmeasured.

**bargaining ownership:** Users buy and can return aids; professional versus self-service tasks reorganised, not provider earnings measured.

**transition:** Weeks to one year for use/decisions; no professional displacement or job-transition panel.

**distribution:** Predominantly White selected older volunteers; lower-income, working-age and non-English users insufficiently covered.

**evidence limit:** No claim about labour-market benefits, optimal lifetime use, all assistive technologies or all deaf/hard-of-hearing people.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: acoustic/task fit; orientation and maintenance; subjective need; social meaning and supportive relations; price and selection

Price assigned sequentially, not randomly; wealthy/educated clinic volunteers not representative. Daily use does not distinguish the randomised arms despite differences in benefits. Qualitative stigma lacks causal identification and is not universal.

## Evidence links

- [PSY-K11](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#psy-k11): In Humes et al.’s 154-completer trial, both effective audiology-fitted and consumer-selected preset aids improved major hearing-benefit measures versus placebo, while satisfaction and preliminary keep intention differed and daily logged use did not differ significantly across service arms.
- [PSY-K12](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#psy-k12): The hearing-aid price comparison was not randomised: earlier participants paid $3,600 and later participants $600; the null price findings for many benefit measures and concentration of returns at the high price cannot identify a causal price effect.
- [PSY-K13](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#psy-k13): In Wallhagen’s 91 Bay Area help-seeking dyads, independently elicited accounts linked visible hearing aids with age, disability and perceived competence; some participants rejected or discounted stigma, especially in supportive relationships.
- [PSY-K14](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#psy-k14): In a separate earlier Indiana volunteer cohort, 105 of 139 analysed never-users purchased hearing aids and 21 purchasers returned them; returners had poorer measured aided outcomes, while nonpurchasers differed mainly in perceived difficulties and expectations.
