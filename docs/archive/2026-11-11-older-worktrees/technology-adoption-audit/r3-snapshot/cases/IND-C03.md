# IND-C03 — British textile mills choosing water, steam and collective reservoirs

**Period:** c. 1780–1850. **Region:** Greater Manchester; Mersey Basin; Britain. **Domain:** textiles; water power; steam power; infrastructure coordination.

Local water scarcity, mixed-power operation and unbuilt reservoir schemes challenge a single national energy-price account.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/IND/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** The relevant choice involved existing prime movers and proposed infrastructure, not a new first invention.

**feasibility:** Hydrological potential differs from dependable and legally accessible power; reservoir technical feasibility is not audited here.

**deployment:** Steam sometimes supplemented or recirculated water before replacing it.

**diffusion:** The 1838 factory return and mill-location reconstruction indicate geographical variation, not uniform British adoption.

**scaling:** Concentrated mills could exceed convenient local water supply; collective reservoirs were a possible but contingent response.

**non-adoption:** Malm attributes non-realisation of major reservoir proposals partly to coordination requirements; abstract-only attribution.

**displacement:** Steam gained in cotton districts while water remained viable elsewhere; no single national replacement moment.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Water-driven textile mills, steam supplementation or replacement, and proposed collective reservoirs; built mixed systems and unbuilt proposals are distinguished.

**task:** Provide sufficient dependable mill power while retaining viable production locations.

**population:** British textile mills with emphasis on Greater Manchester/Mersey; reservoir promoters and potential subscribers are a different observed subgroup.

**interval:** c. 1780–1850, with 1838 factory evidence a benchmark

**contemporary alternatives:** Existing waterpower; Steam supplement or recirculation; Steam replacement; Reservoir-supported water systems (proposed, some unrealised); Relocation or constrained expansion

**adoption measure:** Factory-return power requirements combined with geographic reconstruction; reservoir outcomes qualitative and abstract-only.

**observation limits:** Theoretical water potential is not priced dependable capacity. Proposal existence is not operational feasibility or adoption.

## Actors

**chooser:** Mill owners, reservoir promoters and owners/users of water rights.

**payer:** Individual manufacturers or proposed collective subscribers; detailed project finance not inspected.

**operator maintainer:** Mill engineers and workers maintain engines, wheels and transmission; reservoir operation would require additional coordination.

**affected nonchooser:** Factory workers, neighbouring water users and communities exposed to emissions; impacts not quantified here.

**ability to refuse:** Manufacturers could decline subscription; evidence for workers choosing power sources is absent. Rights holders and collective agreements could constrain owner choices.

## Labour

**tasks:** Power tending and maintenance differ across systems; existing labour access may influence site retention, but this study does not quantify that motive.

**entry barriers:** Finance, land/water rights, engineering competence; comparative worker entry barriers unknown.

**bargaining ownership:** Individual engine ownership contrasts with proposed shared water infrastructure; worker bargaining evidence not acquired.

**transition:** Mixed systems show partial conversion; no labour cohorts followed across power changes.

**distribution:** Fuel and coordination costs affect owners; no inference of improved worker autonomy from dependable power.

**evidence limit:** Hydrology cannot identify labour-control motives, and the abstract-only reservoir source cannot establish scheme-level distributive effects.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: local hydrology; seasonal reliability; industrial concentration; collective financing; water rights and coordination; sunk locations

Run-of-river reconstruction does not price reservoirs or determine initial industrial geography. Abstract-only access limits the coordination account.

## Evidence links

- [IND-K06](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k06): In Crafts’s January 2002 reconstruction, steam contributed less than 0.01 percentage points per year to British labour-productivity growth before 1830, with a larger later contribution including railways.
- [IND-K07](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k07): Jonell and colleagues reconstruct unused waterpower potential across much of Britain alongside local constraints around Greater Manchester, and describe supplementary steam use in water-powered mills.
- [IND-K08](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k08): Malm argues that large proposed reservoir schemes in the 1820s–1830s failed partly because their collective financing and coordination requirements conflicted with manufacturers’ arrangements.
