# PSY-C03 — Malawian agricultural communication: incentives, networks and gendered credibility

**Period:** 2009–2013, two explicitly separated experimental programmes. **Region:** Malawi; eight-district extension experiment and three-district network experiment. **Domain:** agriculture; social learning; institutional design; gender.

Public extension arrangements shape who communicates and receives attention. Targeting and incentives affect diffusion, but neither automatic peer imitation nor a uniform gender barrier to final uptake is established.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/PSY/report.md).

## Stages

**deployment:** Government extension officers train designated lead/peer farmers or two network-selected seeds.

**diffusion:** Knowledge and observed/reported farming adoption followed over two or three seasons.

**non-adoption/niche:** Non-incentivised dissemination often weak; benchmark network villages frequently show no non-seed adoption; most farmers remain non-adopters even with targeting.

**scaling:** Expensive network mapping is a proof of concept, not a demonstrated cheap universal delivery system.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Pit planting for maize moisture retention and crop-residue management; programmes and technologies kept separate where outcomes differ.

**task:** Learn and disseminate cultivation methods through overstretched public extension.

**population:** Programme A (S05/S06): 2009–11, eight districts; 120-village incentive/communicator analysis and overlapping 143-village gender-reservation analysis, rotating noncommunicator samples and separately interviewed spouses. Programme B (S04): 200 villages in Machinga, Mwanza, Nkhotakota, ~5,600 sampled households with network census covering >80% of households per village.

**interval:** A: baseline 2009, follow-up 2010/2011. B: two or three annual rounds 2011–2013 depending on district.

**contemporary alternatives:** Extension officer alone; One locally selected lead farmer; Five ordinary peer communicators with/without rewards; Two seeds selected by extension officer; Two seeds selected using network or geographic algorithms; Continue ridging/flat planting or alternative residue use

**adoption measure:** A: knowledge exams, contact/training reports and physical farm-practice checks; B: seasonal pit-planting adoption and village indicator for any non-seed adoption. Compare exact denominators and seasons, not pooled effect size.

**observation limits:** Rotating respondents/partial panels; network and incentive designs distinct. Most farmers never adopt within observation; weather and soil suitability differ.

## Actors

**chooser:** Ministry/researchers allocate communicator-selection rules; village meetings and officers select eligible people; each farmer decides subsequent practice.

**payer:** Public extension and research programme support training and rewards; households provide cultivation resources.

**operator maintainer:** Extension officers, communicator farmers and household cultivators; communication is additional work.

**affected nonchooser:** Farm household members, unpaid workers and farmers not reached by extension.

**ability to refuse:** Cultivation uptake not mandated; substantial non-adoption. Village role endorsement/selection documented; selected women’s within-household permission and cost of declining are not measured.

## Labour

**tasks:** Demonstration, explaining and answering questions; digging/reusing planting pits, weeding and residue management.

**entry barriers:** Lead criteria included literacy/status/resources; peer criteria broader. No gender gap in measured learning among selected communicators; female-led training receives less attendance.

**bargaining ownership:** Performance rewards acknowledge communication labour; who controls household gains and unpaid burdens is not established.

**transition:** Communicator effort and farm tasks tracked over 2–3 seasons; not an occupational career panel.

**distribution:** S06 directly measures sex-specific reception and subgroup knowledge/adoption; broad aggregate equivalence not proven. S05 reports labour-time changes but not wage or employment distribution.

**evidence limit:** No estimate of economy-wide extension job substitution, female earnings gains or landless-worker losses.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: costly communication effort; locally relevant demonstrations; network exposure; gendered perceived credibility

One versus five communicators confounds identity/coverage; S05/S06 share data. Network adoption-rate difference not significant in final year; reinforcement versus other multi-contact processes unresolved. Female-reserved roles yield poorer reception without a precise pooled downstream adoption/yield penalty.

## Evidence links

- [PSY-K07](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#psy-k07): In the 120-village Malawi extension experiment, performance incentives to ordinary peer communicators increased their experimentation/communication and downstream adoption; simply appointing local communicators without incentives did not reliably produce that diffusion.
- [PSY-K08](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#psy-k08): In the separate 200-village Malawi network experiment, central-seed targeting increased pit-planting diffusion relative to extension-worker selection; adoption-rate differences were significant in year two but not year three, and 45% of benchmark villages had no non-seed adopter after three years.
- [PSY-K09](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#psy-k09): Female-reserved communicator roles in the Malawi 143-village study elicited lower recipient ratings and lower training attendance despite comparable measured communicator knowledge/use; pooled downstream knowledge/adoption and yields did not show a precise corresponding gender penalty.
- [PSY-K10](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#psy-k10): The Malawi network data support a multiple-contact learning interpretation, but complex contagion is not uniquely established: connectedness can change exposure, demonstration and persuasion as well as reinforcement of beliefs.
