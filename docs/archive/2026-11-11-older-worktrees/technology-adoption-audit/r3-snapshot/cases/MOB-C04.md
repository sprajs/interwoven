# MOB-C04 — Ghana: rail-created commercial geography after railway decline

**Period:** 1898–2000, with main early railway episode 1901–1931. **Region:** Gold Coast/Ghana; West Africa. **Domain:** railways; agriculture; urbanization; roads.

Railways helped expand cocoa cultivation and towns; later service and maintenance decline coexisted with road replacement and persistent importance of earlier railway places.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/MOB/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Imported railway technology; no invention claim.

**feasibility:** Western line reached mining areas and Kumasi in 1901–1903.

**deployment:** Mining and military priorities shaped the western route; eastern construction and wartime delays produced a later connection to Kumasi in 1923.

**diffusion:** Cocoa and settlement expansion near rail is studied spatially; access and use are not identical.

**scaling:** Rail transport and commercial agriculture expanded before mid-century.

**non-adoption/niche:** Several planned routes were not built; their comparison helps test selection rather than showing that rail was technically infeasible.

**displacement/reversal:** Rail traffic fell after 1974; roads expanded and old rail towns retained importance through 2000.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Western and eastern colonial railways, cocoa hinterlands and towns, followed by post-independence road expansion and rail decline.

**task:** Move heavy mining inputs and exports, troops, cocoa and people; later sustain transport as rail service deteriorated.

**population:** Historical grid cells, cocoa-producing areas and towns in the study, not a panel of individual farmers or workers.

**interval:** Construction from 1898; early comparisons 1901–1931; persistence to 2000.

**contemporary alternatives:** head-loading; limited canoe/steam-launch routes; lorries and improving roads; surveyed but unbuilt rail alignments

**adoption measure:** Rail proximity, cocoa production, census settlement size and later economic proxies; reported modal cocoa shares are contextual historical series. No generic national technology-adoption rate.

**observation limits:** Main identification uses selected forest-zone cells and excludes major endpoint cities in relevant specifications; historical sources and transport substitutes vary over time.

## Actors

**chooser:** Colonial governors, mining interests and railway planners initially; African farmers responded to opportunities; post-independence authorities managed rail/road investment.

**payer:** Colonial/public finance, railway users and producers; precise incidence and labour financing not independently reconstructed here.

**operator maintainer:** Railway staff, construction/repair labour, carriers, road operators and cocoa cultivators; employment totals appear in historical narrative but individual careers do not.

**affected nonchooser:** Railway workers, bypassed places, forest users and households dependent on local land; precise dispossession and compulsory labour incidence unknown.

**ability to refuse:** The rail network served colonial military power, but that does not establish every producer’s participation was coerced. Farmer use, worker contracts, taxation and community land consent require separate evidence not available here.

## Labour

**tasks:** Rail construction/maintenance, port and station handling, farming, head-loading and later road transport; the study identifies reorganization without a complete occupation balance.

**entry barriers:** Land and crop maturation, market access and equipment matter; tenure, migrant status and capital access are not measured consistently.

**bargaining ownership:** Colonial route priorities differed from farmer interests; later management and fiscal weakness affected service. Local bargaining institutions are not independently examined.

**transition:** Long-run settlement/census sequence and aggregate rail employment context; no individual labour-cohort tracking.

**distribution:** Connected places grew, but no inference that all farmers, landholders or railway workers benefited equally. Forest conversion is described, not monetized.

**evidence limit:** Cannot equate urban persistence with optimal location, general welfare or absence of coercion/externalities.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: colonial routing and lobbying; trade-cost reduction; agricultural response; maintenance-revenue feedback; road substitution; sunk investments and coordination

Later roads preserved absolute transport access; enduring towns do not prove railway operation remained competitive. Cocoa prospects may partly have attracted the eastern line.

## Evidence links

- [MOB-K11](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mob-k11): The Ghanaian western railway was routed toward gold mines and Kumasi for commercial and military purposes, while the eastern route had different political and commercial origins and reached Kumasi only in 1923 after wartime delays.
- [MOB-K12](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mob-k12): Jedwab and Moradi find that railway access encouraged cocoa production and settlement growth in Ghana, using geographic controls, planned-but-unbuilt lines and instruments to address route selection.
- [MOB-K13](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mob-k13): Ghanaian railway traffic declined sharply after 1974, yet towns near former railway routes retained relative economic importance through the study’s 2000 endpoint as roads expanded.
- [MOB-K14](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mob-k14): The same Ghana study describes a feedback between poor service, lower freight revenue and deferred maintenance, alongside cocoa cultivation’s movement into new forest areas, as context for rail decline.
