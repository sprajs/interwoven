# ANC-C06 — Dholavira's runoff storage and uneven urban service

**Period:** Third to mid-second millennium BCE; ICOMOS phases include gaps and a final occupation around 1500–1450 BCE. **Region:** Khadir Island, Kachchh, Gujarat, South Asia. **Domain:** water storage; urban infrastructure; runoff diversion; archaeological interpretation.

Excavated reservoirs demonstrate deployed water management, while new GPR identifications and damage causes remain hypotheses. Political organisation and service distributions are largely unrecovered.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/ANC/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** No first reservoir or unique-inventor claim.

**feasibility:** Large excavated storage and diversion structures are material evidence; water balances not reconstructed.

**deployment:** Combined water features with construction phases.

**diffusion:** One site; no denominator for Harappan adoption.

**scaling:** Reservoir construction associated with an expansion phase; output/population capacity not estimated.

**non-adoption/niche:** No demonstrated decision to reject perennial-supply technology; environment and alternatives differ.

**displacement/reversal:** Unrestored damage and settlement gaps in ICOMOS's narrative; cause not identified.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Excavated runoff/rainwater storage, stream diversion and drains; separately, hypothesised buried water features east of the East Reservoir.

**task:** Store and distribute episodic water while managing runoff.

**population:** City residents and producers across changing occupation phases; total and group coverage unknown.

**interval:** Phased third–mid-second millennium BCE system, not a single unchanged installation.

**contemporary alternatives:** Wells are present in the site's water-management description.; Different storage/distribution arrangements are possible; no ancient procurement comparison survives in inspected sources.

**adoption measure:** Excavated structures and phased settlement narrative; GPR is a modern detection sample, not an adopter census.

**observation limits:** Structures do not determine ownership or use allocation; geophysics alone cannot date damage; source dates differ.

## Actors

**chooser:** Unknown: size and planning do not identify a ruler, council or neighbourhood decision mechanism.

**payer:** Unknown: no construction or maintenance accounts inspected.

**operator maintainer:** Builders, cleaners and distributors inferred from function; identities, status and pay unknown.

**affected nonchooser:** Residents and producers dependent on stored water; differential access not reconstructed.

**ability to refuse:** Unknown; cannot infer consent or hydraulic coercion solely from monumental remains.

## Labour

**tasks:** Excavation, stonework, runoff control, cleaning and distribution; proportions paid/unpaid and labour displaced unknown.

**entry barriers:** Stoneworking and construction competence evident indirectly; recruitment, exclusion and asset ownership not recoverable here.

**bargaining ownership:** No established ownership or bargaining arrangements.

**transition:** Settlement phases show aggregate reorganisation, not individual occupational transitions.

**distribution:** No per-household reliability, labour burden or livelihood distribution quantified.

**evidence limit:** No supported claim that city growth improved every resident's welfare or that water failure alone caused migration.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: Storage shifts water availability across time, conditional on inflow and retained capacity.; Changing settlement demand and maintenance can interact.; Feature identification and causal explanation require separate tests.

GPR offers multiple structural interpretations; decline narratives are not independently re-dated here. Other water sources could persist when monumental features cease.
