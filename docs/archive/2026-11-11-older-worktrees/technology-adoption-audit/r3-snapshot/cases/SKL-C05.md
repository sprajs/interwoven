# SKL-C05 — Dar es Salaam electrical repair: sustaining appliance access under unreliable supply

**Period:** 1985–2020; interviews 2018 and 2020. **Region:** Tanzania: Dar es Salaam. **Domain:** electrical appliances; repair; labour transition.

Repair technicians supplied an intermediate service between an appliance purchase and ongoing usable electricity. Consumer accounts support particular restorations, while population effects and the technical durability of modifications remain untested.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/SKL/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Not a new appliance principle; existing devices and components were repaired or modified.

**feasibility:** Customers reported working repaired appliances; engineering validation of modified protection is absent.

**deployment:** Appliance services and repair kiosks documented in fieldwork, newspaper accounts and interviews.

**diffusion:** Qualitative growth in device and repair use; no appliance-owning household denominator.

**scaling:** Not quantified; recurring breakdown can expand repair demand without improving underlying grid performance.

**non-adoption/niche:** Backup generation was financially bounded, and households could keep repaired/second-hand devices rather than replace them.

**displacement/reversal:** Source proposes formal utility retrenchment feeding informal work; causal allocation and worker outcomes remain underdetermined.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Household appliance diagnosis, power-supply/component repair and technician advice; utility-side work appears only as an explicitly separate labour-supply context.

**task:** Restore or preserve household appliance use despite damage and volatile electricity service.

**population:** Interviewed electricity users and technicians in Dar es Salaam; no representative city sample is claimed.

**interval:** Historical frame 1985–2020; direct interviews in 2018/2020; older labour histories reconstructed using other evidence.

**contemporary alternatives:** Replace the appliance; Continue without the service; Seek utility compensation; Buy protection/backup equipment when affordable; Retain an older repaired or second-hand device

**adoption measure:** Qualitative interview reports, including continued use after repair, and observed kiosks; no denominator, market share or aggregate failure rate.

**observation limits:** Anonymous interviews protect sensitive participants but restrict rechecking; author-translated accounts and retrospective claims are not independent electrical tests.

## Actors

**chooser:** Householders choose repair/backup expenditures; technicians propose parts and interventions.

**payer:** Householders bear repair and interruption costs; payment schedules and informal credit are not systematically described.

**operator maintainer:** Formally and informally trained technicians; household operation and care are not time-budgeted.

**affected nonchooser:** Other household members and neighbours share service/safety consequences; unequal backup access is described without a group-level welfare measure.

**ability to refuse:** Repair versus replacement is constrained by cash and service availability. Utility-side vishoka work has a distinct legal status and is not equated with ordinary appliance repair.

## Labour

**tasks:** Diagnosis, component replacement, wiring/advice and practical learning after formal schooling; adjacent temporary utility work can supply experience.

**entry barriers:** Schooling, workshop practice, tools, customers and contacts; formal qualifications alone do not record practical experience.

**bargaining ownership:** Freelancers obtain fee income but lack the employment position of utility staff; exact earnings/security not measured.

**transition:** Mchome links 1990s utility retrenchment to street electrical work, but no individual/cohort panel allocates effects between technology and structural adjustment.

**distribution:** Some households report access restored at lower cost than replacement; no citywide affordability claim or safety gain is established.

**evidence limit:** Repair demand is not a welfare measure; cannot establish that displaced utility workers regained former pay, security or bargaining power.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: formal and practical training; component replacement; repair affordability; infrastructure unreliability; informal work after retrenchment

Author emphasises resilience; successful repair testimony may omit failed repairs. Appliance import growth and market liberalisation may drive service demand as much as power failure. Utility workforce claims do not isolate computerisation from policy cuts.

## Evidence links

- [SKL-K13](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#skl-k13): Most technicians in Mchome's interview sample said they had formal vocational training and also acquired experience through informal practice after graduation; the evidence does not support an exclusive formal-versus-informal skills dichotomy.
- [SKL-K14](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#skl-k14): Dar es Salaam users in Mchome's study reported continuing to use repaired appliances, including one television four years after repair; component repair offered a route to retained service alongside replacement or going without.
