# ENE-C03 — Planar silicon and merchant DRAM: reproducibility, procurement and bounded learning

**Period:** 1957–1969 and 1974–1992. **Region:** United States; Japan; Other merchant DRAM producer countries. **Domain:** industrial manufacturing; digital hardware.

Two explicitly distinct episodes connect protected-junction manufacturing and Apollo procurement to later tests of generation-specific DRAM learning. The evidence rejects patent-equals-diffusion and simple national-learning-superiority stories.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/ENE/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Hoerni’s coating-retention method disclosed in 1959 patent application; no claim to exclusive invention of the transistor or IC.

**feasibility:** Working planar transistor recorded in March 1959; repeatability and continuing failure mechanisms distinguished from a patent.

**deployment:** Commercial planar transistor in April 1960; Apollo NOR-gate purchases in the early 1960s.

**diffusion:** Commercial licensing described qualitatively; later 32-firm merchant-DRAM shipment panel.

**scaling:** Standardized procurement and inferred within-generation production learning; direct yield panel absent.

**non-adoption/niche:** High-value spacecraft niche precedes broad consumer affordability; core/transistor alternatives were considered.

**displacement/reversal:** DRAM generation transitions include entry and exit; they do not establish whole-firm disappearance.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Episode A: silicon planar transistor process and Apollo NOR logic, 1957–1969. Episode B: merchant DRAM generations 4K through 16M, 1974–1992. No transitive causal proof is asserted between episodes.

**task:** Manufacture reliable compact switching/logic devices and, separately, inexpensive merchant memory.

**population:** Fairchild/process-history and Apollo engineering records; separate Dataquest panel of 32 merchant firms, excluding internal-only producers.

**interval:** 1957–1969; 1974Q1–1992Q4.

**contemporary alternatives:** Mesa/discrete transistors and core-transistor logic; Different gates and redundant/repairable computer designs; Prior or rival memory generations; Internal rather than merchant memory production

**adoption measure:** Documented prototype/product introduction and NASA purchase episode; later quarterly DRAM shipments/prices, not direct costs or plant yields.

**observation limits:** Patent is not proof of uptake; procurement share is narrow in time; structural model infers cost from price/output and market assumptions.

## Actors

**chooser:** Device firms, NASA/MIT system designers and merchant-memory producers; buyers chose different performance/cost trade-offs.

**payer:** Corporate investment and publicly financed Apollo procurement; later merchant sales and investment, exact R&D/procurement shares unknown.

**operator maintainer:** Process engineers, fabrication and test workers, equipment/quality specialists and system designers.

**affected nonchooser:** Taxpayers and manufacturing workers/communities; hazardous-material exposure and labour allocation are not quantified here.

**ability to refuse:** System designers evaluated alternatives; firms could enter/exit merchant product lines. Workers’ employment alternatives, compelled transfers and local community voice unknown.

## Labour

**tasks:** Protective coating/masking/diffusion, contact and packaging work, qualification tests, defect diagnosis, production-process monitoring.

**entry barriers:** Precision/cleanliness, equipment, process knowledge and capital. Cross-generation spillover estimates do not measure every entry barrier.

**bargaining ownership:** Patent assigned to Fairchild and rights licensed according to museum account; firms own production assets. Worker bargaining evidence absent.

**transition:** Firm-level product entry/exit observed; no individual transitions from wiring/assembly to fabrication quantified.

**distribution:** Model tests country-level learning rates; it does not measure wage inequality, gendered assembly work or ownership returns.

**evidence limit:** No claim that semiconductor learning automatically creates accessible work or that 20% inferred cost learning means a 20% labour-productivity gain.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: Surface protection and process control; Standardized high-value procurement; Within-firm learning with international spillovers; Partial knowledge obsolescence

Later DRAM evidence does not identify what Apollo caused; patent, museum and official history share inherited records and lack full factory data.

## Evidence links

- [ENE-K09](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ene-k09): Hoerni’s 1959 patent application specifies retaining a nonconducting surface coating over exposed junction edges; the museum history dates a working planar transistor to March 1959 and commercial introduction to April 1960.
- [ENE-K10](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ene-k10): The Apollo computer programme supported an early IC production niche through a large, standardized NOR-gate purchase, while its engineers valued size, power, timing and reliability rather than the lowest initial component price.
- [ENE-K11](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ene-k11): Irwin and Klenow’s 1974–1992 merchant-DRAM model estimates stronger own-firm than external learning contributions and finds no systematic faster learning rate for Japanese firms; it does not support a simple national-learning-superiority explanation.
- [ENE-K12](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ene-k12): The same DRAM study finds economically important spillovers between adjacent product generations in only two of seven tested transitions, so experience in one generation cannot be treated as a guaranteed permanent entry barrier.
