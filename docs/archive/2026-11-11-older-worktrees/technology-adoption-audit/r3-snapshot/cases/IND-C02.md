# IND-C02 — Coalfield and Cornish steam pumping, fuel economy and collective improvement

**Period:** 1712–1850. **Region:** Britain; Cornwall. **Domain:** steam power; mining; innovation organisation.

A fuel-intensive drainage technology occupied an early niche; fuel economy, information sharing and local financing changed viable applications.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/IND/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Newcomen’s workable atmospheric engine and Watt’s condenser are distinct stages; later high-pressure designs were further changes.

**feasibility:** Atmospheric operation fitted early engineering capability; improved economy mattered in high-coal-cost Cornwall.

**deployment:** Boulton and Watt erected Cornish pumping engines; original installation records accessed through Nuvolari.

**diffusion:** Mine district reporting from 1811 supported comparisons; not a national count of all engines.

**scaling:** Incremental efficiency and expanded investment accompanied district improvements; aggregate early steam impact remained small in Crafts’s accounting.

**non-adoption:** Fuel cost could make early engines unattractive away from cheap-coal locations; engineering and royalties also mattered.

**displacement:** Alternative compound and single-cylinder designs persisted; no universal extinction of earlier pumps inferred.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Atmospheric coalfield pumping and fuel-efficient Cornish pumping, then post-1811 reporting and incremental improvement; these are separate local phases.

**task:** Keep mines drained at acceptable total operating cost.

**population:** Selected British coalfield applications and Cornish mining engines/investors; macro steam accounting is a separate aggregate comparison.

**interval:** 1712–1850

**contemporary alternatives:** Atmospheric engines; Improved condenser engines; High-pressure single-cylinder or compound designs; Hydraulic pumping where suitable; Foregoing deeper mining

**adoption measure:** Nuvolari’s installation narrative and comparative engine-duty series; Crafts’s reconstructed horsepower and growth accounting are not the same population.

**observation limits:** Original contracts and Lean reports are inherited through scholarship; best reported duty may differ from typical annual performance.

## Actors

**chooser:** Mine adventurers and captains choose and commission engines; engineers offer designs and operational alterations.

**payer:** Mine investors pay installations and royalties; Boulton and Watt receive contracted fuel-saving premiums.

**operator maintainer:** Engineers, enginemen and pump crews; operational and minor maintenance changes enter reported performance.

**affected nonchooser:** Miners, engine workers, local communities and coal suppliers; welfare effects not independently measured.

**ability to refuse:** Investors could dispute contracts or order rival engines, subject to patents and courts. Workers’ consent to engine change is not established by owner purchasing evidence.

## Labour

**tasks:** Pumping labour and fuel handling reorganised; engine erection, maintenance, adjustment and performance comparison became important tasks.

**entry barriers:** Engineering knowledge, reputation and access to capital; no complete entry or apprenticeship series.

**bargaining ownership:** Patentees sought royalty income; mine shareholders and managers coordinated expenditure; engineers competed in reported performance.

**transition:** Patent expiry, slack maintenance and later reporting are sequential, but individual careers and displaced-worker absorption are unknown.

**distribution:** Investor and engineer incentives are described; no representative wage, mortality or livelihood effect is estimated.

**evidence limit:** Duty measures useful mechanical work per fuel, not net profit or worker welfare; royalty disputes are not a causal estimate of patent policy.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: fuel-price incentives; engineering improvement; cost-book investment; comparative performance reporting; patent and royalty arrangements; maintenance

Patent expiry, reporting, high-pressure engines and investment were not independent shocks; duty is not mine profit.

## Evidence links

- [IND-K03](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k03): Nuvolari’s Cornish comparison links early Newcomen use to tolerable fuel costs and the uptake of more fuel-efficient Watt engines to the expense of imported coal in Cornwall.
- [IND-K04](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k04): From 1811, Cornish engine reporting made performance and practices comparable across installations; Nuvolari interprets this information sharing and overlapping investment as supports for incremental improvement.
- [IND-K05](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k05): Watt’s fuel-saving royalties and patent enforcement generated documented conflict in Cornwall; Nuvolari argues that broad exclusion also obstructed rival steam development.
- [IND-K06](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#ind-k06): In Crafts’s January 2002 reconstruction, steam contributed less than 0.01 percentage points per year to British labour-productivity growth before 1830, with a larger later contribution including railways.
