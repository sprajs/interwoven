# OCE-C05 — Māori kūmara: local adaptation, experimental limits and knowledge recovery

**Period:** Fourteenth–eighteenth-century archaeological contexts; trials 1999–2002; Pōhatu study published 2025. **Region:** M24/11, Mohua/Golden Bay, Aotearoa New Zealand; Robin Hood Bay and Whatarangi, Cook Strait; Pōhatu, Banks Peninsula. **Domain:** horticulture; soil engineering; storage; knowledge transmission.

Separate site and research episodes show crop cultivation, experimental feasibility and collaborative reconstruction. They are compared as distinct evidence, not merged into one uninterrupted tradition or regional adoption rate.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/OCE/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** No first introduction or independent invention claim.

**deployment:** M24/11 and Pōhatu modified soils and crop evidence support historical gardening at those localities.

**feasibility:** Cook Strait trials demonstrate selected cultivars growing under modern conditions; not ancient yields.

**diffusion:** Local remains and cultivar histories do not supply a regional diffusion denominator.

**non-adoption/niche:** Different crops/sites face different climates; absence of remains does not establish universal rejection.

**displacement/reversal:** Pōhatu land alienation/disconnection documented through community-linked scholarship; contemporary garden re-establishment is a goal, not a measured outcome.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Three explicit subepisodes: A, M24/11 archaeological cultivation surfaces and probable crop microremains; B, experimental Cook Strait gardens using returned collection cultivars; C, Pōhatu modified-soil investigation guided by Mana Whenua knowledge.

**task:** A: grow foods in local conditions; B: investigate possible growth and labor/yield constraints; C: recover cultivation history and support desired reconnection.

**population:** A: historical site cultivators, counts/identities unresolved; B: modern research gardeners with iwi consultation; C: Te Rūnaka o Koukourārata Mana Whenua and research partners. These populations are not interchangeable.

**interval:** A: fourteenth-century and post-1650 contexts; B: 1999–2002 observations published 2003; C: historical precolonial/early colonial gardening context, contemporary study published 2025.

**contemporary alternatives:** Historical other introduced crops, native food plants and aquatic/animal resources; their shares unknown; Different soils, crop varieties, planting times and storage practices; Modern laboratory soil analysis and community knowledge used together, not mutually exclusive alternatives

**adoption measure:** Site microremains and modified soils; separate experimental plot harvests; research workflow and stated restoration aims. No combined farmer/household adoption measure.

**observation limits:** Experimental yields do not measure historical diffusion; site sampling cannot prove crop extinction; collaborative research is not completed commercial or subsistence restoration.

## Actors

**chooser:** A: ancient garden decision-makers unresolved; B: researchers with consultation/support from named iwi authorities; C: Mana Whenua helped frame questions and interpretations.

**payer:** A: unknown labor contributors; B/C: research resources and land access, complete budgets uninspected.

**operator maintainer:** A: gardeners maintaining soil, planting stocks and stores; B: experimental gardeners; C: knowledge holders and researchers. Ancient gender division unestablished.

**affected nonchooser:** Historical communities affected by land transfer; landholders and descendants in contemporary research; no full impact measurement.

**ability to refuse:** Pōhatu historical displacement limits choice; modern iwi consultation and access permission documented. Historical crop selection cannot be read as fully unconstrained preference.

## Labour

**tasks:** Soil amendment, mound preparation, planting, weeding, storage; research sampling, interpretation and knowledge transmission are later distinct tasks.

**entry barriers:** Suitable land/microclimate, viable planting stock, storage skill and knowledge access; trial windbreaks/treated stock change entry requirements.

**bargaining ownership:** Pōhatu’s alienation/private ownership interrupted direct access; current research recognises Mana Whenua authority but is not a documented land restitution.

**transition:** No tracked ancient household transitions or contemporary paid-work cohort. Plant collection’s repatriation/treatment is observed but not equivalent to restoration of all local cultivars.

**distribution:** No income/wage distribution; scientific knowledge recovery is an observed process, food sovereignty an expressed purpose.

**evidence limit:** Cannot infer ancient surplus per worker, gender equality, exact crop portfolios or successful new livelihoods from these different evidence streams.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: crop/environment fit; soil modification; storage and planting material; land access; community-directed research

Starch identifications and ancient fertilizer interpretations are uncertain. Trials have modern confounders. Distinct locations, climates and users cannot be pooled into a historical yield or adoption estimate.

## Evidence links

- [OCE-K16](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#oce-k16): At M24/11 in northern Te Waipounamu, the 2024 study associates probable kūmara starch and other proposed crop remains with fourteenth-century cultivation contexts and identifies later kūmara remains after 1650.
- [OCE-K17](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#oce-k17): The Cook Strait experimental gardens demonstrate feasible growth of selected Māori kūmara cultivars under 1999–2002 conditions, but modern plant treatment, fencing, windbreaks, unreplicated plots and nontraditional storage prevent treating their yields as ancient farm performance.
- [OCE-K18](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#oce-k18): At Pōhatu, the 2025 collaborative study used Mana Whenua knowledge to guide questions and interpret modified soils; weak support for initially considered marine fertilizers led to further testing of terrestrial plant inputs, with kānuka a plausible rather than certain source.
- [OCE-K19](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#oce-k19): Pōhatu’s contemporary investigation is framed by Mana Whenua as recovery of place-based cultivation knowledge after land alienation and disconnection; the published study supports reconnection and food-sovereignty aspirations but does not measure restored horticultural adoption.
