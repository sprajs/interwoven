# AGR-C02 — Balinese subak calendars: irrigation coordination and recombination with modern rice

**Period:** 1970s–1980s intervention history; directly described crop calendar, 1988–1989; later model reanalysis. **Region:** Oos–Petanu watershed; Bali; Indonesia. **Domain:** agriculture; irrigation; coordination institutions.

Shared water and pest ecology make rice calendars interdependent. The observed system combines coordinated calendars with modern varieties, while independent model reanalysis limits claims of universal synchronisation or institutional optimality.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/AGR/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Neither irrigation nor water-temple institutional origin is identified by the modern observations/model.

**feasibility:** Operating canals, calendars and rice varieties are observed; model performance is conditional on ecology and decision rules.

**deployment:** The 172-subak model covers a specified two-river system; a 1988–1989 Manuaba calendar uses coordinated timing and named improved varieties.

**diffusion:** No Bali-wide denominator or independent diffusion curve is reconstructed. National programme history is accessed through scholarly accounts.

**scaling:** Watershed coordination concerns interactions beyond individual plots, but optimal coordination size and distribution are model-dependent.

**non-adoption:** Failure to follow individually intensified schedules can reflect water/pest interactions, not inability to use modern seed.

**displacement:** Restored calendar coordination coexists with modern varieties rather than wholesale technological reversal.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Canal irrigation, rice varieties and subak/temple-linked planting/fallow calendars in the Oos–Petanu setting; model variants are analytically distinct from measured practice.

**task:** Grow rice while allocating water and limiting pest habitat across interconnected fields.

**population:** 172 subaks represented in the model and local crop/water observations; the reported Manuaba calendar is a specific observed configuration, not every Balinese farmer.

**interval:** Intervention/disruption and restoration narratives cover the 1970s–1980s; directly described calendar is 1988–1989. The 1993 model and 2006 proof/2007 reanalysis are evidence dates, not new diffusion intervals.

**contemporary alternatives:** Independent intensified planting schedules; Synchronised fallow and planting; Coordinated staggered schedules where water scarcity warrants them; Different local rice-variety mixes

**adoption measure:** Qualitative records of calendars, named varieties and coordination; simulated yields under alternative schedules. No common measured household adoption share.

**observation limits:** Simulation outputs must not be counted as field outcomes. Model reanalysis shares underlying ecology/data and inherited history; original programme memoranda were not acquired.

## Actors

**chooser:** Individual farmers, subak groups, temple-linked coordinators and national agricultural programmes operate at different decision scales.

**payer:** Farmers contribute cultivation and irrigation resources; state programmes support promoted packages. Complete financial and labour incidence is not reconstructed.

**operator maintainer:** Farmers and irrigation associations implement calendars and water management; the inspected sources do not quantify every paid/unpaid maintenance contribution.

**affected nonchooser:** Upstream/downstream farmers and neighbours exposed to pests/water demand are affected by choices elsewhere; their gains can differ.

**ability to refuse:** Accounts describe programme pressure and farmer restoration of coordination. The evidence does not measure each farmer's sanctions, exit options or consent to every calendar.

## Labour

**tasks:** Crop timing, canal/water management, communication and collective decisions reorganise farming work; yield optimisation can change workload and water access differently by subak.

**entry barriers:** Land/water access, local irrigation knowledge and institutional participation; institutional authority is not directly measured as a credential or wage premium.

**bargaining ownership:** Water and timing decisions are interdependent; aggregate simulated harvest does not identify how bargaining distributes gains among associations or farmers.

**transition:** Historical restoration/recombination is described, but no individual worker panel traces job loss, earnings or career transition.

**distribution:** Janssen shows that maximising watershed output and distributing gains evenly are different objectives; person-level livelihood gains remain unmeasured.

**evidence limit:** Ecological models cannot establish universal worker welfare, consent or the historical cause of institutional formation.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: ecological externalities; timing and information coordination; selective institutional restoration; technology–institution recombination; distribution of coordination gains

Janssen reproduces central results under original assumptions but changes them under other plausible rules. Historical intervention narratives are not independently corroborated by a second field experiment.

## Evidence links

- [AGR-K04](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#agr-k04): In the Oos–Petanu model and associated field account, rice-calendar coordination mediates a tradeoff between pest suppression through synchronised fallow and competing irrigation-water demands.
- [AGR-K05](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#agr-k05): The documented 1988–1989 Manuaba calendar combined temple-linked coordination with high-yielding rice varieties, including IR64, so restoration of coordination did not require wholesale rejection of improved seed.
- [AGR-K06](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#agr-k06): Janssen's model reanalysis makes the claimed coordination advantage conditional on pest dynamics and farmers' decision rules, and distinguishes synchronisation from other coordinated schedules.
- [AGR-K24](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#agr-k24): Across these selected cases, testing a device or plot is often insufficient to evaluate the larger system that must operate, and gains in adoption, effort or output do not alone establish who benefits or whether welfare improves.
