# OCE-C03 — Martu hunting fires and localized mosaic maintenance

**Period:** 2000–2010 field observations; 1999–2010 fire mapping. **Region:** Western Desert, Western Australia; Great and Little Sandy Desert bioregions. **Domain:** fire technology; subsistence; landscape management.

Skilled burning accompanied small-game hunting in a mixed contemporary economy. Comparative landscape data support a local fuel-mosaic mechanism, while observed benefits and motives remain distinct.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/OCE/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Not investigated.

**deployment:** Directly observed hunting and ignition practices, with vehicle access and store purchases.

**diffusion:** No adoption-rate estimate; spatial concentration around community tracks is measured exposure/use.

**non-adoption/niche:** Fire use varies by prey, task, season and distance; remote comparison zones mainly lightning-driven.

**scaling:** Effects are strongest at forager-day-range scale; no uniform continental effect inferred.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Martu cool-season patch burning in spinifex for small burrowed game; vehicle trips to camps followed by foot foraging.

**task:** Acquire food and manage familiar Country through skilled practices; some ecological effects may accompany rather than exhaust stated purposes.

**population:** 2008 study: 421 bouts/59 camp-days in 2002; 2012 study: 104 foragers and 1,811 bouts across 347 sampled days, not all Martu people.

**interval:** 1999–2010 remote imagery; 2000–2010 foraging programme with focused 2002 study.

**contemporary alternatives:** Hunt without burning; Hunt different prey or collect plants; Purchase store food; not a complete substitute for social and cultural purposes; Lightning-driven burning in less-accessed comparison landscapes

**adoption measure:** Recorded behavior by hunting bout and task; fire-regime zones near tracks/communities versus remote zones; no total household denominator.

**observation limits:** The 2012 regime classes cover 10% and 50% of the study region respectively, leaving other/mixed areas outside these categories; sampled fieldwork and spatial proxies limit inference.

## Actors

**chooser:** Individual foragers and groups choose tasks/patches within customary rights; S08 describes potential retribution for burning areas without rights and social value in caring for Country.

**payer:** Foragers supply labor and contemporary transport resources; program-level subsidies/funding not analyzed.

**operator maintainer:** Primarily women in observed small-game burning; men’s task mix includes other hunting and some patch burning; practices recur through ordinary foraging.

**affected nonchooser:** Other users, households receiving food and organisms affected by fire; S08 describes burning unharvested resources as a social cost.

**ability to refuse:** Variation between burning and non-burning hunts indicates task-level discretion, within land rights and obligations; enforcement prevalence and equal access are not audited.

## Labour

**tasks:** Hunting, ignition, tracking, digging, processing, sharing and travel; ecological maintenance overlaps subsistence rather than appearing as a separate paid occupation.

**entry barriers:** Experienced ecological reading, knowledge of land, mobility and access to vehicles/tracks; age/skill and asset distributions not fully quantified.

**bargaining ownership:** Martu are Traditional Owners; sources measure foraging and ecology, not detailed control over land-management payments or household bargaining.

**transition:** Outstation return in 1980s is background; studied 2000s practices combine purchased goods with foraging. No individual employment transitions.

**distribution:** Gender differences in tasks are observed; edible-energy returns are not money or household welfare. No class/age income estimates.

**evidence limit:** Cannot estimate paid ranger employment, carbon revenues, net welfare or ancient labor systems.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: task-specific immediate returns; repeated landscape maintenance; gendered subsistence labor; mobility/access; fuel-age heterogeneity

Observed hunting choices are not randomized. Climate and topography matter, roads proxy access, and fire-age heterogeneity is not a complete measure of biodiversity.

## Evidence links

- [OCE-K09](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#oce-k09): The 2000s Martu studies observe hunting-related burning within a contemporary mixed economy that includes vehicle travel and purchased food; they do not observe an unchanged precolonial regime.
- [OCE-K10](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#oce-k10): The 2008 study reports higher mean winter small-game hunting returns with burning than without (656 versus 478 edible kcal/hour), with 152 burning and 89 non-burning hunts; assignment was observational.
- [OCE-K11](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#oce-k11): In the 1999–2010 study region, zones classified as Martu-burning dominated had smaller and more clustered fires and weaker rainfall-related fire-size expansion than lightning-dominated zones; this challenges a climate-only account for these landscapes.
- [OCE-K12](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#oce-k12): The Martu evidence supports a feedback hypothesis in which immediate small-game foraging benefits help maintain repeated burns and landscape heterogeneity without requiring a central planner to direct every ignition.
- [OCE-K20](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#oce-k20): The Martu study describes customary rights to burn, possible retribution for burning others’ areas, and social value attached to care for Country; localized burning was not institution-free individual optimization.
