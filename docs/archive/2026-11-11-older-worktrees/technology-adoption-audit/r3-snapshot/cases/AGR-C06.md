# AGR-C06 — Rainfall insurance and investment choices in northern Ghana

**Period:** 2009–2011. **Region:** Northern Ghana; Ghana; West Africa. **Domain:** agriculture; risk management; finance; field experiments.

Randomised cash and insurance treatments distinguish constraints more directly than stated obstacles alone. Insurance changes production choices, while demand for the insurance itself and the welfare consequences of greater investment remain separate questions.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/AGR/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** The study tests specific rainfall-index contracts, not priority for agricultural insurance.

**feasibility:** Weather-index triggers, delivery and payout systems operate within the experiment; basis risk is inherent to the tested matching of index and individual loss.

**deployment:** Sampled farmers receive grants or offers under randomised treatment conditions and varying prices.

**diffusion:** Experimental purchase/renewal responses are measured over three years; they do not establish sustained full-cost commercial diffusion.

**scaling:** Delivery cost, insurer viability, index accuracy and credible payment remain untested requirements for a broader market claim.

**non-adoption:** Declining an offer can reflect price, basis risk, beliefs or liquidity timing; payout-sensitive demand does not uniquely identify a psychological bias.

**displacement:** Production choices shift with risk protection, but the study does not establish complete replacement of existing risk-management practices.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Rainfall-index crop insurance, cash grants and randomised offers/prices for sampled farmers, with agricultural expenditure and production measured across seasons.

**task:** Manage catastrophic rainfall risk and decide how much and how riskily to invest in farming.

**population:** Sampled northern Ghana maize farmers in Karlan et al.'s 2009–2011 experiments; not the Akwapim tenure sample and not a representative census of African farmers.

**interval:** Three agricultural seasons, 2009–2011, including grant and purchase opportunities.

**contemporary alternatives:** Cash support without insurance; Lower-risk or lower-input production; Existing household/network coping and resource mobilisation; Declining the offered contract

**adoption measure:** Experimental insurance offers/purchases, response to randomised prices and reported agricultural investment; treatment-assignment denominators are study-specific, not regional penetration.

**observation limits:** The causal offer effect is stronger than psychological interpretations of payout/network correlations. Cost-complete commercial renewal and wider welfare are not established.

## Actors

**chooser:** Researchers/insurers define treatment offers; farmers choose investment and, where offered, whether/how much insurance to purchase.

**payer:** Experiment-funded grants/subsidies and farmers paying offered premiums; full commercial delivery incidence is not evaluated.

**operator maintainer:** Farmers operate farms; insurers and programme staff maintain index, contract and payment delivery. Contract credibility depends on those institutions.

**affected nonchooser:** Household members and workers share production risk and expenditure consequences; their individual consent and gains are not isolated by farm-level responses.

**ability to refuse:** Purchase offers can be declined and price response is observed. Constraints and household authority still shape choices; receipt of a grant is not evidence of willingness to pay its full cost.

## Labour

**tasks:** Investment and production choices change; the paper's causal result is not a universal labour-displacement estimate or new-job count.

**entry barriers:** Premium price, contract understanding, index–loss fit, payment credibility and delivery access; cash alone does not remove every constraint.

**bargaining ownership:** Farm decision-makers allocate supported resources, but intra-household control of gains and losses is not fully identified.

**transition:** Three seasons capture learning/demand changes but do not track long occupational transitions or permanent adaptation after programme support.

**distribution:** Treatment and prior/network payout experiences differ; greater input spending is not by itself a measured gain for every household member.

**evidence limit:** No full-cost insurance-market viability, general constraint ranking, guaranteed profit benefit or universal psychological explanation follows.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: downside-risk protection; mobilising existing resources; price and state-contingent transfer; basis risk; learning and trust; adoption of complements

Insurance and cash treatments differ in value/timing/state contingency. Investment effects do not establish net profit or welfare gains; local causal results cannot show credit never constrains farmers elsewhere.

## Evidence links

- [AGR-K15](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#agr-k15): In Karlan and colleagues' northern Ghana experiments, rainfall-index insurance increased agricultural expenditure and shifted production choices toward greater risk, while cash grants alone produced relatively small investment responses.
- [AGR-K16](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#agr-k16): Subsequent demand for the Ghana rainfall-index insurance varied with price and experienced or network-observed payouts; the paper considers trust, recency and basis risk and leaves the welfare interpretation of greater investment unresolved.
- [AGR-K24](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#agr-k24): Across these selected cases, testing a device or plot is often insufficient to evaluate the larger system that must operate, and gains in adoption, effort or output do not alone establish who benefits or whether welfare improves.
