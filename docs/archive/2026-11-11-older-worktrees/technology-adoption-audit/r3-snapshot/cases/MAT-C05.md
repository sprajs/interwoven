# MAT-C05 — Andean arsenical and tin bronzes: competing properties and political distribution

**Period:** Mainly approximately 800–1530 CE; modern experiments test historically plausible techniques. **Region:** Americas; Andes: Peru, Ecuador and south-central comparison. **Domain:** copper alloys; ore processing; sheet metal.

Arsenical bronze was not simply defective tin bronze; ores, sheet manufacture and Inka distribution altered the choice set.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/MAT/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** This study does not date first American metallurgy or assign a unique alloy invention.

**feasibility:** Modern cosmelting tests establish possible production; archaeological objects establish historical alloy use.

**deployment:** Sheet objects, small implements and other artefacts document differentiated applications.

**diffusion:** Tin-bronze distribution under Inka rule is a scholarly interpretation supported here through Lechtman, not a reaudited imperial dataset.

**scaling:** Established production centres are discussed; no independent throughput estimate.

**non-adoption/niche:** Tin bronze did not completely displace arsenical bronze; hardening potential was not always maximised.

**displacement/reversal:** Partial substitution and regional continuity; political restriction of some production is reported but requires local rechecking.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Copper–arsenic and copper–tin alloys used in differentiated Andean fabrication traditions; modern comparative tests and cosmelting trials

**task:** Sheet manufacture, implements, display/communicative objects and supplying alloy stock

**population:** Selected analysed Andean objects and reported production centres, plus modern laboratory samples; imperial/local users not sampled representatively

**interval:** Mainly approximately 800–1530 CE; modern experiments test historically plausible techniques

**contemporary alternatives:** Unalloyed copper; Arsenical and tin bronzes coexist; available compositions vary with ore geography; Non-metal objects may substitute by task, but no matched quantitative sample inspected

**adoption measure:** Artifact/process occurrence and experimental property comparisons; imperial distribution discussed qualitatively. No percentage of users or tools made of each alloy.

**observation limits:** Modern properties do not reveal all ancient motives. Inka resource-control interpretation relies on inherited historical/archaeological analysis.

## Actors

**chooser:** Smiths could select or process alloys; Inka political influence over distribution is reported, but decision rights are not directly reconstructed here.

**payer:** Unknown for most objects/workshops; imperial distribution is not a comprehensive financing record.

**operator maintainer:** Ore preparers/smelters, hammering and annealing specialists; paid/unpaid status unknown.

**affected nonchooser:** Miners, furnace workers and users of distributed metal; exposure and access outcomes not measured.

**ability to refuse:** Unknown; political restriction reported in scholarship cannot be converted into individual consent/compulsion evidence.

## Labour

**tasks:** Alloy preparation, hammering, annealing and object assembly; task variation rather than measured job counts.

**entry barriers:** Suitable ores and working knowledge; training and asset access unmeasured.

**bargaining ownership:** Imperial resource influence reported; local rights and artisan bargaining unknown.

**transition:** Political-period comparison not individual/cohort transition evidence.

**distribution:** No measured distribution of gains, hazards or access across occupational groups.

**evidence limit:** Mechanical tests cannot establish labour cost, income, exposure or worker preference.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: ore geography; ductility and toughness; fabrication style; alloy selection; imperial resource control hypothesis

Modern properties do not reveal intention automatically; impurity variation and casting choices show heterogeneous control.

## Evidence links

- [MAT-K15](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mat-k15): Controlled comparisons show that tin bronze’s greater possible hardness does not make it universally superior to arsenical bronze; ductility and sheet-forming performance can favour the latter.
- [MAT-K16](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mat-k16): Experimental cosmelting of Peruvian copper oxides with arsenic-bearing sulphide ores produced usable copper–arsenic alloys across multiple mixtures, supplying a feasible alternative to a separate roasting sequence.
- [MAT-K17](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mat-k17): The Andean history summarized by Lechtman includes continued arsenical-bronze use alongside Inka tin bronze, making coexistence and political distribution more appropriate than a complete superior-alloy replacement story.
- [MAT-K24](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mat-k24): Across these purposively selected cases, repeatable processes, complementary crafts and task-specific demand are better-supported comparison variables than a universal ranking of materials.
