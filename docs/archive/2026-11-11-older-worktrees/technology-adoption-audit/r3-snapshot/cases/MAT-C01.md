# MAT-C01 — Forager pottery: early occurrence and later use in China and Japan

**Period:** Approximately 20,000–19,000 cal BP at Xianrendong; Japanese comparison approximately 14,460–8,000 cal BP. **Region:** East Asia; China: Xianrendong; Japan: sampled Jōmon sites. **Domain:** ceramics; food processing.

Early vessels and later Japanese residue evidence separate technical occurrence from the conditions and purposes of wider use.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/MAT/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** No inventor or single origin identified; Xianrendong provides an early surviving occurrence.

**feasibility:** Formed and fired vessels plus use traces demonstrate workable ceramic containers; production frequency remains unknown.

**deployment:** Forager contexts at Xianrendong and sampled Jōmon vessels document actual use.

**diffusion:** Japanese sites provide regional functional comparison, not proof that the technique diffused from Xianrendong.

**scaling:** Early Holocene expansion is reported qualitatively; sample size is not a production denominator.

**non-adoption/niche:** Late Pleistocene use could remain limited; no evidence here that all forager households adopted pots.

**displacement/reversal:** No complete replacement of other cooking/storage methods is demonstrated.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Fired vessel pottery at Xianrendong; separately, residue-bearing Incipient/Initial Jōmon ceramics

**task:** Containment and probable cooking at Xianrendong; aquatic-resource processing in sampled Japanese vessels

**population:** Occupants whose debris survived at Xianrendong and users of sampled vessels at 46 Japanese sites; household populations unknown

**interval:** Approximately 20,000–19,000 cal BP at Xianrendong; Japanese comparison approximately 14,460–8,000 cal BP

**contemporary alternatives:** Non-ceramic containers and direct cooking are candidate alternatives; availability and task-by-task use not documented in the inspected papers; Existing local stone, bone and shell tools coexist; they are not equivalent container substitutes

**adoption measure:** Dated occurrence at one Chinese site; Japanese chemical assignments among 801 selected samples, including 722 yielding interpreted lipids. No denominator of households, all pots or meals.

**observation limits:** Two separated regional subepisodes, not a demonstrated transmission chain. Associated dates, deposition, preservation and selection limit prevalence and functional inference.

## Actors

**chooser:** Unknown: prehistoric vessel makers/users are inferred from remains, with no records of household decisions.

**payer:** Unknown: clay, fuel and labour inputs existed, but household/gender ownership or resource obligations are not identified.

**operator maintainer:** Vessel-forming, firing and cooking practitioners; identities, maintenance roles and unpaid-work allocation unknown.

**affected nonchooser:** Other household members and fuel/clay collectors are possible affected parties, not identified groups in these data.

**ability to refuse:** Unknown; archaeological occurrence cannot distinguish voluntary household choice, dependence or imposed work.

## Labour

**tasks:** Forming, tempering, firing and cooking tasks; no evidence of paid employment or a distinct potter occupation.

**entry barriers:** Practical ceramic skill and access to clay/fuel; apprenticeship and social exclusion unknown.

**bargaining ownership:** Unknown: no direct evidence of ownership, bargaining or gains.

**transition:** Long temporal gaps between occurrences; no individual/cohort transition evidence.

**distribution:** No measured livelihood gains or losses by gender, class or household.

**evidence limit:** Residues and sherds cannot determine labour hours, wages, gender division or welfare.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: culinary practices; available foods; ceramic process knowledge; mobility and transport costs as untested hypotheses

Residue preservation, site selection and associated dating constrain interpretation.

## Evidence links

- [MAT-K01](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mat-k01): Xianrendong pottery-bearing contexts were dated to approximately 20,000–19,000 calibrated years BP, long before farming; this dates surviving use, not the invention of pottery.
- [MAT-K02](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mat-k02): Xianrendong fragments document vessel-making among foragers, while exterior burning makes cooking plausible rather than identifying particular foods.
- [MAT-K03](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mat-k03): The Japanese sample maintained a strong aquatic-processing association across the Pleistocene–Holocene transition, challenging a simple account in which pottery expanded principally to process newly abundant terrestrial foods.
- [MAT-K24](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#mat-k24): Across these purposively selected cases, repeatable processes, complementary crafts and task-specific demand are better-supported comparison variables than a universal ranking of materials.
