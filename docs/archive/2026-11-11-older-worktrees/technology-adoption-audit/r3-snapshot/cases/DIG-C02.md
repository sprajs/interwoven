# DIG-C02 — US household computers and internet: diffusion with coexistence

**Period:** 1984–2015. **Region:** United States. **Domain:** household computing; internet access.

CPS documents rising household access; ACS joint access demonstrates that computers, handhelds and subscriptions frequently coexisted in 2015.

[Read the substantive research dossier](../contributions/DIG/report.md).

## Stages

**invention:** Outside episode; devices and networks pre-existed surveys.

**feasibility:** Assumed operational availability in survey categories, not independently benchmarked.

**deployment:** Households report access/ownership and subscriptions.

**diffusion:** CPS computer series 1984–2015 and internet series 1997–2015.

**scaling:** Population reach grows; supply-side scale economies not identified.

**non-adoption/niche:** A substantial remainder lacks each measured form of access.

**displacement/reversal:** No wholesale replacement supported by 62% with all three categories in 2015.

## Episode

**technology configuration:** Personal computers and home internet in CPS history; 2015 ACS desktop/laptop, handheld and broadband categories.

**task:** Household access to computing and online services; actual task mix not measured by the headline indicators.

**population:** US households; CPS and ACS are separately specified samples and questions.

**interval:** CPS 1984–2015; separate ACS snapshot 2015.

**contemporary alternatives:** No home device; Public/workplace/school access, not fully measured here; Desktop/laptop and handheld devices as coexisting options

**adoption measure:** CPS household computer percentages 8/51/79 in 1984/2000/2015; household internet 18/73 in 1997/2015. ACS joint access 62% in 2015, not minutes of use.

**observation limits:** No harmonised CPS/ACS level-splice; handheld and broadband definitions broader than smartphone/fixed broadband.

## Actors

**chooser:** Household-level access reported; individual chooser or employer influence is not identified.

**payer:** Household and potentially employer resources; survey does not allocate payments.

**operator maintainer:** Household users, vendors and network providers; unpaid household technical support not measured.

**affected nonchooser:** Household members who did not purchase devices; excluded people required to use online services not separately observed.

**ability to refuse:** Non-adoption exists, but survey does not distinguish refusal, inability to pay, disability or lack of need for every household.

## Labour

**tasks:** Potential access to information, government, banking and education; no job-task time series measured here.

**entry barriers:** Income/access differences observed in report; skills, hiring credentials and discrimination not causally evaluated.

**bargaining ownership:** Ownership of devices/subscriptions measured; ownership of platforms and bargaining power not evaluated.

**transition:** Long household adoption series, not tracked worker careers.

**distribution:** Demographic/geographic access differences are reported; no distribution of wages or job gains established.

**evidence limit:** Cannot convert household computer growth into employment creation, deskilling or productivity distribution.

## Mechanisms and counterevidence

Candidate/supported mechanisms: Possible task complementarity; Affordability and demographic differences as unseparated candidates; Multiple people/devices within households

Co-ownership may represent different household members or idle devices; it does not itself prove beneficial task division.

## Evidence links

- [DIG-K03](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#dig-k03): The Census historical CPS series reports computers in about 8% of US households in 1984, 51% in 2000 and 79% in 2015; household internet use was 18% in 1997 and 73% in 2015.
- [DIG-K04](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#dig-k04): In the 2015 ACS, 78% of US households had desktop/laptop access, 75% handheld-computer access and 77% broadband; 62% had all three, showing household-level coexistence.
- [DIG-K25](../data/CLAIM_LEDGER.md#dig-k25): The 2015 US household coexistence of computers and handhelds is consistent with task complementarity, but the inspected access data cannot distinguish it from wealth, different household members or unused equipment.
