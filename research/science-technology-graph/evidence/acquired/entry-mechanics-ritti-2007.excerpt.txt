L12: The sarcophagus of M(arcus) Aur(elius) Ammianos
L13: Travellers leaving Hierapolis (Phrygia) by the gate built by Sextus Julius Frontinus, proconsul of Asia, follow a road winding for c.2 km through the north necropolis. On both sides, the road is lined by both monumental tombs and plain sarcophagi. Set on a podium or at ground level, most of the sarcophagi carry a funerary inscription.² In the 1980s, the necropolis was the subject of extensive investigations by T. Ritti and M. Waelkens. Some 800 m from the gate, on the left side of the road (fig.
L14: 1), was found half of the cover of a limestone sarcophagus (the other half of the cover and the sarcophagus itself have not yet been located³). On its intact pediment is an inscription and, intermingled with the text, a mechanical contraption outlined in relief. The inscription gives the owner’s name: M. Aur. Ammianos of Hierapolis. The contraption shows one large and two smaller wheels (fig. 2) and has recently been recognized as a water-powered twin stone saw.
L15: As the owner of a limestone (rather than a marble) sarcophagus, M. Aur. Ammianos probably did not enjoy a very high social and financial status, yet he was able to purchase a funerary plot at the preferred location next to the road. Presumably the inscription and relief were confined to the pediment facing the road since the sarcophagus’ long side would not have been much visible to passers-by. According to M. Waelkens and H.
L16: Vanhaeverbeke’s chronology of Hierapolis’ limestone sarcophagi, the dimensions of this cover are typical of sarcophagi dating after A.D. 212.⁴ They point out that sarcophagi later than the mid-3rd c. generally lack decoration, but, since this one was not made for purely aesthetic reason, a later date is not ruled out.

L19: The inscription fills the upper section of the pediment and the boss below (fig. 2). The characters are quite regular though not elegant, in the usual mid-Imperial style. The text intermingles with the relief: written between wheels of the mechanism, the lowest line is short and off centre; the last two lines appear on the boss. Letter height varies between 2.5 (ll.1-5) and 3.0 cm (ll.6-7). The space between lines is 0.5-0.7 cm.

L170: Not many drawings or images of technical constructions or machinery have been preserved from antiquity. Only in a few cases has it been possible to reconstruct the workings of a technical contraption (whether a large device or a manual tool) by means of a description or illustration by an ancient engineer or designer.
L171: Usually one has no choice but to extract information from the limited and incomplete physical remains of an apparatus in order to try to decipher the design.41 The Hierapolis relief provides new information about technicalities involved in the mechanism of a water-powered saw mill, which requires the conversion of rotary motion into linear motion. The remains of water-powered stone saw mills have survived at Ephesos and Gerasa, both of the 6th or early 7th c.
L172: A.D., and we will discuss the Hierapolis relief in relation to them.

L198: From the third wheel extend two slanted rods, one to the left, one to the right, towards what are evidently vertical saw frames. The frames consist of two vertical supports connected by two horizontal beams on the upper side and carrying a saw blade below. L199: The linear dimensions of the blocks, with horizontal to vertical ratio of c.2 : 1, suggest that they are of stone (since tree trunks or wooden beams to be sawn are either circular or much longer than they are wide). The space between the horizontal beams is less than that between the lower beam and the saw blade (ratio about 2 : 3), allowing for deep cuttings.
L200: The saws require a horizontal movement to and fro, which means that, at the third wheel, from which the rods extend towards the frames, rotation must be transformed into a reciprocal linear movement. This can be achieved only by a crank or crank disc (a circular disc with an eccentric pin) connected to the rotating shaft of the third wheel in combination with a rod. At one end the rod is movably fixed to the crank, at the other to the saw frame.
L201: When the crank turns with the wheel, the rod will push and pull the saw frame to and fro. This is the crank and connecting rod system.⁴⁴ The Hierapolis relief thus represents a twin stone sawing machine, powered by a vertical water wheel. L202: 44  With a crank a shaft may be turned by a handle. With a crank wheel or crank disc (a wheel or disc equipped with an eccentric pin made of wood or metal) the same action is achieved. A crank shaft is a shaft with an eccentric part. L386: *The invention of the water-driven stone saw mill*
L387: The differences and similarities between the mills of Ephesus and Gerasa and the saw mill of Hierapolis have been listed in Table 1. Both mills have vertical water wheels. In its extended shaft and secondary gear train, the Hierapolis mill resembles the Vitruvian water mill for grinding grain (fig. 19),85 though the right-angle gears of the Vitruvian mill become parallel gears in the Hierapolis mill.
L388: In the Vitruvian mill, the second wheel of the gear train drives the upper millstone; at Hierapolis, it drives the saw frames.
L389: The question is when and where the water-driven stone saw mill first appeared. The history, invention and diffusion of water mills, either vertical or horizontal, is complex and only a few aspects of vertical (Vitruvian) water mills will be referred to here.86

L405: B.C.).88 Gears were common in Hellenistic automata, including parallel and right-angle gears, the latter used widely from at least the 2nd c. B.C.

L431: A fourth condition had to be met for developing the Hierapolis stone saw mill: the crank and connecting rod. L452: Later developments
L453: On the Hierapolis relief the saw frames and the stone blocks are positioned to the left and right of the shaft of the water wheel. This contrasts with the situation at Ephesus and Gerasa, where the blocks are positioned on one side of the wheel shaft, next to one another, with tail race in between. The arrangement at Ephesus and Gerasa, with the water wheel next to a wall from which the water drops, does not allow for a different configuration.
L454: Perhaps at Hierapolis it was the artist who depicted the saws and the stone blocks on either side of the wheel shaft, but the configuration with parallel gears, secondary gear train and saws separated from the water wheel doubtless allows such a positioning.102
L455: 102 Nor is the balancing of the driving wheel disturbed if the eccentric pins for the connecting rods are fixed at an analogous position on the crank discs, assuming that pulling and pushing the toothless sawblades require similar force.

L458: Because of the gears that Ammianos applied for driving his saws, energy is lost to friction. At a later stage, perhaps when it was realised that the process of sawing did not need to be kept out of water and that wet conditions were actually favourable, the parallel gears were left out and the crank discs were fitted directly onto the shaft of the water wheel.
L459: Taking out the secondary gear train, which had a gearing ratio of about 1,103 reduced the friction, resulting in higher efficiency, a wheel shaft of reduced length, and less complex and cheaper machinery overall. This eventually resulted in the mills at Ephesus and Gerasa (fig. 22).
L460: **Epilogue**
L461: M. Aur. Ammianos was quite inventive working with wheels. Daedalus was the inventor of the saw. Thus Ammianos’ work had to do with both water wheels and the sawing of stones. Manually-operated stone saws had already existed for centuries, so his particular inventiveness must relate to something else.
L462: Perhaps he simply realized some minor but worthwhile improvements to sawing machines; possibly (though the inscription does not say so) he had a larger rôle in the development of the water-powered stone saw mill or the crank and connecting rod system.104 Whatever the case, the Hierapolis relief shows that this technique was known in the second half of the 3rd c. A.D.
L463: Because of the findings at Ephesus and Gerasa the invention of the crank and connecting rod system has had to be redated from the 13th to the 6th c.; now the Hierapolis relief takes it back another three centuries, which confirms that water-powered stone saw mills were indeed in use when Ausonius wrote his *Mosella*.
