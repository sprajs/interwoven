Prehistoric agricultural depletion of soil nutrients in Hawai'i

Abstract

We investigated the fate of soil nutrients after centuries of indigenous dryland agriculture in Hawai‘i using a coupled geochemical and archaeological approach. Beginning ≈500 years ago, farmers began growing dryland taro and sweet potato on the leeward slopes of East Maui. Their digging sticks pierced a subsurface layer of cinders, enhancing crop access to the soil water stored below the intact cinders. Cultivation also catalyzed nutrient losses, directly by facilitating leaching of mobile nutrients after disturbing a stratigraphic barrier to vertical water movement, and indirectly by increasing mineral weathering and subsequent uptake and harvest. As a result, centuries of cultivation lowered volumetric total calcium, magnesium, sodium, potassium, and phosphorus content by 49%, 28%, 75%, 37%, and 32%, respectively. In the absence of written records, we used the difference in soil phosphorus to estimate that prehistoric yields were sufficient to meet local demand over very long time frames, but the associated acceleration of nutrient losses could have compromised subsequent yields.

Keywords: agriculture

Since the dawn of agriculture, human societies have struggled to sustain crop yields (1–3). For example, salinization of alluvial soils in Mesopotamia necessitated a shift to salt-tolerant crops and eventually forced abandonment of agricultural fields (4). Soil degradation occurs directly through erosion and salinization and indirectly through nutrient removal at harvest, and its effects on yields may become apparent only after decades or centuries. The rate and trajectory of changes in soil fertility depend on initial site characteristics, especially the sensitivity to disturbance, as well as specific management practices (5, 6). Linking prehistoric land use practices to changes in soil properties is difficult, because detailed records are seldom preserved, and where available, they rarely quantify both agricultural practices and changes in soil fertility. Written records detail changes in crop composition and yields through time in Mesopotamia (4) and 14th century England (7) but do not address changes in soil fertility. Because data on prehistoric crop production are rare, yields from unfertilized field or greenhouse trials have been used to estimate nutrient demands in agroecosystems in Papua New Guinea (8) and New Mexico (9).

Polynesians colonized the Hawaiian archipelago between 700 and 800 C.E., bringing with them root, tuber, and other cultigens, and rapidly established agricultural landscapes in the most favorable environmental zones (10). The sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas [L.] Lam.), of South American origin, may have been introduced by later Polynesian voyagers but was certainly present before European contact. Indeed, sweet potato cultivation was key to a major phase of expansion and intensification of indigenous Polynesian agriculture into the leeward, dryland zones of the Hawaiian Islands beginning around 1400–1500 C.E. (11). By the time of initial contact with Europeans (1778–1779 C.E.), Hawaiian cultivation systems included several vast dryland field systems on the islands of Hawai‘i, Maui, and Moloka‘i, as well as intensive irrigated pondfield systems for taro (Colocasia esculenta [L.] Schott) production wherever topography and water supply permitted (12). Across the Hawaiian Islands, the absence of written records for precontact Polynesian agricultural practices and yields drives us to the archaeological record (13), to ethnographic accounts of traditional agricultural practices recorded after European contact (14), and, for this study, to the soil itself.

Local environment provided both direct and indirect constraints on cultivation of dryland crops across Hawai‘i (13, 15, 16). Gently sloping, stable surfaces on the older volcanoes (West Maui to Kaua‘i) are too infertile because of long-term nutrient leaching, whereas recent lava flows on the younger volcanoes (Hawai‘i and East Maui) have not developed a soil mantle capable of sustaining crop growth. However, intermediate-aged volcanic flows on these younger islands provide a favorable mix of gently sloping topography and (depending on climate) more fertile soils. At lower elevations on leeward slopes, aridity constrains agricultural options. As effective precipitation increases with increasing elevation on the leeward sides of volcanoes, soil fertility increases as weathering releases nutrients. As rainfall increases further, however, nutrients are leached from soils, acidity and anoxia increase, and soil fertility declines (17, 18). Leeward Hawai‘i and eastern Maui have archaeological features demonstrating that agriculture was limited to intermediate-aged substrates in a zone bounded at lower elevations by soils that are too dry and at higher elevations by soils that are too infertile to sustain agriculture (13).

Within this relatively favorable zone, the Kahikinui archaeological research area on the leeward slope of Haleakalā; Volcano, Maui, has soil stratigraphy that facilitated prehistoric agriculture (Figs. 1 and 2; and see Table 3, which is published as supporting information on the PNAS web site). Tephra blankets all lava flows older than 17 ky, covering 37% of the Kipapa-Naka’ohu survey area between 400 and 800 m elevation. The tephra is comprised of a 10- to 20-cm-thick layer of cinders that average ≈4 mm in diameter sandwiched between two layers of fine-grained ash with particle diameter <0.05 mm (Fig. 2a). The tephra package, commonly 80–100 cm thick over lava bedrock, reflects some redistribution of upper ash. This ash–cinder–ash stratigraphy has important hydrologic and agroecological consequences: undisturbed soil profiles with clear textural contrasts such as these slow the vertical movement of water (19). Winter rains could saturate the ash immediately above the cinder and drip through to the lower ash layer. Subsequently, the coarse cinder layer acted as a barrier to roots and to the capillary rise of water from the lower ash layer. Thus the lower, fine-grained ash served as a water reservoir.

During cultivation of dryland taro and sweet potato, Hawaiian cultivators punched through the cinder layer and prepared cone-shaped planting holes by the downward thrust and outward circular rotation of a digging stick (14). This produced a planting zone composed of a mixture of partially crushed cinders and fine-grained ash (Fig. 2b), and gave the crop (but not most weeds) access to the stored water beneath. These “cultivation cones” were typically ≈50 cm apart and were reused with subsequent diggings sometimes causing individual cones to coalesce into larger irregularly shaped features (Fig. 5, which is published as supporting information on the PNAS web site) (14).

In addition to increased access to water, digging stick cultivation affected the nutrient status of these soils in two ways. Because of its low water-holding capacity and low unsaturated hydraulic conductivity, the intact cinder layer had experienced limited chemical weathering. Digging sticks crushed some of the cinder particles, increasing their surface area and hence the rate of nutrient release. Second, the cinders embedded in the moist, fine-grained ash were more susceptible to nutrient release than when they were surrounded by other cinders. This enhanced release of nutrients was matched in part by nutrient removal during crop growth and in part by increased leaching losses associated with the greater hydrologic continuity of the soils.

Here, we examine the balance of changes in soil nutrient status brought on by this pattern of prehistoric cultivation. We used pairs of adjacent cultivated and undisturbed soil profiles and evaluated soil nutrients contributed from both fine-grained ash and coarse-grained cinder sources. We then assumed that the initial (parent material) element concentrations of ash and cinder were the same and used the differences in rock-derived nutrients in the cultivated zone to estimate taro and sweet potato yields, partition nutrient losses into harvest and leaching fractions, and quantify rates of nutrient loss.

Results

Cultivated profiles contained lower nutrient and higher carbon (C) concentrations than undisturbed profiles (Fig. 3). On a volumetric basis that combines <2-mm and >2-mm material, cultivation and the associated disturbance of the cinder layer resulted in nutrient losses ranging from 1 to 15 Mg·ha−1; soil C increased by 18 Mg·ha−1 (Table 1). These nutrient losses, however, were not uniform with depth. The greatest contrast in nutrient concentrations occurred between the intact cinder (Fig. 4e) and the cultivated cinder/ash horizons (Fig. 4b). Concentrations of calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), sodium (Na), potassium (K), and phosphorus (P) in cultivated cinder/ash horizons were 65%, 22%, 87%, 55%, and 46% lower, respectively, than in the adjacent intact cinders. Differences between the upper (Fig. 4 a and d) or lower (Fig. 4 c and f) ash horizons were generally much smaller (<38%). Volumetrically, losses were greatest for Ca and smallest for P; Na losses were more than double those of K, although Na and K have comparable solubilities. These patterns reflect differences in parent material abundance (Ca > Na > K > P), solubility, and plant nutritional importance (20).

Nutrient differences were also assessed just for >2-mm cinder material; these cinders are small and fragile, and therefore susceptible to breakage and chemical weathering. The >2-mm fraction of cinders mixed with ash in cultivated cinder/ash horizons contained concentrations of Ca, Na, K, and P that were 54%, 74%, 65%, and 25% lower compared with the >2-mm fraction in intact cinder horizons (Fig. 4 b and e and Table 2). The undisturbed ash horizon below the intact cinders also has a small amount of >2-mm cinders that settled into small cracks and holes at the time of initial cinder fall, and these cinders showed corresponding nutrient losses that ranged from 44% to 80% (Fig. 4 e and f and Table 2).

Discussion

The lower nutrient status of the cultivated profiles is caused by enhanced nutrient release and subsequent loss. Crushing and mixing of cinders into the fine-grained ash allowed persistent wetting of cinders, favoring weathering. Mixing of cinders and ash also increased hydrologic continuity, triggering differential element removal by plant harvest or by downward leaching of water. The alternative explanation for this pattern, that the differences between undisturbed and cultivated soils result from mixing nutrient-poor ash with nutrient-rich cinder, is untenable. It does not provide consistent loss estimates for multiple nutrients and does not account for the relatively rapid and large weathering losses from cinders after mixing with ash. In addition, by indexing total (<2- and >2-mm) soil P to P concentrations in parent material (best approximated by >2-mm cinder in intact cinder horizons) using the immobile element niobium (21, 22), we find that cinder/ash horizons only contain 54% of the P that was present in parent material. The amount of other elements remaining from parent material ranged from 12% (Na) to 56% (K).

The much smaller nutrient differences between the overlying or underlying ash horizons in cultivated versus uncultivated areas could be due to a number of poorly constrained factors. First, ash, with its greater surface area and water holding capacity, weathers more completely than cinders, independent of whether the ash was in a cultivated or undisturbed profile. Thus, weathering that began after tephra deposition >17 ky ago reduced the nutrient concentrations for ash horizons in undisturbed profiles, overshadowing any effects of cultivation. Second, digging sticks could have mixed upper ash material horizontally between cultivated and undisturbed profiles. Finally, downward percolation of nutrients from the cinder/ash mix could have offset any nutrient losses from the cultivated lower ash horizon.

Estimating Prehistoric Yields.

Virtually all of the documented decline in P is due to crop removal because P is relatively immobile in these volcanic soils (23) and because our focus on subsurface horizons minimizes the impact of potential surface erosion on P balances. We use this one-way allocation of P to estimate prehistoric dryland taro corm and sweet potato tuber yields on a dry-weight basis (Table 1). Based on >170 radiocarbon dates, we estimated that this area was intensively cultivated for 300 years. Because taro and sweet potato have similar P concentrations, estimated yields are comparable: 2.1 and 2.6 Mg·ha−1·y−1, respectively. Modern taro yields from Oceania range from 1.6 to 6.6 Mg·ha−1·y−1 (24). The sweet potato estimate falls within the range of reported low-input yields from across Oceania (1.5–7.2 Mg·ha−1·y−1) (25–27) and of modeled prehistoric yields (1.2–3.1 Mg·ha−1·y−1) from a dryland agricultural zone on neighboring Hawai‘i (28) but is about one-third the average yield (7.3 Mg·ha−1·y−1) for 16 unfertilized, multiyear cropping trials examining yield decline in Papua New Guinea (8).

Our comparison of cultivated and undisturbed soil P does not account for mulching inputs of P; mulch P added to cultivated profiles could cause us to understate P removals, and so underestimate yields. In a 1793 diary entry, Archibald Menzies (botanist on the Vancouver exploring expedition) noted that dryland fields on Hawai‘i were “covered with a thick layer of hay” (29). If we assume that the C surplus in cultivation cones was derived from mulch with a C:P ratio of 500 and take into account respiration losses of C, we estimate that mulching could have added 2.5 Mg of P·ha−1, boosting yields from 2 to 7 Mg·ha−1·y−1. There are uncertainties associated with these estimates, but these calculations help to frame the caloric importance of dryland production for supporting human densities estimated for this area. For example, local densities of ≈50 persons km−2, as estimated with independent archaeological data of radiocarbon-dated household complexes (13), would have required rootcrop dry yields of 0.6 Mg·ha−1·y−1, assuming that rootcrops contain 3,300,000 kcal·Mg−1 (30), per capita daily consumption of 2,000 kcal, and that only half of the suitable soils in the agricultural zone were cultivated. In addition to leguminous plants, mulch may have been an important source of N for rootcrops. Although cultivated profiles contain 1.0 Mg·ha−1 more total N than undisturbed profiles, taro harvests would have removed 21 kg of N·ha−1·y−1 assuming that taro corms contain 1% N (24).

Partitioning Harvest and Leaching Losses.

This long-term taro yield estimate can be coupled to the elemental stoichiometry of taro to approximate the fraction of nutrient depletion due to harvest and to leaching. For most nutrients other than P, observed depletions in cultivation cones were largely due to leaching rather than harvest (Table 1). However, calculated harvest removal of K was 10 Mg·ha−1, more than the difference between cultivated and undisturbed profiles. It is likely that our calculation overestimated K in cultivated soils because centuries of root uptake and translocation to the soil surface by plants could have resulted in soluble K moving from the soil surface into the depth interval used for our calculations. This possibility is supported by our characterization of full cultivated and undisturbed profiles, which do not show the commonly documented exponential decline in base cation concentrations below the soil surface (31) (Fig. 6, which is published as supporting information on the PNAS web site).

Rates of P and K Depletion.

Depletion rates expressed on an annualized basis were comparable with those reported for other low-input agroecosystems. We used two different time intervals to compare the rates at which nutrients were depleted: 300 years for immobile P and 500 years (including the postagricultural period) for mobile K. Losses of P averaged 4 kg·ha−1·y−1, similar to losses of ≈3 kg·ha−1·y−1 reported for sub-Saharan agroecosystems (32, 33) even though those rates are calculated for a period of decades and not centuries. Modeled prehistoric P losses due to harvest from dryland fields on Hawai‘i averaged 1.4 kg·ha−1·y−1 (28). We have two estimates for K losses, one based on differences between cultivated and undisturbed profiles (10 kg·ha−1·y−1) and another based solely on the calculated quantity of K removed by taro harvests (34 kg ha−1 y−1). These losses are more than an order of magnitude greater than those reported for an English grain field (7) but bracket the losses for sub-Saharan agroecosystems (32, 33), although again those African losses were calculated over shorter time intervals.

Nutrient concentrations of >2-mm cinders in cultivated cinder/ash horizons as well as the undisturbed ash horizons underlying intact cinders allow us to compare the rates of nutrient losses due to cultivation with those due to natural processes. Natural weathering processes can be estimated by measuring nutrient losses from >2-mm cinders embedded in the undisturbed ash underlying intact cinders. Considering only >2-mm material, P losses during the (at most) 500 years since intensive cultivation of these soils began amounted to 42% of the P lost from ash horizons that had been undisturbed by humans for >17 ky (Table 2). The effect of this reduction in soil P on crop yields cannot be modeled explicitly, but yields are sensitive to the dynamics of soil fertility as well as to changing social and economic conditions, and these dynamics are unlikely to be linear in space or time (1, 6). Between 1608 and 1638 C.E., for example, a ritual control system manifested by a network of agricultural temples was imposed (34), implying a phase of rapid intensification of agricultural production across the Kahikinui landscape. Such a phase of intensive cropping could have initiated rapid loss of soil fertility 150 years before European contact.

There are limits to such historical insight, and we cannot constrain all of the possible explanations for the large nutrient depletions we observed. However, farmers in Kahikinui took advantage of specific combinations of climate and soil characteristics to grow rootcrops that, on independent archaeological evidence, sustained a relatively high-density local population up to European contact. Our field observations and geochemical and archaeological data suggest that these practices accelerated nutrient losses through harvest and leaching, and these losses could have compromised subsequent yields.

Materials and Methods

Physical Setting.

In Kahikinui, cultivation occurred between ≈400 and 800 m elevation with a range of mean annual precipitation of ≈500–750 mm (35). The agricultural sites sampled in this study are clearly defined by a mosaic of different age lava flows and archaeological features associated with them (Fig. 1). The local landscape is composed of recent lava flows with rocky and thin soils that have partly buried older, tephra-covered, flows. Hawaiians built household and temple complexes on these recent lava flows where they overlooked swales containing thicker soils that formed the basis of their agriculture (13). Lava flows older than 17 ky cover 37% of the agricultural zone within the Kipapa-Naka’ohu study area. Because not all of this tephra-covered area was likely to have been cultivated simultaneously because of edge effects and/or fallowing, we halved this fraction to estimate that ≈20% of the agricultural zone supported cultivation in any given year. Additional studies and discussions of prehistoric agricultural sites and practices within Kahikinui are presented elsewhere (36, 37), and inferred cultivation practices have been compared with ethnographically documented Hawaiian planting practices (14).

Within the swales, we documented changes in soil fertility by comparing undisturbed reference sites with cultivated sites. Our team examined 75 distinct soil profiles and documented intact cinder horizons in 17 profiles. Cultivated soils were only found adjacent to undisturbed cinders in 11 of these 17 profiles, all within the agricultural zone; 10 of these profiles were sampled for geochemical analyses (Fig. 1). The features we interpret as cultivated soils are unlikely to have been produced by tree roots (14) or fossorial animals (38). Our approach to quantifying prehistoric nutrient depletion required a number of assumptions because soils are open chemical systems and nutrient losses have continued to the present. In addition, because we targeted cultivated soils, our samples varied nonrandomly with respect to elevation, rainfall, and lava flow age.

At all 10 locations, soils (Humic Haplustands) were described by using standard methods (39). We sampled profiles consisting of the undisturbed cinder horizon or cultivated cinder/ash horizon and their overlying and underlying ash horizons (Fig. 2). For every profile, we centered a 30-cm sampling frame at the midpoint of the intact cinder or adjacent cinder/ash horizon; this zone extended into a portion of the fine-grained ash both above and below the intact cinder layer. This depth interval brackets the largest differences in chemistry between cultivated and undisturbed profiles (Figs. 3 and 6) and minimizes the influence of historic-period changes to surface soils. In every case where soil profiles or larger excavations exposed undisturbed ash–cinder–ash stratigraphy, roots were restricted to the ash layers. The upper boundaries of cultivated cinder/ash horizons ranged from 27 to 100 cm. Cattle and goats probably redistributed ash over the deepest of these cultivated horizons (36), and our sampling was designed to minimize these recent disturbances. Profiles excavated 20–50 m upslope of deep profiles (with smaller upslope contributing areas) were always shallower. The 10 cultivation cones averaged 25 ± 3 cm in diameter at the depth of the adjacent cinder layer (midpoint 54 ± 7 cm below the surface). Surface horizons were sampled at a few sites (Fig. 6) but were not used in the comparisons because their morphology showed evidence of modification by cattle grazing, which occurred from 1870 to 1990 C.E.

Laboratory Measurements.

We measured exchangeable nutrients, total nutrient concentrations, total carbon, and total nitrogen in the <2-mm fraction, the traditional fraction of agronomic interest, and total nutrient concentrations in the >2-mm fraction, which consisted entirely of cinders. Exchangeable nutrients provide a direct index of plant-available nutrient supply, but their mobility (uptake and cycling) means that values exhibit high spatial and temporal variability, particularly at the soil surface. Coefficients of variation for resin-extractable P and exchangeable potassium (K+), for example, across the 10 paired sets of subsurface samples are 70% and 127%, respectively (n = 60). Total forms of nutrients, by contrast, include the reservoirs from which exchangeable forms are derived and provide a more stable indication of the long-term nutrient capital for this area of prehistoric farming; the corresponding coefficients of variation for total P and K in the <2-mm fraction are 29% and 55%, respectively.

Soil samples were dried and sieved (<2 mm) and analyzed for cation exchange capacity as well as exchangeable forms of Ca, Mg, Na, and K (NH4OAc method at pH 7 modified from ref. 40). The exchangeable form of P was resin-extractable P (41). Total forms of Ca, Mg, Na, K, P, and niobium were determined by x-ray fluorescence (ALS Chemex, Sparks, NV), and total C and N were analyzed with a LECO (St. Joseph, MI) CN analyzer. For <2-mm cultivated cinder/ash soils, the fraction of total nutrients in plant-available forms ranged from <1% (P) to 49% (Ca). In the field, we estimated horizon colors, textures, and structure (Table 3). In the laboratory, we measured soil pH (1:1 H2O), coarse fragment content (>2 mm), and bulk densities (132-cm3 brass rings). All coarse fragment and bulk density determinations are averages of duplicate samples from each of 54 horizons; coefficients of variation averaged <30%. Coarse fragments for cultivated and undisturbed horizons ranged from 15% to 21% and from 18% to 74%, respectively; bulk densities for all horizons ranged from 0.53 to 0.65 Mg·m−3. We estimated coarse fragment content, bulk density, and >2-mm total element concentrations for one profile.

We calculated the mass-based nutrient concentrations for each horizon using the <2-mm and >2-mm total nutrient data and the corresponding coarse fragment content. We then calculated volumetric estimates of nutrient content (X) over a 30-cm depth interval bracketing either the cinder or corresponding cinder/ash horizon using horizon-specific bulk densities (Y; n = 54): X [Mg·(Mg soil)−1] × Y [Mg soil·m−3] × 104 m2·ha−1 × 0.3 m = X [Mg·ha−1·(0.3 m)−1]. Although the 30-cm interval we used for our volumetric calculations brackets the cinder and cinder/ash horizons where we observed the greatest differences in nutrients, this interval does not extend to the surface or fully encompass the root zone. Nutrient differences between cultivated and undisturbed profiles above the overlying ash horizon, however, were small (Fig. 6) and therefore did not affect our yield estimate. The volumetric nutrient losses from cultivated profiles we report here (1.2–15.0 Mg·ha−1) account for all material, both <2 and >2 mm. Typically, only <2-mm material is considered in agronomic studies, but in this case such calculations would have produced misleading gains (0.3–3.1 Mg·ha−1).

Statistical Analyses.

We used a t test to assess nutrient differences between cultivated cinder/ash and undisturbed cinder horizons; P values were Dunn–Sidak-corrected for multiple comparisons. Total Ca, Mg, Na, and P were significantly greater in intact cinder horizons (P ≤ 0.034); total C and N were significantly lower (P = 0.001) and cation exchange capacity was marginally lower (P = 0.060) in intact cinder horizons. We found no significant differences between total K (P = 0.273) or exchangeable base cations, resin-extractable P, or pH (P ≥ 0.190).
