L4: Background
L5: There is archaeological evidence of textile production in Britain from the late-prehistoric period onwards. For many
L6: thousands of years wool was the staple textile product of Britain. The dominance of wool in the British textile industry
L7: changed rapidly during the eighteenth century with the development of mechanised silk production and then
L8: mechanised cotton production.
L9: By the mid-nineteenth century all four major branches of the textile industry (cotton, wool, flax, hemp and jute and
L10: silk) had been mechanised and the British landscape was dominated by over 10,000 mill buildings with their distinctive
L11: chimneys. Overseas competition led to a decline in the textile industry in the mid-twentieth century. Today woollen
L12: production is once again the dominant part of the sector together with artificial and man-made fibres, although output is
L13: much reduced from historic levels.
L14: Innovation
L15: Thomas Lombe’s silk mill, built in 1721, is regarded as the first factory-based textile mill in Britain. However, it was not
L16: until the handloom was developed following the introduction of John Kay’s flying shuttle in 1733 that other branches of
L17: the textile industry (notably cotton and wool) became increasingly mechanised.
L18: In the second half of the eighteenth century, a succession of major innovations including James Hargreaves’s spinning
L19: jenny (1764), Richard Arkwright’s water frame (1769), his carding engine (1775), and Samuel Crompton’s mule (1779),
L20: revolutionised the preparation and spinning of cotton and wool and led to the establishment of textile factories where
L21: several machines were housed under one roof. Although the spinning jenny remained largely hand-powered, many forms
L22: of the new machinery were driven by horse or water-power and later by steam. The term ‘mill’ was used to describe textile
L23: factories because so many, like the earlier corn mills, were water-powered. The textile industry pioneered the use of the
L24: factory system.
L25: The textile industry in the mid-eighteenth century
L26: During the initial stages of industrialisation, water was used to power a range of processes usually housed in separate
L27: buildings. These early attempts at mechanisation ran alongside domestic-based weaving in small dwellings.
L28: The first purpose-built textile buildings were water-powered fulling mills, established as early as the thirteenth century
L29: to wash and pound woollen cloth. Scribbling, the process of disentangling the woollen fibres was followed by carding,
L30: a process by which the fibres were separated using wooden bats into which wires had been inserted. Carding was first
L31: mechanised for the cotton industry in 1775 by Arkwright’s carding engine, a development which led to a boom in carding
L32: mill structures during the 1780s and 1790s.
L33: Jenny shops began to appear from the 1760s, housing hand-, horse- and water-powered jennies spinning both cotton
L34: and wool. Hargreaves’s spinning jenny could spin multiple ends of yarn on one machine and held between 80 and 120
L35: spindles worked by a single large wheel. These machines did not require purpose-built structures and were often installed
L36: in barns or weaver’s cottages. Increasingly jennies were housed together with carding machines in a single building which
L37: came before the integrated cotton or woollen mill.

L50: Flax for linen required a different preparation process from cotton and wool. Flax stems were soaked or ‘retted’ to soften
L51: them, then beaten to break them up. This process often took place in water-powered ‘scutching’ mills. The carding
L52: process for flax was called ‘heckling’ or ‘hackling’.
L53: Spinning mills
L54: Although industrial-scale mills were first introduced in the silk industry, it was the cotton industry that popularised the
L55: spinning mill. The earliest cotton spinning mills were those built to house Arkwright’s water frame. Arkwright’s first water-
L56: powered mill opened at Cromford, Derbyshire, in 1771. From the 1770s to the early decades of the nineteenth century,
L57: the Derbyshire cotton industry was at the forefront of textile manufacturing technology, both in the development of the
L58: powered process and in the construction and design of the mill building. The isolated location of these mills also led to
L59: the development of the factory colony.
L60: These cotton spinning mills were generally 30ft (10m) wide, up to 100ft (33m) long and between three and six storeys high.
L61: The ground and first floors housed the preparatory cotton production processes such as carding, drawing and roving
L62: whilst the middle floors housed the water frames, and the top floors were used for reeling and storage.
L63: The first use of steam-power in cotton spinning took place in 1786 at Papplewick Mill in Nottinghamshire. In 1789, a
L64: Boulton and Watt steam engine was successfully installed in Piccadilly Mill in Manchester. During the 1790s Crompton’s
L65: mule was adapted for steam-power in the city so that by 1800 there were more than 40 cotton spinning mills in the
L66: Manchester area, of which over half were steam-powered.
L67: The dust from the cotton spinning process created conditions likely to lead to fires and so from the end of the eighteenth
L68: century ‘fireproof’ mills using cast iron columns and brick arches were developed. An early experiment was Ditherington
L69: Flax Mill in Shrewsbury. Built between 1796-97 to a design by Charles Bage, it was the world’s first wholly iron framed
L70: building. This mill set the pattern for many of the fireproof mills of the early-nineteenth century. William Strutt’s
L71: correspondence with Bage led to a similar system being adopted for his North Mill in Belper (1803-4), replacing the mills
L72: on his site that had burned down. Mill fireproofing in the first half of the nineteenth century was led by the mill engineer
L73: William Fairbairn who worked with the scientist Eaton Hodgkinson to develop a distinctive brick arched system supported
L74: by cast iron beams on circular section cast iron columns.
L75: The last traditional cotton spinning mill to have been built in Lancashire is considered to be Elk Mill, Oldham, erected
L76: 1925-28, and demolished in 1999. With the decline of the industry the building of traditionally designed flax, silk and wool
L77: spinning mills ended in the mid-twentieth century.
L78: Integrated textile mills
L79: The preparatory processes of the textile industry began to be housed in the same mill building from the mid- to late-
L80: eighteenth century. The integrated textile mill - where raw materials entered the mill and grey, unbleached cloth left -
L81: developed due to the introduction of steam-powered weaving in the 1820’s. Many mills achieved integration through the
L82: addition of a process to an earlier specialised mill. In the cotton and flax industries this usually meant the addition of a
L83: weaving shed to an existing spinning mill. In the production of woollen cloth, powered spinning and later weaving might

L95: Child labour in the textile industry
L96: Many of those working in textile mills in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries were children who often came from
L97: workhouses. Child apprentices began work at the age of nine and were given food, lodgings and an hour of schooling a week.
L98: 
L99: 
L100: Samuel Downe, who was born in Shrewsbury in 1804, worked in Ditherington Flax Mill from the age of 10. He described
L101: working conditions in the factory during a Parliamentary Enquiry in 1832:
L102:  ‘we used to generally begin at five o’clock in the morning till eight at night’. When asked had he received
L103: punishment he replied ‘yes, I was strapped most severely till I could not bear to sit upon a chair without
L104: pillows, and I was forced to lie upon my face at night. I was put upon a man’s back and then strapped by
L105: the overlooker’.  When asked why he was punished he replied… ‘I had never been in a mill where there
L106: was machinery, and it was winter time, and we worked by gas-light, and I could not catch the revolutions
L107: of the machinery to take the tow out of the hackles; it requires some practice and I was timid at it.’
L108: Improvements followed, and in 1834 the 92 children working at Ditherington Flax Mill only worked part-time and had
L109: some schooling between 9-11 am and 3-5 pm.
L110: The future of historic textile mills
L111: In the twentieth century thousands of derelict textile mills were demolished in all the industrial areas of Britain as the
L112: traditional industries became smaller and the appearance of historic mill towns was dramatically altered. The mill
L113: chimneys which were once a distinctive feature of mill towns almost disappeared completely.
L114: By the 1970s it was beginning to be recognised that textile mills and related buildings were historically important. As more
L115: people became interested in industrial heritage, the more significant mills were protected by listing or the designation
L116: of conservation areas and many are often now preserved alongside other types of historic buildings in a town. Former
L117: mills have been converted into a wide variety of new uses including flats, offices, shops or adapted for new types of
L118: industry. Other features that were associated with mills, such as warehouses and canals, are also being conserved, so the
L119: restoration of historic mills has become an important part of the regeneration of many former industrial towns.
L120: The international importance of the textile industry in Britain has been recognised by inscribing the Derwent Valley Mills
L121: which includes Cromford, Saltaire (Yorkshire) and New Lanark (Scotland) as World Heritage Sites.
L122: Further information on Saltaire, a purpose-built textile settlement with model housing for the workforce, can be found in

L51: ## Industry
L52: 
L53: Since the borough of Gedling is a patchwork of rural and urban communities it has attracted a variety of industries. Apart from the usual cottage industries associated with village life, such as blacksmiths and basket makers the borough attracted several major industries, which included:
L54: 
L55: Textiles
L56: Gedling Borough has a long association with textiles. In 1589 William Lee, curate of Calverton, invented a knitting machine for making long stockings, or ‘hose’. The machine became known as a stocking frame and the workers were called framework knitters or ‘stockingers’. By the 18th century, framework knitting was used for making gloves as well as long stockings or hosiery. It was known as a ‘cottage industry’ as workers had a frame installed in their home or in a nearby workshop.
L57: Most villages in the borough had framework knitters living and working there. In other parts of Nottinghamshire, similar knitting frames were later adapted for making bobbin-net lace.

L63: In 1778, George Robertson built his first cotton spinning factory, between Linby and Papplewick, to make cotton thread. During the next 15 years they opened five more mills along the river Leen between Papplewick, Bestwood and Bulwell. George Robinson & Sons was, famously, in 1785 the first company in the world to use a steam engine for powering a textile factory.
L64: The cotton mills were some of the first factories in the area, attracting workers from the surrounding villages. The cotton mills closed in 1828, but the remains of the storage ponds and channels supplying the mills with water have been excavated and are preserved in Moor Pond Woods. In 1794, John Hawksley and Robert Davison built a mill in Arnold to make worsted yarn from long fibres of wool. They also installed an early steam engine, using it to pump water onto their waterwheel.
L65: Davison & Hawksley did not confine themselves to thread production, also employing framework knitters to make clothing. By 1803 they employed 2,000 people. To meet the enormous number of orders, especially for cloth for army uniforms, the firm introduced shift working so that the mill could operate 24 hours a day. The factory closed in 1810 and was quickly demolished.
L66: John Hawksley lived at Arnot Hill House and Robert Davison at Bonington House on Arnold High Street. Both houses still exist, Bonington House now the Labour Club. The remains of the factory millpond can still be seen in Arnot Hill Park.
L67: 
L68: In Netherfield, Bourne’s steam powered Britannia Mill was a major supplier of doubled yarn.
L69: In the 1860s, I. & R. Morley began to develop and use hosiery machinery in its steam-powered factories. The firm expanded in the 1870s, buying a small hosiery factory in Daybrook and rebuilding it in 1883. The premises were extended in 1911 and although the factory closed in 1963, the imposing building has been conserved and converted into homes, still visible on Nottingham Road, near Daybrook Square.

L74: Brickworks
L75: 
L76: 【41†Image】
L77: In the 19th century new large-scale brickmaking companies replaced small family firms. Several companies were formed after the 1850s, financed by local investors, enabling large areas of clay-bearing land to be purchased.  Examples of these were the Mar Hill Brick and the Nottingham Builders Brick companies, both based along Carlton Hill and most significantly, the Nottingham Patent Brick company with works at Carlton and Mapperley who acquired the Dorket Head works of Messrs.
L78: Robinson & Sykes in 1897.  Like many collieries, Bestwood had dedicated brickworks located about a mile to the north of Bestwood Village, these were used to build the original sixty-four dwellings and colliery buildings in Bestwood Village.
L79: One claim to fame is that the Nottingham Patent Brick Company supplied sixty million bricks to the Midland Railway for its St Pancras Station terminus in London.

L90: Iron Works
L91: 
L92: 【46†Image】
L93: The friars from Newstead Priory developed a water-powered ironworks near Bestwood in the 16th century. Known in the 18th century as Bulwell Forge, it was recorded as using water-powered tilt-hammers to shape the iron. The ironworks was closed in 1780 and the site was converted into a water-powered cotton mill by the Robinson family. The present Forge Mill, close to Bestwood Mill Lakes Country Park, was built on the foundations of the cotton mill after this was gutted by fire in 1842.
L94: Following the success of the coal mine at Bestwood, the development of Bestwood ironworks was the next step in industrial progress. The ironworks opened in 1881 with two furnaces, a further two added in 1890. The lads of Bestwood Village were said to have made good use of the flames of the furnaces, using the light to play football late into the evening.
L95: During World War 1, with the threat of German Zeppelin raids, the manager of the ironworks would receive a warning from the military to dowse the furnaces. Bestwood ironworks closed in 1928. See also: 【47†Coal mining and railways†gedlingheritage.co.uk】
