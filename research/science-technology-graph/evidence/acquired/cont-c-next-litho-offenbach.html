The invention of lithography

On a business trip to Vienna in 1799, Johann Anton André, a composer and music publisher from Offenbach am Main, read an advertisement in a Munich newspaper offering a new lithographic printing process. André met Alois Senefelder, the inventor of this new technique, who demonstrated the invention to him in Munich. The businessman André was immediately convinced and acquired the rights. In December 1799, Senefelder and his partner, the Munich court musician Franz Gleißner, traveled to Offenbach to set up a music printing press there. Mozart's piano concertos were the first lithographic prints from 1800 onwards.

Alois Senefelder

Johann Nepomuk Franz Alois Senefelder was born in Prague on November 6, 1771, the son of the actor Johann Peter Senefelder and Katharina Volck. During his school years in Munich, he excelled in physics, chemistry and mechanics. After leaving school, he intended to pursue a career as an actor, like his father. However, he listened to his father's advice and initially studied law and cameral (financial) sciences for three years.

In his free time, Senefelder wrote plays. After his father's death (1792), he wanted to devote himself entirely to the dramatic arts as a poet and actor. For two years he toured German cities with a company of actors - without much success. He gave up and tried to earn a living as a playwright.

Publishers for his work were hard to find and printing was expensive. Senefelder decided to reproduce his plays himself. It was not the desire to develop a new printing process, but material hardship that forced Senefelder to look for a way to print his own plays quickly and cheaply.

He experimented with various possibilities. After many attempts, he practiced the etching technique on a smoothly polished Solnhofen limestone slab using a self-developed "black ink made of wax, tallow, soap, pine soot (derived from resinous components of spruce bark) and rainwater". These stone slabs were often used in Munich to cover hallways. By chance, he discovered that the stone surface not covered with his wax ink could be etched away with water (nitric acid). The highly etched writing could be blackened with a letterpress pad and printed on paper.

Senefelder joined forces with the court musician Franz Gleißner. The first work printed was a "Feldmarsch der Churpfalzbayer'schen Truppen". However, the initial financial successes could not be repeated.

In 1797, Senefelder drew with soap on the polished stone, poured gum water (gum arabic solution) over it and blackened the drawing with oil paint. The greasy areas caused by the soap took on the color, the rest of the stone remained white. Chemical lithography (planographic printing) was invented.

In the same year, technical difficulties with the copper printing presses previously used led to the construction of the wooden bar press with a grater instead of a roller.

The beginnings of success

On a business trip to Vienna in 1799, Anton André read an advertisement in a Munich newspaper in which the business partners Franz Gleißner and Alois Senefelder offered a newly invented lithographic printing process. André got to know Alois Senefelder. The invention was demonstrated to him in the Munich workshop.

The lithographic printing technique convinced the businessman immediately: "75 pages in a quarter of an hour, of which two are always printed at the same time", this made it possible to supply music printed in lithography for 6 Kreuzer per sheet. Copperplate printing had previously cost 24-30 kreuzer per sheet.

On September 28, 1799, Senefelder and the court musician Franz Gleißner signed a contract with the music publisher and printer Anton André and sold the secret of printing sheet music and pictures on stone to André for 2000 guilders.

They agreed that Senefelder should travel to Offenbach, set up the press there and train people to be able to write and print properly on stone.

"... Messrs. Gleisner and Senefelder in Munich agree to communicate to Mr. Anton André from Offenbach a/m the secret they have invented of printing notes and pictures on stone and to show him everything that is to be done in this respect without the slightest concealment, so that the aforementioned Mr. André is perfectly entitled to use both in his music printing at will ..."

"... Mr. Senefelder will travel to Offenbach a/m in order to have all the machines necessary for such a lithographic printing shop and what is furthermore needed for it produced under his direction and with the assistance of Mr. A. André, and will also travel the area there in order to seek out the type of stone necessary for this. All travel expenses will be paid by Mr. A. André ..."

"... For the communication of this secret, Mr. A. André undertakes to pay Mr. Gleisner and Mr. Senefelder two thousand guilders in the following manner: three hundred guilders after the signing of the present contract, seven hundred guilders after the sale of three months from today and one thousand guilders after one year from today ..."

"... The present contract has been executed in duplicate and has been read carefully by the undersigned and signed and sealed with his own hand."

"In December 1799, Senfelder and Gleißner traveled to Offenbach. Senefelder lived in the André house in Domstraße. Andrés provided food and clothing. A friendly relationship developed between the publisher and the inventor. André tried to make Senefelder's stay in Offenbach as pleasant as possible and treated him like his own brother."

Senefelder: "So that I had the exercise I needed for my health, he even kept me a riding horse of my own, and when he bought a new item of clothing for himself, I certainly received a similar one from him as a gift, just as I often had to take part in all the amusements that took place in and outside the house, almost against my will, because sometimes I would have preferred to work. So I had absolutely no further needs ..."

The time of material need was over. But Senefelder had no relationship with money. He left almost all his income to his business partner Gleißner. An entry in André's journal (March 1801) shows that André redeemed Senefelder's pledged watch from a Jewish woman for 38 guilders 6 kreuzer.

Lithography

Senefelder summarized the possibilities of lithography (Greek "lith" = stone) that he had developed as follows: Production of typefaces, musical scores, maps, tables, circulars (circular letters), as well as the reproduction of black-and-white and color images, woodcuts, hand drawings, copperplate engravings.

In 1799, Elector Maximilian Joseph granted the long-awaited Privilegium exclusivum for the Electorate of Bavaria for a period of 15 years. Under penalty of a fine of 100 ducats and confiscation of all supplies and tools, it was forbidden to compete with Senefelder and Gleißner. The privilege applied to everything that could be printed on stone, both in black and in color. In 1801, he was also granted this privilege for Great Britain and Austria.

Message (from the "Kurfürstlich gnädigst privilegirte Münchner-Zeitung", Sept. 26, 1799)

Se.[ine] Kurfürstl.[iche] Durchlaucht haben sich den 3. Sept.[ember] d.[es] J.[es].[ahres] to graciously grant us, the undersigned, the privilegium exclusivum tax- and seal-free for fifteen consecutive years for our newly invented, hitherto unknown method of printing on stone, so that we can print everything that can be printed on stone, and that we shall be entitled to print everything that can be printed on stone, and to use it both in black and in color in the highest parts of the Electorate, as we deem useful for our better advancement, so that no one may erect a similar printing press and imitate our art under penalty of 100 ducats. - With the deepest feelings of gratitude for this supreme grace, we hereby make this known to everyone so that they can contact us with orders. Through our diligence and accuracy, we will endeavor to earn the trust of the public in every case. Our home is in the Schmädlisches Haus in Residenzgasse.
Munich, September 23, 1799 Franz Gleißner, Court Musician

The invention was first used commercially from 1799 in the Offenbach "Notenfabrique" of Anton André with great success. In 1801, Senefelder moved from Offenbach to Vienna. On January 18, 1803, he received a privilege for Austria, limited to 10 years. After busy but ultimately unsuccessful years, he returned to Munich and in 1807, together with Gleißner and other partners, set up his own lithographic print shop for sheet music and art prints.

Of great importance was the active assistance of Johann Christian von Mannlich, then director of the Centralgalerie München (now the Pinakothek), who published the now famous reproduction prints "Les Oeuvres Lithographique".

The two lithographers Johann Nepomuk Strixner and Ferdinand Piloty worked for Senefelder. He had the most capable printer in his pupil Franz Weishaupt. However, he himself was unable to derive any profitable benefit from his invention.

Senefelder entered the Bavarian civil service with the rank of "Royal Inspector of Lithography". Between 1819 and 1824, he spent most of his time in Paris. There he published his textbook in French and worked on improving his stone set. Together with Treuttel & Würtz, he founded the Lithographic Institute Senefelder & Comp in Paris. In 1824, Senefelder returned to Munich for good. Even in the last years of his life, he was constantly striving to improve his inventions.

Alois Senefelder died in Munich on February 26, 183.

In the mid-1930s, highly qualified color lithographs were already being produced in Mühlhausen/Alsace and in London. Towards the end of the 19th century, color lithographs by French artists were world-famous. What had begun with the printing of sheet music had become the artistic medium of democratic sentiment. Criticism of social and political grievances was expressed in lithography, especially in the prints of Honoré Daumier. The lithographed caricature reached all sections of the population through the mass circulation of the daily press and in illustrated sheets.

In his textbook, Senefelder said: "I hope that (lithography) will soon spread all over the world, that it will bring mankind a variety of benefits through many excellent products and contribute to its greater refinement, but that it will never be misused for evil purposes. May the Almighty grant this! Then blessed be the hour in which I invented it!"

The wish has come true. Lithography forms the procedural basis of modern offset printing.

First successfully operating lithographic print shop

There were 10 copper and tin printing presses in the music printing shop. Half of them were replaced by lithographic bar presses and the workers were trained accordingly. Turnover soared. Inspired by the company's success, Andre set up branches in London and Paris and had them managed by his brothers Philipp and Friedrich. Further branches were planned in Berlin and Vienna.

On behalf of Andre, Senefelder traveled to London in 1800 to support the branch there. On June 20, 1801, he received the privilege for England and Ireland and on August 3, 1801, for Scotland. After a seven-month stay in London, Senefelder returned to Offenbach. Disputes over a privilege for Austria led to a temporary break between Senefelder and André in 1801.

In 1802, Friedrich André had obtained a patent in Paris. He thus triggered the development of artistic lithography in France.

While lithography was initially only intended for reproduction, it was soon also used for the fine arts in Germany. The engraver François Johannot (*around 1756 +1838) founded another lithographic print shop in Offenbach around 1802. He was probably initiated into the secrets of lithography by André or Senefelder himself. Since André's music publishing house was mainly concerned with printing sheet music, Johannot set up his print shop primarily for artists' lithographs. Skilled painters and draughtsmen, including Wilhelm Reuter from Berlin, were working in Offenbach at this time.

A contemporary article states:

"...Therefore, in addition to actual works of art, this institute supplies very nicely all genres, writings, bills of exchange, visiting cards, titles to books, vignettes (decorative pictures) and whatever else falls into this subject and also accepts orders for them under the cheapest conditions ..."

Johannot went to Paris in 1806.

Alois Senefelder's invention made its way from Offenbach to the rest of the world through the André music printing company, its branches in London and Paris and the Johannot lithographic printing company

Offenbach - the cradle of lithography

Alois Senefelder (1771-1834) is the inventor of lithography. In his Munich workshop in 1798, he finally succeeded - after experiments since 1796 - in developing a revolutionary, simplified printing process. The tedious work involved in producing a copperplate engraving was overcome with a chemically treated limestone plate that served as a printing plate. Offenbach became the cradle of the oldest planographic printing process by chance: in 1799, on a business trip to Vienna, the Offenbach music publisher and music printer Anton André (1775-1842) read an advertisement in a Munich newspaper in which Senefelder promoted the new process. A demonstration immediately convinced the businessman: "75 pages in a quarter of an hour, of which two were always printed at the same time" allowed the delivery of music for six kreuzer per sheet. Produced using copperplate printing, the sheet cost 24 to 30 kreuzer.

Anton André's business trip to Vienna led to a second, no less momentous result. André bought the composer's estate from Mozart's wife Constance: to support his widow, for the benefit of his company (the manuscripts were, however, dispersed a few decades later when the estate was divided). Mozart himself had visited the André firm in Offenbach in 1790 while passing through, which gives this acquisition a certain precondition. The first lithographed music prints appeared from 1800. Senefelder himself set up the lithographic presses for the André company and trained its workers. At the same time, the company established branches in European countries and sought to protect the process through privileges. The triumphant advance of the new technology was not bound to privileges. In general, lithography became important for the reproduction of artists' prints. The former location of the André company was Domstraße 21. Numerous lithographic printing stones were found in the cellars of the complex, which was abandoned in 1943, during recent excavations.
