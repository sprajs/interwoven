SIR CHARLES PARSONS’ FIRST COMMERCIAL STEAM TURBINE GENERATOR

Guest article by: Geoff Horseman, formerly Chief Turbine Engineer now retired, CA Parsons Works, Siemens, Newcastle-upon-Tyne and David Eaton, Volunteer at the Hawley Tool Collection, Kelham Island Industrial Museum

The photograph shows the first turbine generator (TG) which Sir Charles Parsons manufactured for a customer on display at the Kelham Island Industrial Museum in Sheffield. It was produced at Clarke, Chapman, Parsons & Co in Gateshead in 1885.

One year earlier in 1884, Sir Charles developed the World’s first steam turbine-generator – a completely new type of machine which would shortly supersede the reciprocating steam engine for electricity generation. This was the only type of machine capable of producing enough power to provide affordable, plentiful electricity to everyone.

Parson’s first machine was an experimental set and is now in the Science Museum in London.

The machine at Kelham Island is the first turbine-generator to be used to produce electricity in a commercial application. It was numbered “machine no.4” because it was produced in a factory which manufactured other types of equipment, but has been verified as Parsons first commercial TG. It produced 2 kW while running at speeds up to 10,000 revs/min and was first installed in the ship SS Earl Percy for on-board electric lighting. So, this pioneering machine was relatively small in size but the principles were well considered. In power stations today, giant turbines built using the same basic principles produce up to 1.7 GW ie 1,700,000 kW and supply power to many cities all from one machine.

In the photograph, the steam turbine is on the left with the casing cover open. If you were to examine the shaft, you would see rows of brass vanes formed like a series of water wheels. They actually did work on the same principles as water wheels except that they used steam. The benefits of steam rather than water were that any amount of steam could be produced (the power output wasn’t limited by the size of an available river or stream) and it could produce power anywhere (it wasn’t tied to the location of a river or lake). Also, steam could flow through the vanes at very high speed which helped to produce lots of power.

The generator may be seen on the right. As usual, electricity was produced by causing electrical conductors to move quickly through a magnetic field. In this case, the magnetic field was produced by an electromagnet. The coils of the electromagnet were large and wrapped in black insulation. They can be seen as the prominent large black component in the photo. The coils were wound on an iron core which formed a loop to guide the field lines. The conductors which generated the electricity were mounted on the shaft. The shaft was placed in a gap in the iron core of the magnet at the bottom of the loop, so the conductors rotated within the field lines.

The electricity was extracted from the shaft through a brass commutator. This is the copper coloured section at the right hand end of the shaft. Brass brushes rested on the commutator to remove the electricity as DC power. There were four brushes (two pairs), so that one pair could be repaired if needed while the other continued to carry the power. In the photo, the brushes can be seen underneath the shaft in the retracted position.

The machine will have operated on its own supplying power to electric lamps on a single circuit. If the load was suddenly lost eg a lamp burned out causing the circuit to break, the generator would have offered no resistance to the turbine and the machine potentially could have accelerated to a destructive overspeed faster than a human operator could have controlled it. To prevent this, a special form of control system was used. An electric solenoid was mounted on the generator (the copper component on top of the black coils), which moved as the current through the generator changed. This opened or closed a valve supplying air to a circular leather bellows mounted at the far left side of the machine. As the bellows expanded or contracted, this opened or closed the main steam inlet valve to the turbine and so limited its speed.

The brass pipes in the photo were lubricating oil pipes going to and from the oil tank which was contained in the cast iron bedplate.
