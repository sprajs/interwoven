Consequently, there is much yet to be learned about precontact bison pounding in the Northern Plains.\nL196: **Food Storage and Preservation**\nL197: Although Plains Indians were adept at obtaining fresh meat in winter by means of communal and individual hunting techniques, a continuous supply of fresh meat was never a certainty. Moreover, even if meat was available, it might be too lean to be usable. During late winter, bison fat reserves became depleted and their carcasses yielded a much higher proportion of lean meat than fat.\nL198: Since fat is extremely important to diet, especially in northern latitudes, hunters trying to survive on lean meat might actually starve (Speth 1983:104-106,\nL199: Ancient cold weather adaptations in the northern great plains 209\nL200: \nL201: 148-159). Therefore, in times of plenty Northern Plains Indians found it necessary to preserve and store food, including fat, against possible periods of need, especially during the worst parts of winter. Over thousands of years, they developed three principal methods for creating emergency food supplies: (1) freezing and caching, (2) drying and processing, and (3) resort to the consumption of domestic animals.\nL202: Because the Northern Plains region remains below 0°C for long periods each winter, freezing a bison carcass, or parts of a carcass, and caching or protecting it from consumption by animals, was sufficient storage for many purposes. For a short-term cache, such as a hunter might need when he had made a kill some distance from camp, a common method was to bury the meat under snow. This prevented its discovery by wolves, whose sense of smell would not penetrate the snow covering (Denig 1930:535).\nL203: For longer-term caches, or greater protection, Indians sometimes built platforms on poles, or in trees, above the reach of children, dogs and wild animals (Mandelbaum 1979:59). Occasionally, pit caches were dug into the ground. These would be lined with grass and flooded with sticks. The meat would be laid upon the sticks, covered with grass, and earth would be heaped over the mouth of the pit for closure (Mandelbaum 1979:535).\nL204: Archaeological evidence for caches is not common, but has been found in some very old sites. At the Colby site in Wyoming, piles of mammoth bones associated with Clovis artifacts, dating to about 11,000 radiocarbon years ago, have been interpreted as frozen meat caches (Frison and Todd 1986).\nL205: Similarly, piles of bison bones in the Folsom (10,780 ± 120 radiocarbon years before present), Agate Basin (10,430 ± 570 rcybp) and Hell Gap (10,445 ± 110 rcybp) levels of the Agate Basin site, Wyoming have been described as the remains of frozen meat caches (Frison and Stanford 1982). An unusual pattern of breakage has been identified for frozen bison bones which are inferred to have been retrieved from meat caches at the Harder site, Saskatchewan (3360 - 4410 rcybp).\nL206: This includes fractures through epiphyses, fractures extending from one bone element through adjoining elements, and a strange type of potlid fracture (Morlan 1994). In addition, storage pits used for plant or animal foods have been located in ancient occupation sites, as, for example, in the Medicine Lodge Creek site, Wyoming (Frison 1991:341-342). Consequently, all the known methods for caching food have considerable historical depth in Great Plains region.\nL207: Preservation of food by drying and by additional processing was another important storage tactic used in the Northern Plains, as it was throughout much of North America. Plains Indians dried and stored a number of plants in considerable quantities for winter use. Examples included prairie turnips (Psoralea esculenta), wild peas (Falcata comosa), wild plums (Prunus americana), chokecherries (Padus nana) and buffalo-berries (Lepargyrea argentea) (Ewers 1961:10-13; Gilmore 1919; Hellson and Gadd 1974).\nL208: Archaeological iden-\nL209: 210  Revista de Arqueología Americana No. 20\nL210: \nL211: tification of plant preservation and use, however, has proven difficult, and there has been little development of that subject in the Northern Plains (Shay 1980:270-274). Activities surrounding the preservation of meat products have left a much stronger trace.\nL212: When bison carcasses were butchered, it was common to preserve portions against times when meat would not be available (Wissler 1910:22-24; Verbicky-Todd 1984:181-182). This practice was especially important during fall when bison were in prime condition - fat and ready for winter. At such times, lean meat would be cut into long thin strips and dried on racks in the sun or over a fire (Figure 12). This reduced the weight by as much as 85% and produced a nutritious product which would last for months.\nL213: Dried meat was tough and difficult to eat, but it was easy to store and transport. For more refined use in a product known as pemmican, dried meat was beaten into a granular form nearly like flour. Finally, dried berries, usually chokecherries or saskatoons, might be added for flavour. The finished pemmican would be jammed into flat skin bags which were sewn shut for storage or transportation. This mixture kept well and created an important food reserve for hard times. “Beat” meat might also be stored separately, in bags, for use in soups and stews. Rendered marrow fat or grease was another product which might be stored separately, in bladders, for later use.\nL221: Yet another durable bison food product was the slab of back-fat known to fur traders as “dépouille”. Once removed from the animal, this delicacy might weigh two to five kilograms. For preservation it was dipped in hot fat, then hung on poles inside a lodge to dry and smoke for about 12 hours. Thus prepared, the dépouille kept indefinitely. It was usually eaten with lean or dried meat, and was tender, sweet and nourishing (Hamilton 1905:32, cited in Wissler 1910:24).\nL222: Meat products such as these were made in processing areas situated in or near campsites. The archaeological evidence for production is quite abundant and consists of quantities of highly fractured grease- and marrow-bearing bones, bone-breaking anvils and hammers, bone-boiling pits and stone boiling rings, boiled-bone spill piles, hearths and quantities of fire-broken rock (Frison 1967; Reeves 1990).\nL223: Evidence of this kind has been found in many Northern Plains sites, especially those dating to the last two thousand years. Thomas Kehoe (1973, 1978) has argued that meat-drying technology is one reason that mass bison-killing eventually reached industrial proportions in the Northern Plains. Durable meat products such as pemmican served not only domestic needs, but also became commodities for trade.\nL224: The innovative drying process which enabled these developments is believed to have occurred about 5,000 to 6,000 years ago (Reeves 1990; Walker 1992; Morlan 1992, 1994).\nL225: The final food reserve that will be considered here is the dog, the sole animal domesticated by Northern Plains peoples prior to the arrival of Euro-Americans. It is presumed that dogs arrived with humans upon entry into North America (Walker and Frison 1982:125). Dogs were used primarily as beasts of burden, but there is ethnohistoric, ethnological, archaeological and nutritional evidence which shows that they also served as reliable, easily obtainable sources of nutrients, especially fats (Snyder 1991).\nL226: During early historic times, dogs were eaten by many groups when other foods were in critically short supply and, by some groups, as delicacies during feasts (Schwartz 1997:88-91). Dog bones have been found in many Northern Plains archaeological sites (cf. McCorquodale 1960; Dyck 1977; Krozser 1991; Brink et al., 1985). Yet, the means for distinguishing among the possible reasons for their presence has not been well established.\nL227: As a result, for bison-hunting complexes in particular, which of the dog remains may be interpreted as evidence for human consumption and which are better interpreted as having served other purposes is still poorly resolved (Thurman 1988).\nL228: 212\nL229: Revista de Arqueología Americana No. 20\nL230: \nL231: **Transportation**\nL232: The snow and ice of Northern Plains winters can be both obstacle and aid to transportation. When snow is soft and deep, or wet and sticky, it becomes an obstacle. When it is hard-crusted, one can walk over top. Similarly, when ice is jointed and heaved, its surface is almost impassable, but when it is smooth and clear it creates a natural bridge over what would otherwise be a water obstacle. Snow is by far the most extensive surface covering in the Northern Plains during winter.\nL233: In the course of a single journey, a traveller may find that its condition varies from shallow and of little consequence to deep and difficult to traverse. The key innovations used by historic Plains Indians to aid their passage over snow-covered terrain were the snowshoe, the sled and the toboggan (Bain 1809:281; Ewers 1961:130; see Figure 13, this paper).
