Analysis of microbotanical remains recovered from two chultunes, pits cut into bedrock, in a Late and Terminal Classic period residential group at the site of San Bartolo, Petén, Guatemala, provides the first archaeological recovery of maize starch spherulites, a unique byproduct of nixtamalization, and thus the earliest direct evidence of nixtamalization in the archaeological record. The presence of helminth eggs within the same contexts indicates that, at some point in their use life, the pits were used as latrines and as middens for the disposal of domestic refuse, likely including nejayote, wastewater from nixtamalization. These findings shed light on the daily lives of ancient Maya commoners and inform discussions of subsistence and waste management in Maya cities.\n\n## Keywords\n\nMaize\n\n;\n\nFood production\n\n;\n\nNixtamalization\n\n;\n\nStarch granule\n\n;\n\nStarch spherulite\n\n;\n\nChultun\n\n;\n\nLatrine\n\n## Introduction\n\nDomesticated maize (Zea mays subsp. mays) has been a staple component of Mesoamerican diets from ancient times to the present day. Domesticated varieties were bred from wild Z. mays in the vicinity of the Balsas River Valley of Guerrero, Mexico around 6700 BCE (Piperno et al., 2009; Ranere et al., 2009) with intensive cultivation attested from Guerrero to Panama by 5000 BCE (Piperno 2011). For ancient Maya people, maize had an ideological role reflected in its importance to elite diets (Chase and Chase 2001) and the worship and widespread representation of a Maize God whose death and resurrection dramatized the agricultural cycle (Taube 1985).\n\nA key factor that likely facilitated the widespread adoption of maize and its nutritional importance in Mesoamerica was nixtamalization, the process by which dried maize kernels are soaked and cooked in an alkaline solution (nejayote in Spanish, from the Nahuatl nexayotl “ash water” [Cheetham 2010]). Yet direct durable traces solely tied to this process have not previously been established, so no direct evidence of the nixtamalization process has hitherto been identified from an archaeological context in the Maya world. Here, we present chemical and microscopic evidence that residues of nixtamalized maize, including likely nejayote, were deposited in two chultunes, pits excavated into limestone bedrock, in an ancient Maya residential complex at the site of San Bartolo, Guatemala. As such, our study is the first to document archaeological evidence for a culinary process that fundamentally changed Mesoamerican diets.\n\n\n## Section snippets\n\n### Nixtamalization\n\nNixtamalization is an alkali cooking method that renders certain amino acids more accessible, contributes calcium, and—most importantly—converts bound niacin to free niacin, rendering it biologically available (Katz et al., 1974). Given the ubiquity of nixtamalization in ancient Mesoamerica, it is expected that similar microremains should be identifiable in archaeological deposits throughout the region.
