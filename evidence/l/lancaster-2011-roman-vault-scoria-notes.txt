Provenancing of Lightweight Volcanic Stones Used in Ancient Roman Concrete Vaulting: Evidence from Rome
Archaeometry 53(4), pp. 707–727, version of record
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-4754.2010.00565.x

Original petrographic and geochemical study. Read the published PDF at https://www.rilievoarcheologico.it/home-articoli-li_g000027.pdf, with printed p. 707 on PDF page 1. The Pantheon sample is reported from Gioacchino De Angelis d’Ossat’s 1930 study and was outside the authors’ own analytical sample set.

pp. 707–709, Introduction, Previous Studies and sample selection
Roman builders embedded large stone pieces (caementa) in mortar. The Pantheon is given as an example of lightweight volcanic stone in a vault. In 1929 De Angelis d’Ossat cut thin sections from a scoria sample collected during restoration of the dome. His tentative Vesuvian attribution was based on texture and mineralogy. Lancaster et al. analysed other buildings.

p. 711, fig. 2 and sample 021
The team sampled dark scoria used as coarse aggregate in a room vault against the Capitoline Hill, assigned to 46–44 BCE. The captioned photograph shows the exposed material. The authors credit Amici (1991, pp. 52 and 162) with the earlier site observation.

pp. 712–713, fig. 3b and sample 022
Quoted excerpt: the same combination found in the crown of the Pantheon dome
A fallen chunk from the Basilica Ulpia’s north aisle, dated by the study to AD 112, has alternating layers of dark scoria and Tufo Giallo della Via Tiberina. The authors explicitly compare those coarse aggregates with the crown of the Pantheon dome. The overall form and support of the basilica vaults remain debated.

pp. 713, 724–725, fig. 3d and sample 05
The Baths of Diocletian sample is a piece of pumiceous scoria used as coarse aggregate in the frigidarium’s buttressing arches, dated AD 298–305 by the study. The authors compare trace-element ratios to Campi Flegrei materials and leave the reasons for the shift in sourcing open.
